## Quick reference

Mental skill is the second-largest performance factor in climbing — Magiera 2013 ranked it behind only finger strength among performance predictors (canonical weight −0.410). Fear of falling, focus discipline, route preview, and emotional regulation are all trainable. The coach treats mental skill as a domain to be trained with the same dedication as physical training: process-oriented framing, evidence-based protocols (ANSWER Sequence, two-type visualization, structured preview), and explicit handling of the four primary climbing fears.

## Core findings

### 1. Psychology is the #2 performance factor — and the least systematically trained

Mangan et al. 2024 — the first comprehensive systematic review of climbing psychology — screened 504 records and included 83 studies. Their consensus: flow, confidence, anxiety facilitation (interpreting anxiety as helpful rather than harmful), and perception (route reading, affordance detection) are the key psychological mediators of climbing performance. Elite climbers exhibit lower anxiety than the average population; risk-taking is *not* a defining characteristic of climbers (contra popular belief).

Magiera 2013 (cited in T01 §2.1) ranked psychology second only to finger strength in a canonical-correlation analysis of performance determinants. Yet most users approach training as if it were 95% physical. The coach's job is to normalize mental training as parallel to physical training — not as an afterthought once the body is ready.

**Hörst's framing (Ch.3 opening):** all performance operates from the inside out. Beliefs, focus, fears, confidence, preparation, and problem-solving ability form the foundation that determines whether physical and technical capacity actually shows up on the wall.

### 2. The four primary climbing fears (Hörst Ch.3 §4)

Hörst distinguishes **reasonable fears** (groundfall without protection, objective hazard) that must be heeded and mitigated from **unreasonable fears** that derail performance and must be eliminated.

**Fear of falling.** What's actually feared is *not knowing what the fall will be like*. This explains why the first fall is the scariest and subsequent falls are much less stressful. **Protocol — progressive fall practice:**
1. Steep climb with bomber protection
2. Double-check knot and buckle
3. Start with 2-foot falls
4. Build to ~12-foot falls (gear just below feet)
5. More experienced climbers: mentally replay past inconsequential falls during preclimb warm-up; believe fully that falls on this climb will be no different (if that's true)

Coaching consensus (Hörst, Ilgner) supports this graded-exposure approach. It's the most evidence-supported intervention for fear of falling.

**Fear of pain.** Causes climbers to give up before reaching physical limit. Climbing a sustained route hurts; the pain is brief; perseverance pays. Decision: push into the discomfort zone a bit further each time → pain threshold redefines.

**Fear of failure.** Often instilled in childhood as binary success/failure framing. Symptoms: attack less aggressive than required, second-guessing sequences mid-execution, breathing shallow, paralysis by analysis. **Three counter-protocols:**
1. **Focus on the *probable* outcome**, not the worst-case. Based on past experience, what's the realistic outcome? Usually it's "I might fall and try again," not catastrophe.
2. **Process focus, not outcome focus** — attention on precise foot placements, relaxed grip, moving to the next rest. Don't ponder the send or the fall.
3. **Adopt "it's okay to fall"** — accept the outcome in advance. Willingly accepting the fate dissolves the fear.

**Fear of embarrassment.** Occasional bad-performance days are inevitable. Accept them, analyze them, bury them. Bold attempts that occasionally fail are better than mediocrity that always succeeds.

The coach handles fear conversations with calibration. Reasonable fears get respected ("yes, that pitch needs better gear before you try it again"). Unreasonable fears get reframed using the protocols above. Never minimize ("just send it") and never overpromise ("you'll be fine"). Honest framing only.

### 3. The ANSWER Sequence — 6-step in-climb centering (Hörst Ch.3 §5.3)

The most app-actionable protocol from the chapter. Designed for use at every mid-route rest position. Initially takes a few minutes; with practice, executes in ~10 seconds.

| Letter | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Awareness | Turn thoughts inward; perform tension check; catch rising anxiety before it snowballs |
| **N** | Normalize breathing | Deep, regular belly breaths. Exception: Valsalva on a single hard move |
| **S** | Scan for tension | Quick sweep of forearms (overgripping?), shoulders, upper back, abdominals, calves; contract then release any tight muscle |
| **W** | Wave of relaxation | Single deep breath; feel relaxation wash from head to toes |
| **E** | Erase | Let go of last failed attempt and possible future outcomes; refocus on now |
| **R** | Reset posture and smile | Postural reset + smile → peak performance state |

The Coach can prompt this in guided-session mode at rest points. Concrete enough to use right away, simple enough to remember mid-climb. This is the single highest-leverage mental drill the engine can surface.

### 4. The Energy-Emotion Matrix — pre-climb state diagnostic

Hörst's 4-quadrant model (Ch.3 §3.2):

| Quadrant | Energy | Emotion | Climbing state |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | High | Negative | **Avoid** — fight-or-flight, overgripping, death grip |
| II | High | Positive | Ideal for short powerful routes/boulder problems |
| III | Low | Negative | Depression, withdrawal — climbing not advisable |
| IV | Low | Positive | Ideal for technical/tactical climbing, learning, testing |

**Climbing-specific rule:** Quadrant IV (low-energy, positive) is the default optimal — creative thinking, learning, fine motor skills all demand relaxed positive state. Quadrant II (high-energy, positive) is appropriate only for short, powerful efforts (boulder problem, short pumpy route). **Never climb in Quadrant I** — high energy with negative emotion produces tension and overgripping.

Practical use: at the base of a climb, the user scores their current energy (0-10) and emotion (-5 to +5). The coach can ask the question, the user diagnoses themselves, and the action depends on the gap: positive emotion shortfall → use Hörst's mental interventions (replay past successes, visualize future goals, deep belly breaths, postural reset, smile). Excess energy → low-intensity warm-up, breathing protocol, ANSWER Sequence.

### 5. Visualization — disassociated vs. associated

Two types, each for a specific purpose (Hörst Ch.3 §6).

**Disassociated (observer / on-TV view):** Use for reviewing past poor performances, route-finding, imagining possible sequences, risk management, fall-risk assessment. The on-TV perspective creates emotional distance — you can analyze without re-experiencing the negative emotion.

**Associated (first-person, through your own eyes):** Use for preprogramming a future ascent — redpoint or onsight preparation. Build a positive, perfect mental movie of the climb.

**Critical rule:** When projecting positive future events, always use **associated**. Switch to **disassociated** when visualizing what could go wrong (risk management).

**Preprogramming a redpoint — protocol:**
1. Disassociated: review last attempt; note what to correct
2. Switch to associated: perfect mental movie through your own eyes
3. Imagine crux moves, gear placements, rest positions
4. Feel both the movement and the relaxed centered state
5. End with feeling of topping out

**On-sight preparation:** no first-hand experience → mostly disassociated. After studying from below, project yourself climbing from on-TV perspective; if route allows it, create two possible sequences through the crux so you can switch if the first doesn't work mid-climb.

**Climbing injured or tired:** Vivid associated visualization produces low-level neuromuscular activity that supports motor learning even without physical practice (Feltz 1983). Useful when laid up with finger injury or as a smarter alternative to thrashing late-session.

### 6. The Singular Focus Drill and pinpoint focus

Focus in climbing is a zoom lens — pinpoint focus on the immediate critical task, wide-angle when scanning for the next hold. Most climbers struggle with the *redirection* skill, not the focus itself.

**Singular Focus Drill (Hörst Ch.3 §7.2):**
On a route 1-2 grades below max, focus only on *one* aspect of movement — e.g. hand placements only (best grip on each hold, minimum grip strength, sense the purchase change as you pull). Allow intuition to handle feet, balance, belayer. When focus wanders, redirect to the predetermined task.

Progression: each session, change the focus aspect (feet only, weight shifts, breathing, etc.). Then convert to dynamic focus drill — focus shifts to the most critical task at each moment.

**Pinpointing Focus pre-climb exercise (Hörst Ch.3 §7.3):**
Final task before starting up the route, after preclimb ritual and belay-on:
1. Extended posture; eyes closed
2. Fingertips of dominant hand touch the rock — light contact, no grip
3. Three deep belly breaths (in for 5, out for 10)
4. Narrow focus to the sensation of fingertips on rock; feel the thermal energy exchange
5. Hold 30 s to 2 min; redirect if focus wanders
6. When mind is still: open eyes and begin climbing

These are zero-cost, fully app-compatible exercises. The Coach can prompt them in guided session mode.

### 7. Six Enemies of Concentration

Hörst's taxonomy (Ch.3 §7.4) — useful diagnostic when a user reports "I lost focus":

1. **Focusing on mechanics of well-learned skills** — skills with high competence should be turned over to preconscious. Conscious focus on them creates mechanical, lower-efficiency movement.
2. **Dwelling on internal fatigue sensations** — magnifies them; opens door to stopping thoughts. Antidote: dissociate from non-injury "good pain."
3. **Nonproductive self-talk** — antidote: positive, productive self-talk (see §8).
4. **Focusing on the past** — looking at the rearview mirror derails performance.
5. **Focusing on the future** — outcome thinking generates pressure; makes the unwanted outcome more likely.
6. **External distractions** — visual, auditory, social. Antidote: systematic distraction elimination + practice blocking out crowd/sound.

When a user describes a focus problem, mapping it to one of these six lets the coach target the antidote rather than offering generic "stay focused" advice.

### 8. Positive self-talk and reframing

Hörst's mental-model: inside the head, a "critic" voice and a "doer" voice. The doer compels action and keeps state productive. The critic is useful in narrow situations (evaluating weakness, risk management) but otherwise non-useful. **Goal: doer rules the mind ~95% of the time.**

Reframe technique (Hörst Ch.3 §2 Strategy 9):
- *"This route looks impossible"* → *"This route looks challenging"*
- *"I feel nervous"* → *"I feel energized"*
- *"I'll probably fall"* → *"I think I can do this; if I fall it's okay because I'll get it next time"*

This isn't denial — it's interpretive shift. Mangan et al. 2024 found that **anxiety facilitation** (interpreting anxiety as helpful rather than harmful) is a key performance mediator. The somatic symptoms of anxiety (elevated HR, alertness, focus) are physiologically similar to those of high readiness; the interpretation determines whether they help or hurt.

### 9. Route preview — the most actionable cognitive skill

Three convergent research lines establish preview quality as a direct, measurable performance lever:

- **Sanchez 2012** — preview doesn't help completion, but improves *form*: fewer stops, shorter stops. Expert climbers benefit most. Preview errors are a major reason for falling.
- **Seifert 2017** — preview directly reduces jerk (smoother climbing); ratio of exploratory to performatory movements drops. Shorter preview correlates with greater fluency (experts preview faster *and* more effectively).
- **Medernach 2024** — elite previewers: shorter durations, fewer scans, "superficial scanning" strategy. Movement repertoire accounts for expertise differences. Visual search efficiency and pattern recognition predict preview effectiveness.

**Two functions of preview (Sanchez 2019):**
1. **Ascent strategy forecasting** — which holds, which sequence, where to clip, where to rest
2. **Ascent effort forecasting** — where the crux is, how to pace, where to recover

**Structured protocol (D75 — coach surface):**
1. Identify rest positions
2. Plan crux sequence (which holds; which direction to face; what kind of move)
3. Visualize clipping positions (or top-out for boulder)
4. Note hazards or commit points

2 minutes per route is enough for most. The engine prompts this in guided-session mode. Cross-reference: `06_technique_movement.md` §15.

### 10. Fear of falling — gender-specific evidence

Sendín-Pérez & Pans 2025 (SR of 15 studies): fear of falling disproportionately impacts female climbers. Anxiety from fear of falling directly influences performance in women.

**Garrido-Palomino & España-Romero 2023** — landmark intervention study. Psychological training based on emotional regulation:
- Improved climbing ability in women with fear of falling
- Reduced *both* cognitive and somatic anxiety
- Increased self-confidence and interoceptive awareness
- Physical training alone reduced only cognitive anxiety (not somatic)

**Implication:** psychological intervention adds value *beyond* additional climbing practice. The coach should not assume that more climbing will eventually dissolve a fear of falling pattern — emotional regulation work (breathing, reframing, graded exposure) is the specific lever.

For male climbers, similar mechanisms apply but the gender-specific evidence base is smaller. The protocols (gradual exposure, emotional regulation, breathing, self-talk reframing) transfer.

### 11. Flow state — what it is and when it appears

Csikszentmihalyi's flow concept maps cleanly onto climbing. From Mangan 2024:
- Flow arises in novel, challenging climbing situations
- Increases both enjoyment and performance
- Conditions: clear goals, immediate feedback, challenge-skill balance, deep concentration, sense of control

Climbing is one of the most flow-prone sports because: immediate consequences, progressive challenge (grade system), full-body engagement, environmental novelty.

**Training for flow** — not directly trainable, but conditions can be optimized:
- Climb at challenge-skill boundary (flash to 2-session project range)
- Vary venues, styles, wall angles (novelty)
- Minimize distractions during sessions

Note (Schüler & Nakamura 2013): flow can lead to risk-taking in some individuals. The coach distinguishes flow (optimal performance state) from recklessness — flow shouldn't override the safety protocols in L0.

### 12. Pre-climb rituals — building the foundation

Hörst Ch.3 Strategy 7: the minutes before a climb form the foundation for performance. Quality of foundation depends on quality of pre-climb rituals. The user develops their own from past experience — what did you think and do before your best ascents? What did you eat/drink? How did you warm up? How long did you rest?

**Hörst's personal example:**
1. Scope route → identify path, key moves, rest positions (5-10 min)
2. Mental rehearsal and visualization (a few minutes)
3. Put on shoes, tie in
4. Final preclimb ritual: slow deep breaths, straight posture, crack a smile
5. Total: 5-15 min depending on route

The coach can suggest constructing a ritual but shouldn't impose one. Personal rituals are SDT-aligned (autonomy-respecting) and tend to be more durable when self-derived.

### 13. Chunking down hard projects

Hörst Ch.3 §9 Strategy 3 — for long hard routes: break the route into smaller chunks. Each chunk = short-term goal. While failing on the full project for days, you experience short-term success solving chunks → keeps motivation and progress visible.

**Critical rule:** avoid becoming obsessed on any one chunk — frustration there is self-defeating (drains energy needed for the others). Move on to the next chunk at first frustration or judgmental self-talk ("I can't do this," "I'll never figure this out"). Come back later, fresh.

**Working sequence:** hardest chunks first; then link sections from the top down.

This applies equally to boulder projects with linked cruxes. Maps onto how the engine's adaptive periodization handles project effort — Performance phase concentrates the most cognitive load on the project, with explicit rest between attempts to keep the chunk-by-chunk strategy viable.

### 14. Try something ridiculous — beta freedom

Hörst Ch.3 §9 Strategy 6 — the biggest block to learning is self-judgment. Self-talk like *"others use this sequence, so that must be the best way"* is self-sabotage. The brain doesn't know what you can or can't do until you tell it.

**Practice:** when stuck, ignore the obvious solution; attempt completely different, even improbable sequences. Try unchalked holds. Try the "off-route" foot 30 cm to the side. Eliminate the seemingly must-use hold.

The coach can encourage this when a user reports stuck on a project — most "stuck" is a beta box, not a strength gap.

### 15. Bouldering as mental-health intervention (context, not prescription)

Karg 2020 + 2025 SR (PMC, 7 studies, 471 participants): indoor bouldering + mindfulness ("Bouldering Psychotherapy") significantly reduces depression symptoms (~−8.3 points MADRS, exceeds MCID). Longer interventions (8-10 weeks) needed for lasting effects.

**climb-agent is not a mental-health app.** This finding is relevant because users sometimes attribute motivation drops and persistent malaise to "weakness" rather than to broader wellbeing factors. The coach can acknowledge that climbing has documented therapeutic benefits when the conversation goes that direction, but never positions climb-agent as a therapeutic tool. When signs of depression emerge in user reports, route to professional resources (see also [[L1_coach_voice]] sensitive-topic protocols).

## When user asks…

**"I'm scared of falling on lead. What do I do?"**

Two layers. First, separate reasonable from unreasonable fear: is the gear good? Are the falls clean? If yes, the fear is unreasonable in Hörst's sense — meaning it deserves to be eliminated rather than heeded. The protocol is graded exposure: find a steep route with bomber protection, double-check your knot, start with 2-foot falls, build to 12-foot. The first fall is always the scariest; subsequent falls are dramatically less so. Second layer — Garrido-Palomino 2023 showed emotional regulation training (breathing protocols, cognitive reappraisal, interoceptive awareness) adds value *beyond* exposure alone. Use ANSWER at every rest. Practice reframing: "I feel nervous" → "I feel energized." Don't expect this to resolve in one session. 4-8 weeks of consistent practice is the realistic timeline.

**"I lose focus mid-route. How do I train this?"**

Diagnose first using Hörst's six enemies of concentration — which is yours? (1) Auto-pilot on familiar moves; (2) Dwelling on fatigue; (3) Negative self-talk; (4) Replaying past; (5) Projecting outcomes; (6) External distractions. The antidote depends on which. Generic "stay focused" doesn't work. Then train focus directly: Singular Focus Drill weekly (climb a route 1-2 grades below max, focus only on hand placements; next session, only feet; next, only breathing). Pinpointing Focus exercise as your final preclimb ritual. ANSWER at every rest. Focus control is a trainable skill, not a personality trait.

**"My mental game falls apart at competitions."**

That's specifically anxiety-under-performance-pressure, which has the largest evidence base for intervention. Three concrete steps: (1) Energy-Emotion check before each climb — diagnose Quadrant, adjust toward IV or II per the climb type. (2) Use anxiety-facilitation reframing (Mangan 2024) — interpret the elevated HR and alertness as readiness, not threat. (3) Build a pre-climb ritual you trust and stick to it — Hörst's argument is that the ritual is the bridge from chaotic gym/comp environment to a focused performance state. The ritual is yours to build, not mine to prescribe. If anxiety remains a recurring barrier, a sport psychologist is the right escalation — climb-agent supports mental training but isn't a substitute for clinical work when it's needed.

**"I'm working a project and getting frustrated. Should I push through?"**

Hörst's specific answer: no. Chunk the route — work the hardest section first, then link top-down. When frustration or judgmental self-talk rises on a chunk ("I can't do this"), move to the next chunk. Come back fresh. Pushing through judgmental self-talk grooves it into the subconscious and makes failure more likely. The non-obvious move: also try something completely different on the chunk that's giving you grief — ignore the obvious beta, attempt an improbable sequence, try the off-route foothold 30 cm sideways. Most "stuck" is a beta box, not a strength gap.

**"How important is visualization? Does it actually do anything?"**

Yes, and there's specific protocol. For redpoint preparation: disassociated first (observe yourself, note corrections from last attempt), then switch to associated (through-your-own-eyes mental movie) for the perfect ascent — feel the moves, feel the centered state, end with topping out. Make it bright, detailed, complete. The brain doesn't fully distinguish vivid imagined experience from real (Kubistant 1986); Feltz 1983 showed low-level neuromuscular activation during associated visualization. It supports motor learning even when injured. Critical rule: visualize *positive* outcomes in associated mode; switch to disassociated when visualizing what could go wrong (risk management). Don't visualize a fall from your own eyes.

**"I've been climbing for a while but my onsight grade isn't moving. Is it mental?"**

Likely partly. Three diagnostic questions: (1) Are you previewing routes systematically before climbing, or just looking at them? Seifert 2017 showed preview quality directly reduces movement jerk. Structured 2-min preview using the rests-first protocol is a typical 0.5-1 grade jump. (2) Are you relying on partner beta? Hörst's rule: beyond the first few months as a climber, beta dependency caps onsight ability because you don't develop the problem-solving schema. Shut off partner beta on routes you're trying to onsight. (3) Are you practicing random sequences (Stick Game, link different boulder problems)? Onsight is fundamentally a random-practice skill. If you only ever work projects, your onsight stays where it is. Cross-reference: `06_technique_movement.md` §7 (practice taxonomy).
