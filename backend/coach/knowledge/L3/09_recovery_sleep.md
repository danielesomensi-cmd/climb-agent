## Quick reference

Recovery is not the absence of training — it's where adaptation happens. The coach treats recovery as a proactive skill, not passive waiting. The three-period model (Recharge → Refuel → Rebuild) maps the time-scales: 10 s to 60 min (intra-session), 1-24 hr (intraday/overnight), and 1-4 days (interday/supercompensation). Sleep is the single highest-leverage recovery input (Charest & Grandner 2020). Adequate energy availability (cross-reference [[08_nutrition]]) and the 30-minute post-exercise refueling window are the other two pillars.

## Core findings

### 1. The three recovery periods (Hörst Ch.12 §2.2)

Recovery is exponential, not linear (Bompa 1983, Figure 12.1):
- ~70% of complete recovery occurs in the first 1/3 of the total recovery period
- ~90% after 2/3 of total recovery time
- The final 10% takes the remaining 1/3 of time

Three nested timescales matter:

**Period 1: RECHARGE (10 s — 60 min).**
Intra-session and immediate-post-session. ATP-CP resynthesis (complete in <5 min) + H+/lactate clearance (<60 min). After ~3 min the majority of ATP-CP is back; after ~40 min blood pH has returned to near-normal.

This is the time-scale of: mid-route shakeouts, the 5-minute rest between sets, the 10-minute pause between boulder problems. Rest intervals in strength sessions (3-5 min between max hangs, between max-effort boulder attempts) are designed around the ATP-CP curve.

**Period 2: REFUEL (60 min — 24 hr).**
Medium-term recovery. Blood glucose and glycogen replenishment (muscles + liver), minor tissue microtrauma repair. ~90% of refueling occurs in the first 16 hours.

A 12-hour overnight break gives ~80% glycogen replenishment, *assuming immediate post-exercise refueling*. Skipping the 30-minute window can extend incomplete refueling by another full day.

**Period 3: REBUILD (1 — 4 days).**
Long-term recovery. Muscle growth and neuromuscular adaptation occur here. DOMS subsides over 48 hours (mild) to 4+ days (severe). **Supercompensation requires this period.** A single rest day allows Recharge + Refuel but truncates Rebuild — muscle returns to baseline but not above. Chronic under-resting prevents adaptation and increases injury and illness risk.

This is why deload weeks exist (cross-reference [[01_periodization]] §5): adaptation lives in Rebuild, and Rebuild needs more than overnight.

### 2. Five causes of fatigue (Hörst Ch.12 §2.1)

The coach uses these to diagnose "why am I tired?":

1. **ATP-CP depletion** — max-intensity work, <12 sec efforts. Recovery in 3-5 min full rest (Bloomfield 1994). Drives all max-effort rest-interval prescriptions.
2. **Metabolic by-product accumulation (H+, lactate)** — 12 sec to 2 min sustained efforts. Recovery 10-60 min (active vs. passive matters). Strength/PE training improves cellular buffering.
3. **Glycogen depletion** — 90 min to 2 hours of steady exercise; climbing's stop-and-go can stretch this to all day with proper fueling. **Complete replenishment takes 24 hours**, not overnight.
4. **Low blood glucose** — as glycogen depletes, muscles draw on blood glucose; brain runs only on blood glucose → exhaustion + mental fatigue.
5. **Central fatigue** — brain-based, not muscular. Reduced neural drive, impaired coordination, decreased focus. Hardest on the CNS: repeated high-intensity movements (campus, max hangs, lunging), excessive same-pattern bouldering. **Nerve cell recovery takes up to 7× longer than muscle cell recovery** (Bompa 1983). Persistent malaise after multiple rest days is the hallmark — may take 2-10 more days to fully recuperate.

When a user reports "I'm not bouncing back like I used to," the most common cause is central fatigue (especially after dense Strength + PE phases) followed by inadequate Refuel (see [[08_nutrition]] §6).

### 3. Short-term recovery — what actually works mid-climb

**G-Tox technique** (Hörst-developed, Roberts 2003 university validation):
- At a marginal rest, alternate the resting arm between the normal dangling position and a raised-above-head position, every 5-10 seconds
- Mechanism: in dangling position, blood flow *into* the muscle may slightly exceed venous return, perpetuating the pump. Raised-arm position uses gravity to aid venous return, accelerating H+ removal. Can't hold raised the whole time (upper-body fatigue), so alternation is the optimum.

**Active rest between climbs** (Watts 2000, Monedero 2000, Draper 2006):
- Active rest accelerates lactate clearance by ~35% vs. passive sitting (Watts 2000, 15 climbers, 5.12b redpoint study).
- Combined active rest + self-massage is even better (Monedero 2000).
- For bouldering: walking around between attempts > sitting (Draper 2006).
- Practical: after a pumpy redpoint attempt, 20-minute casual walk + sip water. Cheap, easy, evidence-based.

**G-Tox is the in-session lever. Active rest is the between-climbs lever.** Both cost nothing.

**Pre-hydrate before sessions.** Muscles are >70% water. 1.5% BW dehydration cuts max strength measurably (cross-reference [[08_nutrition]] §5). Pre-hydrate with ~500 ml before training; sip 240 ml/hr through the session.

**Climb efficiently** — the most underrated recovery tool. Less ATP-CP burn, less H+ accumulation, less to recover from. Cross-reference [[06_technique_movement]] §5 (high economy).

### 4. The 30-minute post-exercise window (Refuel period anchor)

The strongest single evidence in the recovery literature:

- Waiting 2 hours after exercise to consume carbs reduces glycogen replenishment by ~50% (Burke 1999)
- High-GI foods post-exercise substantially increase glycogen replacement rate (Richter 1984)
- Glycogen resynthesis ~40% faster with protein + carb together (Niles 1997)
- Best protocol: **4:1 carbohydrate-to-protein** ratio in first 30 minutes (Burke 1999)
- For a 72 kg climber: ~100 g carb + ~25 g protein
- Chocolate skim milk is a near-perfect option (~50 g carb + ~17 g protein per pint)

This window is the highest-leverage moment in the day for recovery. Detailed protocol in [[08_nutrition]] §6.

### 5. Sleep — the dominant interday lever (D65)

Charest & Grandner 2020 review establishes sleep as the #1 recovery input:

- **Growth hormone surges during deep sleep (N3 stage)** — critical for tissue repair, protein synthesis, muscle growth
- A single night without sleep can reduce testosterone by ~25%
- Chronic sleep deficiency → elevated cortisol → increased protein breakdown, limited recovery capacity
- Sleep deprivation impairs decision-making, motor coordination, reaction time — directly relevant to route reading, move execution, risk assessment
- **Poor sleep predicts injury.** Adolescent athletes sleeping <8 hr had significantly increased injury rates

**Recommendations:**
- Adults: minimum 7 hr (IOC consensus); ideal 8-10 after strenuous training
- Adolescent athletes: 8-10 hr
- Multi-day big-wall / alpine recovery: 8-10+ hr

**What works for sleep improvement (Cunha 2023 SR of athlete sleep interventions):**
- **Sleep extension and naps** were the most effective strategies for improving sleep and subsequent performance
- Sleep hygiene education alone had limited effects
- Mindfulness and light manipulation: promising but need more research
- Cold water immersion: no effect on sleep specifically

The coach surfaces sleep as the first thing to optimize when a user reports flat performance, persistent fatigue, or stalled adaptation. Sleep before nutrition, before supplements, before "harder workouts."

**Sleep-nutrition interaction:** Driller 2023 (chrononutrition) — training adaptations are maximized by optimal nutrition both pre- and post-exercise, and nutrition itself affects sleep quality. Before bed: small carb+protein snack (skim milk works) supports overnight rebuilding without disturbing sleep.

### 6. Active recovery between sessions

Hörst Ch.12 §5.7 + research consensus:

**Best active-rest activities for climbers:**
- Hiking
- Light jogging
- Easy mountain biking
- Limited less-than-vertical climbing (ARC-style — cross-reference [[05_aerobic_endurance_arc]])

**Key constraints:**
- Low enough intensity that you don't break heavy sweat or get pumped/winded
- 30-60 minutes total
- Should leave you feeling refreshed, not depleted

The reasoning: active rest enhances circulation, nutrient delivery to damaged muscles, and produces a general loosening effect. Sitting on the couch all rest day is suboptimal — but so is going for a hard run.

### 7. Stretching, massage, foam rolling

**Cross-fiber friction massage** (Hörst Ch.12 §4.2): the most effective stroke for climbers. Braced finger, simple push followed by short push back and forth across the muscle fiber. Keep stroke short and rhythmic; gradually increase pressure to penetrate deeper. Target muscles: finger flexors and extensors, pronator teres and brachioradialis (upper forearm), biceps/triceps, large muscles of shoulders and back.

**Direct-pressure technique** for trigger points: firm constant straight-in pressure with a braced finger, Armaid, wooden Bodo/shepherd's crook, or friend's elbow. Hold 15-30 seconds. Relieves spasms, increases local circulation, aids healing.

**Never apply sports massage tactics to tendons, joints, or injured tissues.**

**Foam rolling + mild stretching** to conclude. 10-20 min/day post-strenuous training is the standard prescription.

**CUE-02 — no heavy static stretching pre-performance** (see [[L0_safety_hard_rules]]). Static stretching ≥30 s of forearm flexors or other major working muscles reduces grip strength for up to 60 minutes (GTO inhibition + decreased musculotendinous stiffness). **Static stretching belongs between sessions or after climbing — never as warm-up before a hard effort.**

Dynamic mobility (joint circles, light traversing, warm-up repeaters at low load) is the pre-performance approach. Static stretching as part of cool-down or rest-day mobility work is fine.

### 8. DOMS and the "do I rest or push?" decision

Hörst Ch.12 §5.1: DOMS = signal that microtraumas occurred and at least another 24 hours of recovery is needed. Two options:

1. **Go climbing/work out anyway** — performance will be reduced; injury risk elevated; supercompensation truncated.
2. **Take 1-2 days off** — neuromuscular system recuperates to a level *higher* than before the workout (supercompensation curve).

**Proper rest is as important as training stimulus.** Under-resting is a primary injury cause; enthusiastic indoor and sport climbers are the most common culprits.

**Weekend trip exception:** given proper nutrition, good warm-up, and prudent approach, climbing through DOMS on a multi-day trip is acceptable — limited windows, real-world constraints. During regular indoor training or off-season, prefer rest. The trip exception is a calibration, not a license.

### 9. Cortisol, stress, and the personality dimension

Hörst Ch.12 §5.8 — strenuous exercise releases both growth hormone (anabolic) and cortisol (catabolic). Chronic stress and Type A aggressive behavior elevate baseline cortisol and depress growth hormone (Williams 1982). Behavior modification reverses this (Dinan 1994).

**Coach surface:** a relaxed approach to climbing, ability to laugh at bad sessions, calm disposition outside the gym — these are not motivational platitudes. They have measurable hormonal consequences for recovery. The coach can name this when a user reports overwork stress affecting training: stress management isn't separate from recovery, it's part of it.

### 10. Alcohol and recovery

Hörst's blunt summary: little quality nutrition, enhances dehydration, suppresses growth hormone response, possibly slows protein synthesis following exercise. The coach doesn't moralize ("don't drink"). Coach makes the trade-off transparent: alcohol the night after hard training hurts overnight recovery measurably. User decides. Process-orientation, not paternalism.

### 11. The proactive-recovery framing

Hörst's central thesis (Ch.12 opening): if you are not playing a proactive role in the recovery process, you are not training optimally. Recovery ability is a function of age, sex, and conditioning — but you can recover faster by intervening, not by waiting.

**The shift in framing:** recovery is not "what happens between training" but "the second half of training." The post-session 30-min window, sleep optimization, active rest between sessions, stress regulation, hydration — these are training inputs with the same evidence base as the training itself.

When a user asks "what's the one thing I should change?" and they're already training consistently, the answer is almost always a recovery lever — usually sleep.

## How the engine applies this

- **Rest interval prescription between sets:** 3-5 min for max-effort work, aligned to ATP-CP resynthesis curve (Bloomfield 1994).
- **Rest day frequency:** the 24-hour glycogen replenishment + 1-4 day Rebuild informs the engine's periodization: high-CNS-load days (max hangs, campus when permitted, limit boulder) get 48 hours between them; rest days are programmed at the macrocycle and weekly levels.
- **Active rest day content:** 30-60 min light activity (hiking, easy jog, ARC if user enjoys it). Engine's rest-day session structure follows this.
- **Sleep hygiene cue (D65):** post-session feedback and rest-day guidance include sleep prompts (target 7 hr min, 8-10 hr after hard training). Engine flags persistent sleep <6 hr as a recovery risk to escalate.
- **CUE-02 — no static stretching pre-performance** (cross-reference [[L0_safety_hard_rules]]). Warmup protocol uses dynamic mobility only.
- **30-min refueling window cue:** post-session feedback surfaces the 4:1 carb:protein guidance for users who report skipping post-climb meals.
- **G-Tox cue:** the Coach can prompt G-Tox at rest points in guided sessions (especially in PE/endurance content).
- **Adaptive deload triggering:** when fatigue proxies elevate (ACWR, RPE trend, sleep degradation, performance plateau), the engine inserts a mini-deload — cross-reference [[01_periodization]] §9.

## When user asks…

**"How much sleep do I really need?"**

7 hours is the floor for adults (IOC consensus). 8-10 hours is the target after strenuous training days, and the same range applies to adolescents always. Growth hormone surges during deep sleep — it's the dominant lever for tissue repair, protein synthesis, and muscle growth. One night below 6 hours can cut testosterone by ~25%. Charest & Grandner 2020 also found adolescent athletes sleeping <8 hours had significantly higher injury rates. If you're trying to optimize one thing, sleep before nutrition, before supplements, before harder workouts. Sleep extension and naps are the two interventions with the strongest evidence (Cunha 2023 SR).

**"Should I take a rest day or train through DOMS?"**

If you're following a regular training plan (not on a weekend trip), prefer the rest day. DOMS means microtraumas occurred and 24+ hours of recovery is needed. Pushing through it reduces performance, raises injury risk, and truncates the supercompensation window — the muscle returns to baseline but not above. That's where adaptation lives. The exception is multi-day trips where the window is limited and the choice is "climb sore on day 2 or don't climb at all" — given proper warm-up and prudent grade selection, climbing through trip-day-2 DOMS is normal. For routine indoor training, rest. Under-resting is the most common cause of plateau and tendinopathy.

**"What should I do on rest days?"**

Active recovery: 30-60 min of light activity that doesn't break heavy sweat — hiking, easy jog, mountain biking, or limited easy ARC climbing (less than vertical, big holds, talking pace). The constraint matters: low enough intensity that you finish feeling refreshed, not depleted. Active recovery enhances circulation and nutrient delivery to damaged muscles, and produces a loosening effect that sitting on the couch doesn't. Pair it with stretching/foam rolling 10-20 min, and consume meals at ~65:15:20 (cross-reference [[08_nutrition]] §2) with adequate carbs for Rebuild. Save static stretching for after sessions, never as warm-up before training.

**"I keep feeling exhausted even on rest days. What's going on?"**

Three possibilities to rule out before concluding "I need to train harder." (1) **Central fatigue** — nerve cells take up to 7× longer to recover than muscle cells (Bompa 1983), and dense high-CNS-load training (campus, max hangs, max-effort bouldering) accumulates over weeks. The fix isn't more rest *days*, it's an actual deload — full reduction in intensity and volume for 5-7 days. (2) **Under-fueling** — low energy availability is documented across climbing populations (Regulska-Ilow 2023, cross-reference [[08_nutrition]] §4) and produces exactly this pattern. Track intake for a week. (3) **Sleep debt** — even an hour short nightly accumulates. If you're at 6 hours when you should be at 7-8, that's likely your primary lever, not training adjustments. If the pattern includes missed periods, frequent illness, or persistent mood decline, the right escalation is a sports physician or dietitian, not a training change.

**"How do I recover better between hard climbs at the gym/crag?"**

Two interventions with the best evidence. (1) **Active rest between climbs:** instead of sitting and chatting, walk around for the rest period. Watts 2000 showed active rest cleared lactate ~35% faster than passive in climbers. For bouldering specifically, walking between attempts beats sitting (Draper 2006). (2) **G-Tox at mid-climb rests:** alternate the resting arm between dangling and raised-above-head every 5-10 seconds. Raised-arm uses gravity to aid venous return; alternation prevents upper-body fatigue from the raised position. Roberts 2003 university research confirmed noticeable recovery acceleration. Both cost nothing. Add: pre-hydrate (500 ml before session, then 240 ml/hr through), refuel within 30 min post-climb with ~100 g carb + 25 g protein. These four changes typically produce a perceptible difference within one session.
