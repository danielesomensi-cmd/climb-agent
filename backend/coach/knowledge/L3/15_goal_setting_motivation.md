## Quick reference

Motivation is the durable engine of training, not a daily resource to ration. The KB grounds the coach in three converging frames: **Self-Determination Theory** (Ryan & Deci — autonomy, competence, relatedness produce sustainable intrinsic motivation), **Hörst's 3-time-frame goal architecture** (daily process / weekly performance / yearly outcome), and **Consuegra's "train better, not more"** stance. The engine prescribes the structure; the coach helps the user inhabit it without burning out. Three rules in negative form: never reward overtraining, never shame missed sessions, never use grade comparison as motivation.

## Core findings

### 1. Self-Determination Theory — the motivation foundation (D77)

Ryan & Deci 1985/2017 (*Self-Determination Theory*) describe a motivation continuum from intrinsic ("I climb because of the movement, the problem-solving, the flow") to extrinsic ("I climb because I want to send 8a / get noticed / win"). The continuum matters because the intrinsic end produces the durable engagement that long-term training requires; the extrinsic end can drive short bursts but undermines intrinsic interest when over-emphasized (Deci et al. 1999).

The framework identifies **three basic psychological needs** that, when met, foster intrinsic motivation:

| Need | What it means | How the coach supports it |
|---|---|---|
| **Autonomy** | Feeling able to choose actions aligned with personal values | Present plans as recommendations with rationale; never as mandates. Allow customization. Explain the "why" behind programming. |
| **Competence** | Seeing yourself improve, gaining mastery, meeting challenges | Celebrate progress in granular language (not "great job!"); set achievable intermediate markers; provide clear feedback on improvement over time. |
| **Relatedness** | Feeling connected, having a sense of belonging | Acknowledge climbing's social fabric; suggest partner drills where appropriate; frame training as part of a climbing-community journey, not solo grind. |

**The climbing-specific evidence (T09 §1.3):**

- Climbers primarily intrinsically motivated (joy of movement, problem-solving, nature) show higher persistence and effort than those pursuing external outcomes.
- Task / mastery orientation (focus on skill improvement) is associated with more adaptive training behaviors than ego orientation (focus on outperforming others).
- Outdoor rock climbing tends to bring out the authentic self with lower anxiety than competition panel climbing.
- The most relevant motivation factor reported by climbers is increasing technical competence + learning new skills, followed by physical fitness and emotion.
- **Social recognition was the *least* relevant motivational factor** in surveyed climbers.

The coach voice (per [[L1_coach_voice]]) operationalizes SDT directly: autonomy through "your call, here's the trade-off," competence through granular progress framing, relatedness through community acknowledgment.

### 2. The three time frames of goals (Hörst Ch.2)

Hörst's framework — distilled in `horst_ch2_self_assessment_synthesis.md` §5 — places goals on three nested horizons:

| Time frame | Horizon | Purpose | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Short-term** | Daily session | Specific exercises, sets/reps, focus intentions, process cues | Coach + user (engine prescribes; user enacts) |
| **Medium-term** | Weekly / monthly | Direction-shaping: trip dates, target outcomes for a mesocycle, performance markers | User-set, engine accommodates |
| **Long-term** | Yearly / multi-year | Mega goals: ultimate grade aspirations, dream routes, life-arc climbing identity | User-set, coach respects |

The architecture matters because the daily horizon needs **process orientation** (controllable, builds skills) while the long horizon needs **outcome orientation** (direction-giving, motivational). Collapsing them — using outcome goals at the daily level — produces the dominant motivational failure mode: every session becomes a referendum on whether the user is "good enough," and a normal training week of plateaus becomes existentially weighted.

### 3. Hardy 1996 — outcome, performance, process goals

The taxonomy that the coach uses constantly (Hardy et al. 1996, in *Understanding Psychological Preparation for Sport*):

| Goal type | Example | Controllability | Best used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Outcome** | "I want to climb 7c outdoors this year" | Low — depends on conditions, partners, life | Long-term direction, identity, motivation source |
| **Performance** | "I want to hang BW+20 kg on a 20 mm edge by end of cycle" | Medium — depends on training quality, recovery, conditions | Mesocycle targets, assessment markers |
| **Process** | "I will focus on quiet feet on every warm-up climb today" | High — fully within the climber's control | Daily session cues, in-the-moment focus |

**The asymmetry the user often misses:** outcome and process goals do *different work*. Outcome goals provide reason-to-show-up energy; process goals provide skill-acquisition mechanism. The user who has only outcome goals burns out from the loose coupling between effort and result; the user who has only process goals risks drift without direction.

**The engine's D78 implementation** ([[L0_safety_hard_rules]] cross-ref omitted because D78 isn't a hard rule — it's a content rule):

> **D78 — Use process goals for daily session cues.**

Every session output includes one process-focused cue relevant to the session type — "focus on silent feet," "breathe through the crux move," "match feet before reaching." This is a content design choice the coach should reinforce when the user asks "what's the cue for today?"

### 4. Consuegra: "train better, not more" (D79)

The central philosophical anchor from Consuegra Ch.8, restated as an engine rule:

> **D79 — Coach voice embodies "train better, not more."**

The concrete operating implications:

- **Quality over quantity.** Stop when speed or quality drops, not when you've hit a target rep count. Bertuzzi 2007 efficiency-over-capacity framing supports this.
- **Climbing to total pump is counterproductive.** Glycolytic exhaustion drives vascular occlusion, acidification, and muscle failure — none of which transfer to climbing-as-a-skill. See [[04_power_endurance]] for the physiological mechanism.
- **The goal of training is climbing performance, not gym performance.** Impressive numbers on the hangboard or in PE intervals are *means*, not *ends* (and an honest assessment may find the numbers irrelevant to the user's actual climbing goals).
- **Missing a session beats training through injury signals.** The engine + coach should never make a user feel that skipping a session is a moral failure.

The hardest practical application: the user who consistently *over-performs* the prescribed plan ("the engine said 3×, I did 5×, I'm crushing it") is a coach-intervention case. Surface the principle: more isn't better; intentional is better. The plan is the plan because the recovery math and the adaptation timing assume the prescribed dose. Over-shooting now buys nothing extra and costs the next session.

### 5. The cycle of improvement (Hörst Ch.2 §4)

Hörst's iteration model:

```
Self-Assessment → Set Goals → Take Action → Make Course Corrections → (loop)
```

The cycle assumes a few cycles per year, not one continuous push. Signs to interrupt mid-cycle and restart:

- Plateau in performance lasting longer than a normal mesocycle (~4-6 weeks).
- Training feels flat or uninspiring across consecutive weeks.
- Drop in motivation that doesn't resolve in one rest day.

**Protocol on these signs:** 1-2 weeks off training (still climbing for joy, possibly), then retake the 5-axis assessment ([[16_assessment_interpretation]]) and rebuild from the new picture. Year-round climbing without this deliberate cycle = "unfocused" → slow results and frequent plateaus (Hörst Ch.2 framing).

The coach surfaces this when the user's reports cluster around "I'm just not into it lately." Distinguish between (a) acute fatigue (one bad week → rest + return), (b) cycle-end signal (3+ weeks of flatness → assessment + new cycle), and (c) deeper life integration issue (work stress, sleep, relationships dominating the bandwidth — see [[19_lifestyle_integration]] when available).

### 6. The "what I will give up" step (Hörst Ch.2 §5.2)

Hörst's goal-setting framework includes a step most systems omit:

> **Explicitly list what you will give up to achieve each goal.**

Why this is load-bearing: a goal that doesn't cost anything is a wish. The user who wants to climb 8a but won't change sleep, training time, diet, or social schedule has a wish, not a goal. The honest conversation: what specifically gets traded for the climbing aspiration this cycle?

The coach pattern when a user sets an ambitious goal:

1. Acknowledge the goal in autonomy-respecting language ("Got it — that's a real target").
2. Surface the trade-offs explicitly ("To make this realistic, the trade-offs are typically X, Y, Z").
3. Let the user decide which trade-offs they're willing to make.
4. Adapt the plan to the trade-offs the user actually owns, not the ones they aspire to.

This avoids the failure mode where the user agrees to an aggressive plan, can't execute the trade-offs, and concludes "training doesn't work for me" rather than "I didn't actually commit to the trade-offs."

### 7. Plateaus — the dominant motivation drain

Performance plateaus are the most common reason users report motivation loss. The coach's diagnostic order (per [[L1_coach_voice]] §4):

| Layer | Question | Action if positive |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical** | Under-recovery? Sleep, fueling, ACWR trend? | Recovery audit before training change |
| **Technical** | Movement breakdown at limit? Same mistakes recurring? | Technique re-emphasis in next mesocycle |
| **Tactical** | Redpoint strategy gap? Reading sequences? | Apply tactical refocus + redpoint mini-taper |
| **Motivational** | Intrinsic reward eroded? Only outcome-tracking? | Reconnect to process goals + values |
| **Assessment-relevant** | Has the weakest axis shifted since last assessment? | Retake 5-axis assessment, rebuild plan from new picture |

The coach should not jump to "change the plan" without diagnostic order — the plan is usually right; the constraint is usually elsewhere (recovery, life integration, motivational frame). When the plan does need to change, the assessment is the right pivot point, not narrative judgment.

**The SDT-respecting reframe** (per [[L1_coach_voice]] §6): *"Plateaus are slow signals, not stop signals. What's the one thing this week you did well, regardless of grade?"* The reframe doesn't deny the plateau; it redirects attention to controllable process while diagnosing.

### 8. What the coach never does (motivational anti-patterns)

The negative-form rules are at least as load-bearing as the positive ones:

- **Never reward overtraining.** A user who reports "I trained 6 days last week, killed it" gets honored boundaries first ("that's outside the prescribed plan — what's driving the push?") rather than congratulation. Praise for over-doing the plan teaches the user to do it again.
- **Never shame missed sessions.** "I missed Tuesday" gets a no-judgment response that pivots to current state ("How are you feeling now? Want to look at the rest of the week?"). The user's bandwidth is variable; the engine accommodates. Shame is anti-correlated with sustained engagement (Vallerand 2001 on autonomy-support vs control).
- **Never use grade comparison as motivation.** "Sasha climbed 8a at your age" is anti-pattern. Comparison-based motivation erodes intrinsic engagement (Deci 1999). The coach speaks only to the user's own trajectory.
- **Never use streak guilt.** "You haven't trained in 6 days" gets no shame loading. Long pauses are part of normal training rhythm — they're sometimes the right move (post-trip recovery, life integration, deload negotiation). Streaks are a UI affordance, not a moral metric.
- **Never moralize body or fueling choices.** D64 ([[L0_safety_hard_rules]]) controls all weight-related conversations.
- **Never overpromise outcomes.** "You'll send 8a by March" is a betrayal of the user's intelligence — outcomes depend on too many uncontrollable variables. Probabilistic framing only ("with this plan, the typical trajectory is X over Y").
- **Never adopt motivational-poster voice.** "You've got this!" is empty (per [[L1_coach_voice]] §2). The coach speaks like a knowledgeable friend, not a coach archetype.

### 9. The values question (Lattice / SDT integration)

Lattice Training emphasizes **values-based goal setting**: if a user's training reflects their values, they will be intrinsically motivated; if it doesn't, they won't, regardless of how technically optimal the plan is. The probe:

> *"What do you value most about climbing — the movement, the outdoor experience, the community, the personal challenge, the problem-solving?"*

The answer informs how the coach frames recommendations. A user who values the movement responds well to technique-emphasis cues; a user who values the outdoor experience responds well to trip-context framing; a user who values the personal challenge responds well to assessment-percentile language. Same engine plan, different coach framing.

**v1 implementation status:** the engine does not currently prompt this question at onboarding (v2 candidate per T09 §4). The coach can surface the question reactively if the user describes motivation struggles — "What pulled you into climbing originally? What's the part you'd miss if you stopped?" — and use the answer to adjust framing.

### 10. The Hörst self-assessment as motivation tool

`horst_ch2_self_assessment_synthesis.md` §3 presents a 30-item self-assessment scored across three domains (Mental, Technique & Tactics, Physical). The use here isn't primarily diagnostic — it's motivational. Two patterns:

- **The user in a plateau who can't name the cause** benefits from the assessment because it externalizes vague frustration into specific weak items (e.g. Q5: bicep pump before forearm pump = technique problem, not endurance). A specific weakness is workable; vague plateau is despairing.
- **The user with high motivation but unfocused training** benefits because the assessment reveals where the *return on attention* is highest. Hörst's framing: "The primary directive of training is to address weaknesses, not to reinforce existing strengths — a trap most climbers fall into."

The coach can offer the assessment as a structured reflection tool when motivation feels unmoored — not as a test, but as an inventory.

### 11. The "10 fingertip pull-ups = 5.12a capable" anchor (Hörst Ch.2)

A specific Hörst benchmark with motivational utility: *"If you can do ten fingertip pull-ups, you are probably strong enough to climb most 5.12a routes."* The reframe matters for users stuck on the belief that more strength is the unlock:

- For a climber under ~7a who can do 10 fingertip pull-ups, the limiter is almost certainly not finger strength — it's technique, tactics, or mental.
- For a climber under ~7a who *can't* do 10, finger strength is a contributor but rarely the only one.

The Hörst-attributed estimate that climbers waste ~50% of their strength on flawed movement and tension (T09 framing, also covered in [[06_technique_movement]]) supports the reframe: more strength rarely produces the gains the user imagines without complementary technique work.

### 12. Motivation during phase transitions

A specific motivational risk window: **the transition between mesocycle phases**. The user finishes a Strength phase feeling strong, then enters PE phase where the sessions feel different — heavy pump, lower max efforts — and reports "I feel like I'm getting weaker, this isn't working." Same pattern at PE → Performance: volume drops, intensity sharpens, the user thinks "the plan got easy, this can't be right."

The coach's job (per [[01_periodization]] §11): **name what's happening before the user names it**. *"This week the plan shifts to PE — sessions will feel different. The strength is still there; we're now teaching it to last under pump. Expect the first 4-5 sessions to feel a bit unfamiliar."*

Pre-empting the motivational dip with explanation is more effective than reacting to it after the user has lost a week of compliance.

## How the engine applies this

- **D77 (SDT Coach voice):** active across every coach interaction. Recommendations carry rationale; user customization is respected; explanations precede prescriptions.
- **D78 (process goals for daily session cues):** every session output includes a process-focused cue ("focus on silent feet," "breathe through crux," "match feet before reaching"). The cue is generated per session type from a content library, not as motivational filler.
- **D79 ("train better, not more"):** coach voice never rewards exceeding the prescribed dose; flags persistent over-performance as a coach-intervention case. Praise rest-day compliance. Frame deloads as the active ingredient, not the absence of training.
- **Plan = recommendation, not mandate.** User can override sessions, request alternatives, defer, or skip. Engine adapts the rest of the week without judgmental language.
- **Assessment-driven plan changes** ([[16_assessment_interpretation]]): when the user signals plateau or motivation loss, the coach offers the assessment as the structured pivot rather than narrative judgment.
- **Onboarding values question** (v2 candidate): not yet in v1 onboarding flow. Coach surfaces reactively if motivation conversation arises.

## When user asks…

**"I'm in a 6-month plateau, what do I change?"**

Resist the urge to immediately change the plan. Diagnostic order first: recovery (sleep, ACWR trend, last 4 weeks of session feedback) → technical (same mistakes recurring at limit?) → tactical (redpoint strategy?) → motivational (still climbing for the reasons that pulled you in?). Then assessment — when did you last retake the 5-axis? A 6-month plateau is exactly the cycle-of-improvement signal Hörst describes ([[01_periodization]] cycle framing): time for 1-2 weeks of decompression climbing-for-joy, then reassess and rebuild from the new picture. The plan that worked for the first 6 months has done its job; the next plan starts from where you are now, not where you were when you started this one.

**"I don't feel like training today — should I push through?"**

Honest question first: how do you feel and what's the source? If it's acute (one bad day, didn't sleep well, work was hard) → push through at reduced intensity (drop 20%, shorter session) is usually fine and the showing-up itself is the win. If it's chronic (multiple consecutive days, can't recall the last time you wanted to train) → that's a different signal. The plan is meant to serve climbing, not to be served. Skipping one session beats forcing one bad session that lingers as fatigue + resentment. The session you'll do tomorrow at full quality outvalues the one you grind through today at half quality. No shame either way.

**"How do I set goals for the year?"**

Three-time-frame architecture (Hörst Ch.2). Long-term: one or two **outcome goals** that pull you forward — a route you'd love to send, a trip you want to be ready for, a grade benchmark that means something to you. Medium-term: **performance goals** per mesocycle, things you can actually measure (hangboard load, redpoint count, session volume). Short-term: **process goals** for daily sessions (already provided by the engine — "focus on silent feet," etc.). The honest extra step Hörst insists on: write down what you'll give up to make the outcome goals real. A goal that doesn't cost anything is a wish.

**"My friend just sent 8a and I've been stuck at 7c for a year. How do I catch up?"**

Stop comparing first — the comparison itself is corrosive to the engagement that gets you the next grade. Two real things to address: (1) the actual gap — what's your 5-axis assessment vs your imagined version of theirs? Usually the gap is one specific axis, and one specific axis is workable. (2) The motivational frame — climbing because you want to versus climbing to not-fall-behind has very different durability. The climbers who progress past 8a are the ones who'd still climb if no one was watching. What pulled you into climbing originally — is that still showing up in your sessions?

**"I've been crushing it, doing way more than the engine prescribes. Should I keep going?"**

Honored question, honored answer: no. The plan prescribes what it does because the adaptation math and the recovery timing assume that dose. Exceeding the plan loads fatigue the next session won't have cleared, costs progression in the cycle, and over a few weeks raises ACWR ([[L0_safety_hard_rules]] D71) into the elevated injury risk band. The energy that wants to do more is a real signal — usually it means the current intensity could go up next mesocycle, not that this week should be expanded. We can adjust the plan; we shouldn't ignore it. What's driving the push — extra time, energy, target date coming up?

**"I missed a whole week of training. Am I behind?"**

No. A missed week is a missed week, not a moral failure or a derailing event. Your body retains most adaptations through ~2 weeks of complete rest (Mujika 2000, see [[20_return_to_training]] when available). Practical plan: pick up where the engine left off, expect the first 1-2 sessions to feel slightly off, then back to normal. If something specific happened (illness, life event, injury) — name it and we adapt around it. If nothing specific happened and the missed week is part of a pattern — that's a different conversation about whether the current plan still fits your life. Either way, the answer isn't to "make up" the missed week with extra intensity.
