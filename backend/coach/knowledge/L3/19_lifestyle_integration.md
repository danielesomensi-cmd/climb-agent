## Quick reference

Training lives inside life, not the other way around. The two non-negotiable principles: (1) **central fatigue compounds** — work stress, life stress, and training stress draw on the same nervous-system recovery pool, and the engine's load math (ACWR) only sees the training portion (Hörst Ch.12 §2.1.5, Bompa 1983: nerve cells recover up to 7× longer than muscle cells); (2) **SDT autonomy** — the user owns the life; the coach informs about tradeoffs and protects against harmful patterns, but never moralises about life choices ([[L1_coach_voice]] + [[15_goal_setting_motivation]]). Concrete operational moves: under sustained high life load, *reduce volume* (sessions, duration) before *reducing intensity*; protect sleep and one full rest day per week absolutely; treat concurrent training (running, cycling, lifting) as load — count it, don't pretend it's free.

## Core findings

### 1. Central fatigue: the invisible accumulator

Hörst Ch.12 §2.1.5 (citing Bompa 1983): **a nerve cell takes up to 7× longer to recover than a muscle cell**. That single number controls more of lifestyle integration than any other piece of physiology. Concretely:

- Sleep restriction, work stress, parenting load, and emotional load all draw on the same central-nervous-system recovery pool that high-recruitment training draws on (max hangs, campus, limit bouldering, redpoint attempts).
- The engine's ACWR (D69 — see [[17_readiness_overtraining]]) measures training load only. If the user's life stress is high, the *perceived* load is higher than the engine's number suggests.
- Symptoms of central fatigue (Hörst Ch.12 §2.1.5 / Bompa 1983): generalised malaise, inability to summon peak strength or power even after multiple rest days, slow movement / poor coordination, depressed mood. Most diagnostic: feeling "off" persists even after the muscles feel recovered.

**Operative rule for the coach.** When a user reports being chronically off, the first question is not "did you train too much?" — it's **"what's happening outside training?"** Sleep, work hours, life events. If life load is high, training-side adjustments (reduce volume, hold intensity) are the right move; if life load is normal, then look at the training prescription.

### 2. The hierarchy: what to cut, in what order

When life forces a reduction, the order matters. The wrong cut accelerates losses; the right cut preserves the core of what's been built.

**Cut-order under sustained life pressure (Hörst Ch.12 + Mujika 2003 detraining science — see [[20_return_to_training]]):**

1. **First, cut session volume** — drop a session per week, or shorten sessions by 25-40%. Volume is the dominant fatigue driver but the slowest fitness-decay axis (strength holds 2-4 weeks; aerobic capacity drops faster).
2. **Second, cut session frequency** — go from 4 to 3 sessions, or from 3 to 2. Maintain intensity in the remaining sessions.
3. **Last, cut intensity** — detraining of the neural / max-strength side starts inside 7-10 days when intensity drops. Avoid this cut unless health requires it (injury, illness, pregnancy, etc.).

**The wrong cut order** is "I'll do all the sessions but lighter." Doing all sessions at reduced intensity preserves the fatigue + life-cost of attending the gym but drains the strength stimulus first. Either be there hard or don't be there.

### 3. Concurrent training: climbing + other activities

UC22 — frequently asked. Climbers cross-train (running, cycling, lifting, yoga, other sports) and the engine doesn't see the cross-training as load. The honest framing:

**General principle (Hickson 1980, Wilson 2012 meta on the "interference effect"):** concurrent endurance and strength training *can* interfere — high-volume aerobic work (≥3-5 hours/week at moderate intensity) reduces strength-training adaptations slightly. Climbing already includes a strength/power side and a strength-endurance side; piling external endurance training on top compresses recovery and competes for the central-fatigue budget.

**Climbing + cross-training pairings, with the v1 guidance:**

| Pairing | What works | What doesn't |
|---|---|---|
| **Climbing + running** | Easy aerobic running (Zone 2, ~30-60 min) on rest days or after low-CNS climbing sessions — supports general aerobic capacity, recovery (Hörst Ch.12 §5.7 active rest) | Hard intervals + climbing on consecutive days; long-distance training (marathon prep) during a peaking block — central fatigue compounds |
| **Climbing + cycling** | Easy cycling as active rest is excellent (lower joint impact than running, supports recovery) | Hard cycling intervals competing with climbing intensity sessions in the same week |
| **Climbing + lifting (general strength)** | Pulling-focused lifting that complements pulling axis ([[03_pulling_strength]]) + lower-body work that doesn't compete (single-leg squats, hip mobility); antagonist lifting always good ([[12_antagonist_postural]]) | Heavy bench-press blocks scheduled the day before max-hang sessions (shoulder + tendon recovery overlap); high-volume hypertrophy work in a Performance phase (adds body mass + recovery debt) |
| **Climbing + yoga / mobility** | Generally additive — low CNS cost, supports recovery and mobility (Hörst Ch.13 antagonist principle 4: "stretch and foam roll daily") | Hot yoga / Bikram immediately before performance attempts (impaired strength up to 60 min post — same mechanism as CUE-02 static stretching, see [[L0_safety_hard_rules]]) |
| **Climbing + team sports** | Casual play on rest days fine | Contact-injury risk + unpredictable load make competitive team sport scheduling around peaking blocks a poor combo |

**Operative rule:** count cross-training as load. A 60-min hard run is a session; the engine doesn't see it but the coach should encourage the user to log it (a manual ACWR adjustment is better than a hidden one).

### 4. Work-stress interaction

The "I work 50 hours a week, can I progress at 8a?" question (audit §4.1 Q-25). Honest framing in three parts:

1. **Progress is still possible**, but the ceiling and pace differ from a full-time training scenario. Hörst (multiple chapters), Lattice public statements, and the r/climbharder community data all converge: weekly climbing volume + sleep + nutrition consistency matters more than total hours. A 4-sessions-per-week user with high consistency can outprogress an 8-sessions-per-week user with poor recovery.
2. **The leverage shifts toward quality**. With less time, every session has to count. "Train better, not more" (D79 — see [[L1_coach_voice]]) goes from a slogan to an operating constraint. Skip-the-warmup is not a valid corner to cut. Drop a session is.
3. **Sleep + nutrition leverage rises sharply** under work load. Watson 2017 (<7 h sleep + elevated injury risk in athletes) hits harder when CNS load is already high from work. The work-loaded user should defend sleep and food more aggressively than the user with abundant training time.

**What the coach does NOT do.** Promise that work-loaded users can match full-time training schedules. Moralise about hours worked. Suggest the user should work less (outside the engine's scope and SDT autonomy violation — see [[L1_coach_voice]] §4).

### 5. Acute life events: the planned-disruption protocol

Surgery, bereavement, moving house, new baby, major work crunch, pandemic. These are not "you should pause training" events automatically — they're "the engine and the user negotiate the right adjustment" events.

**General pattern (D77 / D78 SDT autonomy + [[20_return_to_training]] logic):**

- **Acute event, 1-2 weeks** → maintenance mode; the engine drops to 2 short sessions per week (40-50% normal volume) at full intensity. Strength holds (Mujika 2000); aerobic holds reasonably. Resume normal cycle when life clears.
- **Acute event, 2-6 weeks** → switch from progression to maintenance for the duration. Engine doesn't advance the macrocycle phase. Resume the *previous* phase, not the planned next phase, when the user returns.
- **Sustained life change, >6 weeks** → regenerate the macrocycle around the new reality. The engine's macrocycle is a contract with the user's available bandwidth; if bandwidth changed, the contract should change. The coach's job: surface the option, explain why ("your current macrocycle assumes 4 sessions/week; if you're realistically at 2, the engine can plan for that — the alternative is fake-completing sessions and confusing the closed-loop").

For injuries specifically, the cleavage point is acute vs chronic and severity — see [[10_injuries_fingers]], [[11_injuries_shoulder_elbow]], [[20_return_to_training]].

### 6. Sleep — the leverage point

Hörst Ch.12 §5.5 + Watson 2017:
- **Minimum** for athletes: 6-7 h/night.
- **Optimal** after strenuous training: 8-10 h/night.
- **<7 h sustained** correlates with elevated injury risk (Watson 2017).

For the work-loaded user this is the hard constraint. If sleep is consistently below 6-7 h, the training prescription should be honestly scaled. Hörst Ch.12 §5.5 explicit suggestion: identify low-value time sinks (TV, social media, certain low-relatedness social events) that can be reduced. The coach can surface the *math* (sleep deficit → recovery compromised → injury risk up → progress down) without moralising about *what* the user is doing with their non-sleep time.

A practical sequence for chronically sleep-deprived users: reduce volume (per §2), defend the rest day(s) absolutely, naps if life allows (20 min, before 3 pm; Hörst Ch.12 §3.5), evaluate sleep hygiene basics (caffeine cutoff, screens, room temperature) before adding any new training.

### 7. Cortisol, life stress, and the coach's framing

Hörst Ch.12 §5.8 (Williams 1982, Dinan 1994): chronic stress + Type A behavior → elevated cortisol (catabolic) + reduced growth hormone response → impaired recovery and adaptation. Mechanism aside, the *practical implication* is that high-stress periods absorb more recovery capacity than the engine's training-load math accounts for.

The coach's framing — *not* a stress-management lecture, but a load-math observation:

> *"During high-stress weeks, your training-side cost is higher than the engine sees. The fix isn't pushing through; it's matching the prescription to what the body is actually able to absorb. Reduce volume by a session; keep the remaining sessions at intensity. When the stressful period passes, the engine will pick up the progression — you won't have lost anything that holds 2-4 weeks (which is most of what strength training builds — see [[20_return_to_training]])."*

The coach does **not** prescribe meditation, breathwork, "self-care," or other lifestyle interventions outside training scope. SDT autonomy + scope: the coach is a training coach.

### 8. Recreational drug + alcohol patterns — narrow scope

The coach handles two specific patterns and stays out of the rest:

- **Alcohol around hard sessions** (Hörst Ch.12 §4.4: "avoid excessive alcohol — suppresses growth hormone, slows protein synthesis, enhances dehydration"). Surface as load math: alcohol the night of a hard session reduces overnight recovery; alcohol the night before a hard session reduces the hard session. The user decides what to do with the information.
- **Smoking and tendon healing** (Hörst Ch.12 §3.5 + Hörst Ch.13: smoking inhibits collagen production, slows tendon/ligament healing). Relevant specifically for users with a current finger / shoulder / elbow injury — the coach can mention that smoking is one of the variables that slows healing in injured climbers. Stops there.

Everything else (caffeine timing, recreational drug use, broader substance patterns) is outside the engine's scope. The coach does not diagnose addiction, does not prescribe behavior change in this domain, and refers to appropriate professionals if the user raises it.

## How the engine applies this

- **ACWR (D69)** — tracks training load only. The engine does *not* see external stress / cross-training; the coach surfaces the gap to the user honestly.
- **D71 (≤10% volume cap)** — defends against returning-from-life-event volume spikes; the cap resets against actual chronic load (see [[20_return_to_training]] §3).
- **Cut-order under reduced bandwidth** — when the user updates `availability` (sessions/week) downward, the engine prioritises preserving session intensity over session count.
- **Sleep / readiness** — the readiness checklist (see [[17_readiness_overtraining]] §5) is the v1 way work-stress + sleep deficit surface into the day-of decision.
- **Concurrent training** — engine v1 doesn't track external sports. Logging them is on the user (manual addition via outdoor / free-session logs is the closest current path).
- **Macrocycle regeneration** — for sustained life changes, `/api/macrocycle/start-new-cycle` is the right path (atomic: archive + goal review + regenerate).
- **What the engine does NOT do (v1):** automatic detection of life-stress periods, cortisol / HRV-based prescription adjustment, integration with calendar / work-hours data, sleep-tracker integration. These belong to v1.1+ candidates.

## When user asks…

**"I work 50 hours a week and have two kids. Can I still get to 8a?"**

Possibly — but not on the volume timeline a full-time-training climber assumes. Honest framing: weekly sessions matter, sleep matters, recovery matters, consistency matters. With 3-4 high-quality sessions/week + protected sleep + careful nutrition, progression continues. The ceiling and the pace are different from someone training 6 sessions/week, but the direction is the same. The leverage point under your constraints is **quality + recovery > volume** ("train better, not more" — D79). What the coach won't do: promise an 8a timeline (no honest one exists for any climber) or tell you that you need to work less. The coach will: help structure the 3-4 sessions you have to extract maximum signal.

**"I'm starting to run again — does that affect my climbing plan?"**

Yes. Easy aerobic running (Zone 2, 30-60 min) on rest days is generally additive and supports recovery — Hörst Ch.12 §5.7 active-rest principle. Hard interval running + climbing intensity sessions in the same week compete for the central-fatigue budget (Bompa 1983) — that combination should be sequenced or alternated, not stacked. Concrete: log the runs (manual addition) so the engine has visibility; flag if your readiness checklist starts to go yellow on weeks with both hard climbing and hard running. If you're training for a marathon, expect climbing progression to slow during the peak weeks of run training (interference effect — Wilson 2012).

**"I've been sleeping 5 hours for two weeks. Can I keep training?"**

You can, but the prescription needs to flex. Per §6: <7 h sustained is in the elevated injury-risk zone (Watson 2017). Practical sequence: drop one session this week (per §2 cut-order), keep intensity in the remaining sessions, protect the rest day(s) absolutely. Investigate what's driving the sleep deficit (work crunch, stress, kids, sleep hygiene) and address what's addressable. Don't add new training stimulus while sleep is compromised — the adaptation won't happen and the injury risk is real. If the pattern lasts >2 weeks and the readiness checklist is going red, that's the territory where the §6 referral discussion in [[17_readiness_overtraining]] applies.

**"I'm super-stressed at work. Should I push through or back off?"**

Back off the volume; protect the intensity. Per §7: high life-stress periods absorb more recovery capacity than the engine's training-load math accounts for. Concrete: drop a session, keep the remaining sessions at full intensity, defend sleep and one full rest day absolutely. When the stress period passes, the engine picks up the progression — what you preserved (strength + neural recruitment) is the highest-leverage stuff. What you don't push through is the climbing-volume side; that bounces back fast (1-2 weeks) once life clears.

**"I drink wine with dinner most nights. Is that a problem?"**

Honest answer: it's load on the recovery side. Hörst Ch.12 §4.4 specifically calls out alcohol as growth-hormone-suppressing, dehydration-enhancing, and protein-synthesis-slowing. A glass with dinner is not the same risk as binge patterns; the relevant question is whether you're hitting your training adaptations and recovery as expected. If progression has stalled and the readiness checklist shows yellow, alcohol is one variable to consider. Beyond that: the coach is not in the lifestyle-prescription business (§8 + SDT autonomy). You have the data; you decide. **What the coach will not do:** moralise or recommend abstinence.

**"Should I lift weights alongside climbing?"**

Depends on the goal. For pulling-strength axis development [[03_pulling_strength]], weighted pull-up work is *already* lifting — the engine prescribes it. For antagonist + injury-prevention [[12_antagonist_postural]], lifting is essential. For lower-body strength supporting climbing (single-leg squats, hip stability), occasional lifting is beneficial. For pure hypertrophy (building muscle mass for its own sake): generally counterproductive for sport climbing (mass-to-strength ratio matters — Mermier 2000, MacKenzie 2020) but reasonable for boulderers at certain levels. Don't add a heavy lifting block during a Performance phase — too much recovery competition. Bechtel's "Integrated Strength Training" is the gold-standard source on this (v1.1 will integrate; v1.0 is principle-level).

**"I'm moving to a new city and won't have a gym for a month. What do I do?"**

Treat as a planned-disruption (§5, 2-6 weeks). Switch the macrocycle from progression to maintenance for the duration. Set up minimum-kit at the new place if possible ([[18_equipment_fallback]] §4): hangboard + pullup_bar + bands = ~$80-160 of kit that covers most of the off-wall training space. Bodyweight + outdoor climbing if available. Don't try to redline the last sessions before the move to "bank" capacity (you can't); don't expect to redline the first sessions after re-joining a gym (the engine will catch up — see [[20_return_to_training]] §3 for the post-disruption ramp).
