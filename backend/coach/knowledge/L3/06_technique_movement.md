## Quick reference

Technique is the highest-ROI training lever in climbing, especially for beginners — elite climbers use as little as 20% of the energy of novices on the same circuit (Baláš 2014b). The engine treats technique as a primary axis trained through deliberate practice drills rather than strength work, with a phase-shifted prescription (beginners get more, advanced less). Coach principles draw from Hörst's motor-learning framework (cognitive → motor → autonomous stages) and the practice taxonomy (blocked → variable → random).

## Core findings

### 1. Why technique dominates returns for most climbers

Baláš 2014b (in Consuegra Ch.7) measured energy expenditure on identical circuits across skill levels. Elites used ~20% the energy of novices. Bertuzzi 2007 found that movement economy outweighs metabolism — a climber with poor technique can't compensate with fitness. The implication is uncomfortable for users who want a "training plan": for beginners and most intermediates, the largest unrealized gains live in technique, not in finger strength or PE intervals.

Footwork specifically: Baláš 2014a showed that placing more weight through the feet produced 11% less energy expenditure and 14% lower heart rate. Elite climbers produced more ATP via alactic + aerobic pathways and avoided glycolytic crossover at moderate intensities (Saul 2019). The pump that beginners experience on routes elites cruise is not just a fitness gap — it's a routing-of-energy gap driven by movement quality.

### 2. The motor-learning framework (Hörst Ch.4)

Hörst's three-stage model is the engine's mental model for "where is this user, and what kind of practice helps them right now?"

**Cognitive stage** — the user is thinking through every move. Movement is jerky, energy-wasteful, visually-driven (not feel-driven). Coach input is maximally valuable here: demonstrate skills, give specific verbal cues, correct *major* flaws (not minor details). The danger in this stage: thrashing up climbs and grooving bad habits. "Success by way of thrashing is a failure when it comes to learning."

**Motor (associative) stage** — the brain begins wiring neural associations through repetition. Movement becomes fluid and economical; energy expenditure drops. The user can self-diagnose with proprioceptive cues. Coach input should *decrease* — the climber needs space to feel what right vs. wrong execution feels like.

**Autonomous stage** — movement is automatic; conscious attention frees up for sequencing, problem-solving, mental state. Takes more than ten years of dedicated practice to reach reliably. Even elites still refine technique.

**Implication for the engine and the coach:** the same drill (e.g. silent feet) means different things at different stages. For a cognitive-stage user, it's *learning* the skill. For a motor-stage user, it's *refining* the proprioceptive feedback loop. For an autonomous-stage user, it's *maintenance* and unconscious wiring. The drill doesn't change; the framing the coach uses around it should.

### 3. Skill transference is small or absent

Hörst Ch.4 + research consensus: practice in crack climbing improves crack climbing but not face climbing. Cross-training activities (Hacky Sack, slack-lining, etc.) develop cognitive attributes (focus, balance) but contribute negligibly to climbing skill. **You must climb to improve climbing.** Within climbing, transfer is also narrow: bouldering improves bouldering skill, not (directly) lead skill. Saeterbakken 2021 found that 5 weeks of discipline-prioritized training in advanced climbers didn't decrement the other discipline (so transfer isn't zero), but the asymmetric transfer pattern is real.

Practical coaching: when a boulder-focused user wants to improve trad lead, no amount of fingerboard work will substitute for time on trad routes. The engine differentiates by discipline; the coach reinforces that specificity is non-negotiable for technique.

### 4. Proprioception as the trainable expert skill

Hörst's central claim about technique: the awareness and diligent use of proprioceptive cues separates master climbers from the mass. Proprioception lives on a continuum: beginners feel only coarse cues (foot placement quality, balance, pump level); elites detect a steady stream of subtle internal signals.

**The key habit (transferable to any session):** when practicing a new skill or working a crux, ask yourself *"How does it feel when I do it the right way (most efficiently) compared with when I do it the wrong way?"* Making this distinction empowers on-the-fly correction. Many falls off rehearsed crux moves come from inattention to proprioceptive cues, not absence of strength.

This is one of the highest-ROI coaching cues the engine can surface and costs nothing — a post-attempt question rather than a prescription.

### 5. The five attributes of high economy (Hörst Ch.4 §6.4)

Economy = doing each move with minimum energy expenditure. Five components, all trainable:

| Attribute | What it is | Coach cue |
|---|---|---|
| **Quiet feet** | Feet that don't pop off or skid; foot placement is silent | "If you hear your feet, you're losing energy. Place quietly enough that you can't hear it." |
| **Rhythm + momentum** | Step-step-reach-reach (or context-appropriate variant), continuous motion through cruxes | "Don't stop at the crux to plan — plan from the rest below, then flow through." |
| **Smooth moves + relaxed body** | Only contract muscles needed for the move; antagonists stay passive | "Scan for tension in shoulders, jaw, free arm. Let what isn't working be soft." |
| **Pace** | Move as briskly as technique allows; back off pace if technique degrades | "The right pace is the fastest you can climb without skidding or botching sequence." |
| **Steady breathing** | Deep belly breaths, especially at rests and through cruxes | "Restricted breathing = tight muscles. Reset breath at every rest." |

These are not five separate skills — they're five symptoms of the same underlying capacity. Improving any one tends to improve the others. The Coach can surface them as a 5-point post-attempt self-check.

### 6. The Left-Right Rule

Maximum stability comes from pairing a left hand with a right foot, or a left foot with a right hand. Ascending a ladder is the intuitive case — the body refuses to barndoor. On climbing walls, hold position and shape complicate this, but the principle is the engine of intuitive sequence-finding.

When a user asks "what's the best beta?", a good first-pass heuristic is: which left-right pairings are available? On harder climbs the rule breaks down (a sequence may demand a same-side combination), but it's a strong starting point and the failure to apply it explains most beginner barndooring on overhangs.

### 7. Practice taxonomy: blocked → variable → random

The single most important practice-design insight from Ch.4. Five practice types, with strict progression rules:

**Blocked practice** — identical repetitions of one move. Fast skill acquisition. Use during initial 2-3 successful trials of a new skill. **Beyond the first few successful trials, blocked practice is for blockheads.** Continued blocked practice produces a false sense of mastery and poor transfer to novel settings.

**Variable practice** — same skill, different conditions (angle, hold size, friction, body position). This is where schema-rules develop — the underlying rules that let the brain apply a motor program to novel situations. Practice a drop-knee on a vertical wall with positive holds, then a slight overhang with smaller holds, then a steeper section with poor footholds.

**Random practice** — link a sequence of *different* moves back-to-back. Hörst's Stick Game: a partner points (with a broomstick or laser pointer) the next hold to use, forcing on-sight sequence execution. Another version: climb 10-15 widely different routes (slab, finger crack, overhang, roof) in rapid succession.

**Fatigued practice** — skills you already possess, practiced under fatigue. Solidifies retention; "wires it in." Crucial point: never use fatigued practice for *new* skills — fatigue impairs the learning of novel motor patterns.

**Session structure that uses all four:**
- Minutes 0-30 (fresh): new skills, blocked practice if literally first attempts
- Minutes 30-90 (warm): variable practice on recent skills, normal climbing volume
- Minutes 90+ (fatigued): random practice + reinforcement of known skills

This maps directly onto how the engine sequences a session — warm-up + new-skill drills first, then climbing volume, then power-endurance or volume-tired work. The coach can name *why* the order matters when a user wants to flip it.

### 8. The drill catalog (v1)

Drills covered in current research files + Hörst Ch.4. These are the v1 drill set; Bechtel/Mobråten will expand it but not replace it.

**Footwork precision**
- **Silent feet** — climb at 2-3 grades below onsight; place each foot so silently no sound is audible. If it makes noise, return and retry. Develops awareness and proprioception. Mandatory warm-up drill (D74).
- **Sticky feet** — once your foot touches a hold, it cannot adjust ("as if glued"). Forces first-attempt precision; develops sequencing awareness because you must plan foot orientation before placement.
- **Target practice** (Hörst) — on a technical route 1 grade below onsight, sustain focus on feet: identify the best spot on every foothold, place toe with laser precision, stare at the foot for 2-3 seconds before continuing.
- **Tennis ball hands** (Anderson & Anderson) — slab climbing with tennis balls in hands so palms can't touch holds. Forces instep technique, hip opening, complete reliance on footwork.
- **No-hands slab** — climb a slab section using only feet. Develops balance, weight shift, foot trust. Even V0 is challenging without hands.

**Body position / movement**
- **Tracking** — climb a route placing feet on the exact same holds used by hands. Makes a route significantly harder; trains foot focus. Pick routes 2 grades below normal for this.
- **Downclimbing** — downclimb every warm-up route or boulder. Forces foot focus (you lead with feet going down) and is excellent for proprioception. Eccentric strength > concentric, so it's also a sneaky strength stimulus.
- **First Touch** — climb a route using each handhold exactly the way you first grab it. No readjusting. Trains route reading from the ground.

**Core-on-wall drills (Matros 2013, via Consuegra Ch.8)**
- **Freeze** — climb a problem with no dynamic moves; pause 2-3 seconds in position after each move. Trains core, lock-off, technique, efficiency simultaneously.
- **Tic Tac Toe, Diagonal, Get'em!, Feet Forwards, Hang Around** — wall-based core exercises documented in Consuegra Ch.8.

**Sequence + skill practice**
- **Stick Game** (Hörst) — partner points the next hold with a broomstick. Trains on-sight sequence execution under uncertainty. Best random-practice drill in the catalog.
- **Traverse with elimination** — traverse using only two fingers per hand, or only open-hand grip, or only side pulls, or "cross-through only." Develops specific technical patterns through constraint.
- **Speed training** — climb wired routes (1-2 grades below max) several times, each ~10% faster than the previous, backing off at first technique degradation. Hörst: "Climbing quickly is primarily a function of skill, not strength. The less strength and endurance you possess, the more important this skill becomes."

**Mental / cognitive**
- **Singular Focus Drill** — climb a route 2 grades below max, focusing on one aspect of movement only (e.g. only foot placements, or only grip light-touch). When focus wanders, redirect. Builds mental endurance and focus control. Cross-reference: `07_mental_fear_focus.md`.
- **Route preview protocol** — structured 2-minute preview before climbing: identify rests → plan crux sequence → visualize clipping positions → note hazards. Cross-reference: `07_mental_fear_focus.md`.

The engine prescribes these via the technique-session catalog. The coach can describe any of them when asked or when a session resolves to "technique drills" without obvious specificity.

### 9. Beta dependency — the developmental trap

Hörst on beta: it's extrinsic feedback in its most condensed form, and it's invaluable during the cognitive stage. Beyond the cognitive stage, continued use of beta handicaps the development of problem-solving skills. Long-term beta reliance produces a false sense of ability — performance degrades sharply when beta isn't available.

**The practice rule from Hörst Ch.4 §4.3:** *"Beyond first few months as a climber, make it a practice to shut off beta from your partner."* Exception: safety beta (gear placement warnings) or speed when truly needed.

The coach should mention this proactively when a user reports relying heavily on partner beta — it's invisible to most users that this is the bottleneck on their on-sight grade.

### 10. Fundamental techniques (Hörst Ch.4 §6 — taxonomy)

Hörst's Table 4.1 self-assessment lists 12 fundamental techniques rated on a 4-level scale (Excellent / Good / So-So / Poor). The 12:

1. Precise, quiet foot placements that carry your weight
2. Handholds gripped lightly; arms in a secondary role
3. Use of the Left-Right Rule for stable movement; economy in statics, pace, posture
4. Use of rest positions
5. Use of non-cheezy and non-obvious handholds (side pulls, undercling, slopers)
6. Use of flagging to add stability and prevent barndooring
7. Hand-foot matching and mantling
8. Twist lock, backstep, efficient movement on overhanging terrain
9. Creative footwork (heel hooks, toe hooks, knee locks)
10. Dynamic moves (deadpoints, lunges)
11. Dynamic route climbing core and respect
12. Jam crack climbing

This is a useful checklist for the coach. When a user asks "what should I work on?", and the engine doesn't have axis-level guidance to give, walking through this list (often via the self-assessment in onboarding) surfaces gaps. v2 may incorporate this as a structured technique sub-axis assessment (D-FRA / future audit candidate).

### 11. Specific techniques to know (coach reference)

The coach doesn't teach these in conversation — that's video / in-person territory — but should be able to describe them when asked.

**Twist lock + backstep** — on overhanging terrain, the way to put weight back on the feet. Turn the hip opposite the pulling hand into the wall; use the outside edge of the foot below or behind the body; press in unison while the lead arm pulls in. Limit early practice to ~20° overhang; expand as skill builds.

**Heel hook** — pull the heel toward your rear end to maximize leverage; opposite foot inside-edges or smears for balance. Use to turn lips, top out boulders, or cop a steep-terrain shakeout.

**Toe hook** — hook as much of the laces side of the shoe as possible on a protruding hold. Typically paired with an opposing push from the other foot. Roof navigation.

**Knee bar** — toe into a hold, knee bracing against a larger opposing surface. On overhanging walls, sometimes the only available rest. Solid knee bars allow no-hands inverted rests.

**Flagging** — when no left-right pairing exists, the free leg flags (out, across, or behind) to shift center of mass. Two variants based on whether the supporting foot is inside-edging or outside-edging.

**Deadpoint** — calculated, controlled dynamic move using the moment of stillness at the peak of an upward bounce. Not a lunge — a precision tool when a single-hand static stance isn't holdable.

**Lunge (dyno)** — full-body launch toward an out-of-reach hold. **Last-resort move**: strenuous, stressful, dominant cause of climbing shoulder injuries. Lunge only when static would cost more energy than the launch.

### 12. Crack climbing — a separate motor program

Crack technique is largely orthogonal to face technique. Finger jams, hand jams, fist jams, foot jams, lieback — these require completely novel motor programs and schema-rules. A climber operating at V8 face may flail on V2 cracks. The engine flags this asymmetry when crack frequency is reported low; the coach should never assume face strength transfers.

Practical: toprope first. Use the security to experiment with subtle variations (thumb-up vs thumb-down hand jams; constriction-seeking vs continuous jams). Climb single-pitch cracks a few times in a row — refine the finesse moves. Skill develops faster than fitness in this domain.

### 13. The practice vs performance distinction (Hörst's "stupid training")

Hörst's name for the most common training error: spending all session time climbing at maximum difficulty. The climber repeats poor execution under stress, grooves bad habits, and may eventually tick the route — but learns no transferable skill in the process. Hörst calls this *stupid training*.

The alternative: dedicate a block of time when fresh to *practice* on routes 1-2 grades below limit. Performance climbing has its place (Performance phase, redpoint attempts), but it shouldn't dominate. Hörst's guideline: ~1/3 time on routes beyond limit (boundary-stretching), ~2/3 on routes that allow focused practice and weakness work.

The engine's session structure embeds this — technique drills + sub-limit volume in Base, performance climbing concentrated in Performance phase. When a user asks "why am I climbing easy stuff in Base?", that's the answer: this is *practice*, and practice is the slow lever that lifts the ceiling.

### 14. Movement quality metrics (research context, not engine inputs)

For completeness — these are research tools, not currently practical for a consumer app:

- **Jerk coefficient** (Seifert 2014) — third time derivative of hip position. Captures fluency (spatial + temporal). Decreases with practice. Requires IMU.
- **Geometric Index of Entropy** (Cordier 1993) — spatial efficiency of trajectory vs. convex hull.
- **Immobility-to-Mobility Ratio** (Orth 2017) — time still vs. moving.
- **Contact time per hold** (Seifert 2020) — fluency indicator. Requires instrumented holds.

The engine uses *proxies* instead: onsight/redpoint gap (large gap = strength outpacing technique), board grade vs. finger-strength MVC (low board grade relative to MVC = technique is the limiter), self-reported technique scores. These are coarser but actionable today. Future iterations (v3+) may incorporate wearable IMU data if available.

### 15. Route reading — the cognitive-motor pre-climb skill

Sanchez 2012, Seifert 2017, Medernach 2024 converge: route preview quality directly affects climbing fluency (lower jerk coefficient). Experts preview more efficiently — shorter durations, fewer scans, superficial scanning rather than detailed. Eye-tracking studies (embodied planning, 2024) found holds looked at during preview are used 2× more often during climbing, and fixation duration is longer for holds subsequently used.

**Engine prescription:** structured pre-climb preview cue (extends D28). Coach guidance:
1. Identify rest positions first (where can I shake out?)
2. Plan the crux sequence (which holds in which order; which way to face)
3. Visualize clipping positions (or top-out, for boulder)
4. Note hazards or commit points

This costs nothing and measurably improves climbing. Often a user's "fitness ceiling" is partly a route-reading ceiling.

## How the engine applies this

- **Technique drill allocation by experience level (D73):** beginners get ~30% of session time on technique drills, intermediates ~20%, advanced ~10-15%. The proportion decays as climbing volume itself becomes the dominant technique stimulus and other axes (PE, strength) need the time.
- **Silent feet as mandatory warm-up component (D74):** included in default warm-up protocol alongside D33 warm-up generation.
- **Route preview cue (D75):** the guided session shows a structured route-preview checklist before the main climbing block. Extends D28.
- **Drill catalog (D76):** the engine ships with the drills documented in §8 above. Future expansion via Bechtel/Mobråten will be additive.
- **Practice-vs-performance phase weighting:** Base and PE phases emphasize practice routes (sub-limit volume + drills); Performance phase concentrates on max-effort projecting. Performance volume drops to make space for redpoint quality.
- **Onsight/RP gap and board-vs-MVC ratio** are technique proxies surfaced in the assessment and `16_assessment_interpretation.md`.

## When user asks…

**"What technique drills should I do?"**

Start with three: silent feet (warm-up, every session), downclimb your warm-ups, and one weekly Stick Game or random-practice equivalent. That's enough to move the needle for most users. The engine already prescribes the first two by default; the third needs a partner. After 2-4 weeks of consistent practice, ask which feels most uncomfortable — that's where your next drill should target. Don't try the full catalog at once — pick 2-3, run them long enough for the proprioceptive feedback loop to develop.

**"Why am I climbing easy routes in Base? I want to push my grade."**

Two things. First, Base is about building the engine — capillary, mitochondrial, technical, postural — and that engine builds at sub-limit intensity (D44, D45). Pushing grade now converts the stimulus to a different (less useful) adaptation. Second, what Hörst calls *practice* — focused work below your limit — is what raises the ceiling. *Performance* — projecting at limit — is what tests the ceiling. They're different activities. The plan does both, sequenced: practice in Base/Build, performance concentrated in Performance phase.

**"Should I keep working this same hard project or move on?"**

Depends on stage. If you're in the cognitive stage on this route — still discovering the sequence, still finding moves you didn't see — keep working it (blocked practice is fine in early trials). Once you've wired the sequence and the bottleneck is just executing it on the day, you've crossed into pure performance work. At that point, link-ups (chunking down) and varied attempts (different starts, different rest tactics) are where the gain lives, not more identical attempts. And if frustration is rising and self-talk is going negative, Hörst's rule applies: move on to another problem before grooving the failure. Come back fresh.

**"How do I get better at on-sight climbing?"**

Three levers. First, route reading: spend 2 minutes per route reading from the ground using the structured protocol — rests, crux, clipping, hazards. Second, random practice: link sequences of widely different moves back-to-back, or use the Stick Game with a partner. Third, shut off beta from your partner. Hörst is direct on this: long-term beta dependency caps on-sight ability because you don't develop the problem-solving schema. The on-sight grade gap closes when you train the cognitive piece, not just the physical one.

**"My technique feels worse when I'm tired — should I stop the session?"**

It depends what you're practicing. New skills need fresh — fatigued attempts at new motor patterns can groove the wrong wiring. Known skills (silent feet on routes you've done before, downclimbing warm-ups) benefit from fatigued practice — that's how retention solidifies. Hörst's session structure: new skills first 30 min, climbing volume in the middle, fatigued reinforcement of known skills at the end. If you're hitting technique breakdown mid-session, that's the signal to stop *learning* new things, not the signal to stop the session.

**"How important is preview before climbing?"**

Measurable and underused. Seifert 2017 showed preview quality directly reduces jerk (smoother climbing) and the ratio of exploratory to performatory movements. Experts preview faster and more efficiently — it's a trainable skill, not just a habit. Use a structure: rests first, then crux sequence, then clipping (or top-out for boulder), then hazards. 2 minutes per route is enough for most. If you skip it, you're climbing the route partly as discovery — costing energy on every move where you have to figure out which way to face.
