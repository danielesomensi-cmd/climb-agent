## Quick reference

Shoulder is now the **2nd-most-common climbing injury site** (17%, up from 5% a decade earlier — Schöffl 2015), driven primarily by overhanging indoor terrain, campus training, and dynamic moves. Elbow is 4th (9%). Both share the same underlying setup: muscle imbalance built by years of pulling without corresponding push/antagonist/postural work (Hörst's "climber's posture"). The two highest-frequency shoulder diagnoses are SLAP tear (33%) and impingement (26%); for elbow, medial epicondylitis ("climber's elbow") and lateral epicondylitis dominate. Prevention is unambiguous and evidence-supported: **strength training is the only intervention with direct evidence of preventing shoulder and elbow injuries in climbers** (Quarmby 2023 systematic review). The engine's role is making that antagonist training non-skippable (D58, D60), capping volume progression (D71), and gating campus / heavy hangboard behind experience minimums (D35, D41).

## Core findings

### 1. The root cause: "climber's posture"

Hörst Ch.6 calls it out explicitly (Figure 6.2): years of climbing-only training produces a predictable postural signature — tight pectorals, tight biceps, weak lower trapezius, weak rotator cuff (especially external rotators), forearm imbalance with strong flexors and weak extensors. The visible result is rounded shoulders and upper-back kyphosis ("climber's back," Förster 2009). The biomechanical result is that the head of the humerus elevates and the scapula doesn't stabilize properly during overhead arm movement — which is **most climbing movement.**

Why this matters: the same posture that produces impingement also predisposes to medial and lateral epicondylitis (forearm muscle imbalance), to instability (loose anterior shoulder capsule + weak posterior stabilizers), and to SLAP tear under dynamic load. **A single training imbalance produces a cluster of injuries.** Fixing the imbalance is correspondingly the single most leveraged injury-prevention intervention.

This is the empirical basis for engine decision D58 (postural correction exercises anti-climber's-back) and the L0 / D60 commitment to wrist-extensor work as default antagonist content.

### 2. Shoulder injuries — distribution and mechanism

Hörst Ch.13 Table 13.2:

| Diagnosis | Frequency |
|---|---|
| SLAP tear | 33% |
| Impingement syndrome | 26% |
| Shoulder sprain | 11% |
| Dislocation | 10% |
| Supraspinatus tendinitis | 5% |
| Instability | 5% |
| Tendinosis of long biceps tendon | 3% |
| Rupture of long biceps tendon | 3% |
| Rotator cuff tear | 3% |

The shoulder injury rate has **jumped from 5% to 17%** of all climbing injuries in a decade (Schöffl 2015). Hörst's primary attribution: "the recent proliferation and inappropriate use of campus training boards," combined with the move to overhanging indoor terrain. Each of these activities loads the shoulder joint dynamically through ranges of motion where the rotator cuff and scapular stabilizers must hold the humeral head in the glenoid — and if those stabilizers are weak, the joint articulates against soft tissue rather than against the labrum and capsule.

**Impingement / bursitis / tendinitis.** Closely related conditions, common in any sport demanding overhead arm movement. Mechanism: insufficient rotator cuff strength → humeral head elevates → tight biceps and pecs internally rotate the shoulder → weak rhomboids and trapezius fail to position the scapula properly for overhead movement → soft tissue (subacromial bursa, supraspinatus tendon) gets pinched on every overhead reach. Treatment shape: ice, brief NSAIDs if swelling is present, **cessation of climbing and overhead hand movement** during the acute phase. Once pain subsides — gradual rotator-cuff and scapular-stabilizer rehab guided by a physio. Minor cases dedicated to rehab: 1–2 months to return. Serious cases: 6+ months. The "see a physio" call here is high-value because the specific eccentric loading and scapular cueing matters more than generic rest.

**Shoulder instability.** Increasingly common among high-end climbers who specialize in overhanging routes and in users who hangboard with shrugged, relaxed shoulders. Mechanism: long-term repeated exposure to straight-arm hangs, Gaston moves, severe lock-offs → chronic stretching of capsular ligaments + atrophy/weakness of stabilizing antagonists → joint becomes loose. Once loose, every move risks subluxation or further capsular damage. Treatment is also two-phase: rest until pain diminishes, then rotator-cuff strengthening rehab (2–6 months) before climbing reintroduced. **Avoid all overhead motion** that provokes pain during the rehab window.

**SLAP tear** (superior labral tear from anterior to posterior). Now the **#1 shoulder diagnosis in climbers** (33% of shoulder injuries, Hörst Ch.13). Symptoms: aching after a hard session, a "catching" sensation in the shoulder during climbing, shoulder pain when sleeping on the side, decrease in arm and grip strength (because the central governor de-rates the system when it perceives joint instability). Mechanism: out-of-control dynamic arm moves, campus training with straight-arm grabs, near-limit overhanging climbing in a fatigued state. **Almost always requires arthroscopic surgical repair.** Post-surgical rehab is long: weeks of ROM, ~2 months of slow strengthening, full return ~6 months. An extremely fit climber with a lower-grade SLAP returns fastest. The biggest predictor of needing this surgery in the first place is climbing dynamically when fatigued — the engine's RPE-trend + ACWR monitoring (D69, D70) and the "don't climb to complete exhaustion" rule (Hörst Ch.13 Tip #7) target this directly.

**Straight-arm passive hanging — the specific mechanism to know.** Hörst Ch.13 explicitly calls out five situations to avoid:

1. Dropping all the way to a straight-arm position with relaxed shoulders during pull-ups.
2. Hangboard training with fully straight arms and shrugged shoulders.
3. Hanging on a straight arm with completely lax shoulder/scapular stabilizers at a rest position on overhanging climbing.
4. Catching a lunge with a fully extended arm and shoulder.
5. Any campus move (especially drop-downs and long upward grabs) that forcefully engages a straight arm and extended shoulder.

The common thread: the shoulder is loaded with stabilizers disengaged. The fix is teaching engaged-shoulder cues (scapular depression and retraction — Hörst's "best climbing exercise nobody does" is the scapular pull-up, EX-SCAP-04 in the catalog) and avoiding the load pattern entirely when fatigued.

### 3. Elbow tendinopathy — medial and lateral

Pain near the medial (inside) or lateral (outside) epicondyle is one of the most common chronic climbing complaints. Onset is almost always gradual: microscopic injury that fails to repair before the next training session, accumulating tendinosis over weeks and months. **Tendon repair is ~10× slower than muscle repair** (Hörst Ch.13) — eventually muscle force exceeds the tendon's adapted tensile strength and microtrauma outpaces repair.

**Three injury types — important distinction:**

- **Tendinosis** — gradual onset, degenerative (not primarily inflammatory) collagen disorganization. **Most common type.** Ice and NSAIDs largely ineffective past initial days.
- **Tendinitis** — acute onset during a single hard move, palpable swelling, primarily inflammatory in the acute phase. Less common than tendinosis but easier to recognize.
- **Tenosynovitis (paratenonitis)** — inflammation of the outer tendon layers and the synovia-lined sheath.

Climbers and even general medicine often use "tendinitis" generically — the distinction matters because the treatment differs. Mature tendinosis needs progressive eccentric loading (under physio guidance), not rest + ice + NSAIDs. The Cook 2017 continuum model of tendon pathology underlies this — climbers benefit from knowing it because "just rest it" is the wrong strategy for the most common variant. **And:** after acute tendon injury, repaired tendon strength can remain 30% below baseline for months or years (Lead-better 1992; Liu 1995). Patient rehab matters.

**Medial epicondylitis (golfer's / climber's elbow).** Pain at the medial epicondyle in the tendons connecting the pronator teres and finger flexors. Root cause mechanism:

- All finger flexors anchor to the medial epicondyle.
- Hand pronator muscles (turning palm outward — required for almost all climbing grips) also originate there.
- Biceps contraction supinates (turns palm up), but climbing requires sustained pronation.
- The battle between the supinating biceps pull and the necessity to maintain pronated grip strains the often-undertrained pronator teres and its attachment.
- Contributing factors per Hörst: climbing too often, finger training too hard, resting too little.

**Lateral epicondylitis (tennis elbow).** Pain at the lateral epicondyle in the tendons connecting the finger/wrist extensors. Extensors are antagonists to the flexors used in gripping — they're chronically disproportionately weak. Key insight: grip strength is greatest when the hand is in the **extended wrist** position (which is why the arm "chicken-wings" away from the wall when fatigued — the wrist extends, recruiting more flexor force). Chronic chicken-winging on small crimps loads the extensor attachment beyond its tensile capacity. **Reducing crimp-grip use lowers risk** (Hörst Ch.13 direct quote).

**Two-phase treatment framework** (Hörst Ch.13, qualified for engine use):

> ⚠️ The full protocol below requires medical guidance. The coach can describe the general shape and modify training around it; it does not prescribe rehab loading.

- **Phase I — withdraw and de-inflame.** Stop climbing and climbing-specific training. Icing 20 min, 3–6×/day if there's palpable swelling; **brief** NSAID course only with physician guidance (see L3/10 §6 — chronic NSAID use likely impairs tendon healing). Duration: 2 weeks to 2 months depending on severity. Cortisone injection is sometimes proposed but Nirschl 1996 notes potential detriment to healing — defer to your physio's call.
- **Phase II — rehab the imbalance.** Forearm-muscle imbalance is primary, so the rehab is **specific**: hand-pronation strengthening for medial tendinosis, hand/wrist-extension strengthening for lateral tendinosis. Pull-ups, fingerboarding, and campus training are typically problematic during Phase II and should only be done in small doses if at all. Avoid any exercise that causes sharper pain or lingering/worsening pain afterward. Mild stretching + light massage + heating pad (10–15 min, 3×/day) once pain has subsided.

**Phase III — return to climbing.** After 3–4 weeks of pain-free Phase II training, gradual reintroduction starts with low-angle / easy vertical routes. Limit crimp use. Cease climbing and return to Phase II if pain returns. Continue the forearm pronator/extensor strengthening **indefinitely** — Hörst's framing: "as long as you are a climber, you must engage in these preventive measures." Failed rehab and relapse can eventually require surgery.

### 4. The 8-step elbow tendinosis protocol (Hörst Ch.13)

> ⚠️ Reproduced here so the coach can speak coherently when users ask — the actual return is governed by the user's physician, not the engine.

1. Cease climbing and climbing-specific training.
2. Ice + brief NSAIDs only if palpable swelling (uncommon in tendinosis); discontinue both as soon as swelling/pain diminishes — further use may slow healing.
3. **Never** use NSAIDs to mask pain to continue climbing. Regular NSAID use (and smoking) may weaken tendons.
4. If no swelling: mild stretching, light massage, heating pad 10–15 min ×3/day. Twice-daily forearm stretches (Hörst Ch.6) are central.
5. Armaid daily on the forearm muscle tissue to improve tissue quality. **Never on tendons or epicondyles directly.**
6. If swelling minor: every-other-day rehab — arm circles, finger flexions, massage, heat. Reverse wrist curls + reverse arm curls for lateral; pronator isolation for medial.
7. Return to climbing only when pain-free and only after **2–4 weeks** of strength-training rehab. Start with easy, foot-oriented climbing; limit crimp. Stop and return to step 2 if pain returns.
8. Commit to long-term forearm pronator + extensor training and daily stretching + Armaid use **for as long as you climb.**

### 5. Knee — brief note

Drop-knee technique under high force load can tear the meniscus (Stelzle 2000) — partial or total tear during forced knee rotation while the foot is fixed. Pain ranges from mild to severe with possible swelling, clicking, locking. Arthroscopic surgery is highly successful; return to climbing typically 2–3 months. **Mechanism the engine recognizes:** users specializing in steep indoor terrain accumulate drop-knee volume rapidly. Variation in foot position and avoiding extreme drop-knee under maximal load is the main prevention lever — though once the move is required for the climb, technique modification has limits.

### 6. The prevention frame — Hörst's 10 Tips, condensed

These are the long-form behaviors the engine encodes structurally:

1. **Technique before strength early on.** Beginners and intermediates: avoid fingerboard/campus the first ~2 years. Tendons strengthen much slower than muscles.
2. **Progressive warm-up always.** 5–10 min aerobic + mobility + easy climbing graduating to limit over 30–60 min.
3. **Antagonist + stabilizer training 2×/week — non-negotiable.** Priority 1: scapular stability + rotator cuff. Priority 2: forearm extensors + pronator. 20–40 min per session.
4. **Daily mobility + foam roll.** 15–30 min/day improves movement quality and reduces injury risk.
5. **Self-awareness on potentially injurious moves.** "Good pain" (muscle pump, acute fatigue) vs "bad pain" (tendon/joint at limit) — never push through the second category.
6. **Avoid passive straight-arm hanging.** See §2 above — five specific situations to avoid.
7. **Don't climb to complete exhaustion.** End-of-day injury risk spikes; leave gas in the tank if climbing the next day.
8. **≤4 climbing/training days per week** total combined indoor and outdoor. Drop to 2–3 at first sign of finger / tendon / joint pain.
9. **Use periodization** to vary intensity/volume and ensure recovery windows.
10. **Sleep + nutrition** are prerequisites, not optional.

The engine encodes these into structural defaults: D71 caps weekly progression at 10%; the catalog includes scapular/rotator-cuff/extensor exercises and the planner allocates them 2×/week as standard antagonist sessions (see `[[12_antagonist_postural]]`); warm-up generation is a planner default (D33); the periodization framework is the entire `[[01_periodization]]` macrocycle.

## How the engine applies this

**D58 — Postural correction exercises (anti-climber's-back).** Every active phase includes scheduled scapular stability, rotator cuff, and antagonist push work. Not optional, not skippable. The prescription density reflects the user's load — harder-training climbers get more, beginners get a baseline. Detailed catalog and prescription logic in `[[12_antagonist_postural]]`.

**D60 — Wrist extension protocol for epicondylitis prevention.** Reverse wrist curls + wrist-extension isometric work appears as a default antagonist component. The empirical basis is the flexor/extensor imbalance + chicken-wing mechanism for lateral epicondylitis (Hörst Ch.13 §5.2).

**D68 — Onboarding injury history as permanent gate.** A user reporting shoulder or elbow injury history triggers reduced load in affected areas, extended warm-up, and specific antagonist emphasis. Re-injury rates run 2–5× first-injury rates (Quarmby 2023). As with finger history, the gate is paced, not absolute — pain-free function for 18+ months reopens the conversation.

**D71 — Weekly volume ≤10%.** Volume spikes are the dominant trigger for both pulley and elbow tendinopathy (Quarmby 2023, Gabbett 2016). The engine refuses week-over-week increases above 10%; ACWR held in 0.8–1.3.

**D41 — Campus board prerequisites + auto-stop.** Campus requires ≥7a redpoint + ≥2 yr systematic + no current finger / elbow / shoulder issue. Auto-stop after 2 missed jumps or any sharp finger sensation. Campus is the highest-impact tool and the dominant driver of the shoulder-injury increase per Hörst — the gate isn't about being strong enough, it's about baseline tissue durability.

**Warm-up prescription (D33) + CUE-02.** Progressive warm-up before every session; **no heavy static stretching of forearm flexors pre-performance** (reduces grip strength up to 60 min). The warm-up itself reduces shoulder injury risk via increased tissue temperature and synovial fluid distribution — the warm-up vs no-warm-up distinction matters at the population level (Fradkin 2010), even where direct SR evidence on injury rates is mixed (Quarmby 2023).

**No diagnostic claims, no return-to-climb dates.** Even with all of the above, if a user describes an active shoulder or elbow injury, the engine routes to medical evaluation. The differential diagnosis between SLAP, impingement, instability, and supraspinatus tendinitis matters for treatment and is not something to guess from a conversational description.

## When user asks…

**"I keep getting a catching feeling in my shoulder when I lock off — should I just rest it?"**

> Catching sensation + lock-off pattern + climbing context = SLAP tear is on the list of things to rule out, and SLAP usually needs surgical repair, not rest. Hörst Ch.13 lists "catching feeling in the shoulder while climbing" as a primary SLAP symptom; the other red flags are aching after hard sessions, shoulder pain when sleeping on the side, and any sense of decreased arm/grip strength. The differential is broad here — could also be impingement, instability, biceps tendinopathy — and the diagnosis affects the treatment significantly. I'd push you toward a climbing-aware sports physician or orthopedic shoulder specialist this week, not next month, because catching feelings on lock-offs tend to escalate. In the meantime: no campus, no dynamic moves, no max bouldering, no overhead pulling beyond pain-free range. I'll pause the harder shoulder-loaded sessions on your plan and we'll rebuild once you have a diagnosis.

**"My medial elbow has been achy for a couple months but I've kept climbing — getting worse though."**

> Two months of progressive medial elbow pain with continued climbing is the textbook tendinosis progression Hörst describes — accumulating microtrauma outpacing repair, and the longer it runs, the longer the rehab. The repair-vs-muscle adaptation rate is roughly 10× slower (Hörst Ch.13), so a 2-month accumulation doesn't unwind in 2 weeks. Honest path forward: stop climbing-specific training, get a physio evaluation to confirm it's medial epicondylitis and rule out other things (cubital tunnel, FDS strain), and start the imbalance-specific rehab — for medial tendinosis the key is pronator strengthening, because the pronator teres is the chronically undertrained muscle whose tendon you've been overloading. The 8-step Hörst protocol is the general shape, but Phase II loading specifics should be your physio's call, not mine. While you're out, the engine will give you lower-body and core work that doesn't load the elbow.

**"I want to start campus board — am I ready?"**

> The hard gate I work with (D41): ≥7a redpoint, ≥2 years of systematic training, and no current finger / elbow / shoulder issue. All three are required, not most-of-three. The gate isn't about whether you're "strong enough" — it's about whether your tissue has the baseline durability to handle campus loading without injury, and campus is the highest-impact tool we have. The shoulder-injury rate in climbing has jumped from 5% to 17% in a decade and Hörst attributes it primarily to "proliferation and inappropriate use of campus training" (Ch.13). Even when the gate opens: start with hand-over-hand laddering on big rungs, not double-dynos; auto-stop on any sharp finger sensation or 2 missed catches; build slowly into max work. If you're not at 7a + 2 years yet, hangboard work buys most of the same finger gains at a fraction of the risk.

**"Why does antagonist training matter so much? It's the boring part."**

> Hörst's Ch.6 framing is the strongest claim in the book: stabilizer + antagonist training may be the single most important section, and the consequence of neglecting it shows up not in the next workout but 6–12 months later as elbow tendinosis, impingement, or worse. The mechanism is concrete: years of pulling-only training produce tight pecs + tight biceps + weak rotator cuff + weak lower trapezius + flexor/extensor imbalance — Hörst's "climber's posture," and it's the same imbalance that produces impingement *and* medial epicondylitis *and* lateral epicondylitis *and* predisposes to SLAP under dynamic load. One imbalance, cluster of injuries. The prescription is small — 2×/week, 20–40 min — and it makes the difference between climbing into your 50s and 60s or retiring at 38 with a chronic elbow. The Quarmby 2023 systematic review is direct: strength training is the only intervention with consistent evidence of reducing shoulder and elbow injuries in climbers. It's not optional infrastructure.

**"Are NSAIDs okay for my elbow?"**

> Same answer as for finger injuries (see `[[10_injuries_fingers]]`): brief courses under physician guidance for acute swelling are fine; chronic use to mask pain so you can keep climbing is counterproductive and may actively slow tendon healing (Almekinders 1999, 2003). For mature tendinosis specifically — which is what most climber's elbow turns out to be — the injury isn't primarily inflammatory (Cook 2017), so ice and NSAIDs past the first few days don't accomplish much. The actual intervention is the imbalance-specific eccentric strengthening, not the anti-inflammatory.
