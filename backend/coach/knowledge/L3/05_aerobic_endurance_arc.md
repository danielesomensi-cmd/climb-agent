## Quick reference

Aerobic endurance — the forearm's capacity to sustain submaximal contractions and recover between hard moves — is the **largest single driver of long-route performance** and the unsung hero of bouldering recovery between attempts. The engine builds it through **ARC** (Aerobic Restoration and Capillarization training): 20-45 min continuous easy climbing at **<25% MVC** (D45, hard cap) for a minimum of **6 weeks** (D44, hard floor — capillary + mitochondrial biogenesis is time-locked, Mujika 2012). The coach explains *why* ARC must feel easy (above 25% MVC the stimulus converts to glycolytic) and *why* duration matters more than intensity for this adaptation. **Critical Force as a sustainable-intensity boundary is acknowledged but not prescribed in v1** — Baláš 2024 validates the 4-min all-out test, CAMP4 2025 raises reliability caveats.

## Core findings

### 1. What aerobic endurance does in climbing

The forearm's aerobic system has two jobs:
1. **Sustained low-intensity work** — long routes, multi-pitch, sustained gym lead routes. Direct aerobic ATP supply at submaximal contraction levels.
2. **Recovery between hard moves** — the brief rest between crux moves is where the aerobic system resynthesizes PCr (alactic system fuel) and clears metabolites. Elite climbers **re-oxygenate ~83% faster** in their forearms than intermediate climbers (Fryer 2015a; cited in Consuegra Ch.7).

The second job matters even for boulderers. Limit-bouldering rest between attempts is aerobic-driven; the climber whose forearm clears H+ and Pi faster gets more high-quality attempts per session.

**Maciejczyk 2021** (cited in `04_power_endurance.md`): intermittent climbing tests are 60% aerobic-driven by energy-system contribution. Aerobic capacity is the platform under everything else.

### 2. ARC — Aerobic Restoration and Capillarization

The protocol is the most rigorously specified in the climbing literature (consensus across Hörst, Bechtel, López, Lattice, Climbing Doctor):

| Parameter | Specification | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Duration | 20-45 min continuous climbing | Hörst, Bechtel, Climbing Doctor |
| Intensity | **<25% MVC (D45, hard cap)** | López-Rivera; Barnes, Byström, Sjøgaard |
| Pump scale | ≤1-2 / 10 (light, not deep) | All sources |
| Grade | 3-4 number grades below onsight max | Hörst podcast #26 |
| Self-test | Pass talk test, can nose-breathe | Universal |
| Frequency | 2-4×/week typical (up to 6× because low fatigue) | Bechtel: "3× is plenty, could do 6×" |
| Progression | **Increase duration** (20→30→40→45 min), not intensity | All sources |
| Minimum cycle | **≥6 weeks (D44, hard floor)** | Mujika 2012 mitochondrial biogenesis |
| Modality | Continuous laps, traverse, auto-belay, treadwall | Various |
| Phase | Primary in Base; maintenance in others | Hörst 4-3-2-1 |

The **<25% MVC ceiling (D45, L0)** is non-negotiable. Above 25% MVC, forearm vascular occlusion begins (full occlusion at >50% MVC, Hörst T4C); the stimulus converts to a glycolytic adaptation that doesn't build capillaries. The coach should never relent on this cap, even when the user reports the work "feels too easy". *That's the point.*

The **6-week minimum floor (D44, hard)** comes from Mujika 2012's mitochondrial biogenesis timeline. Capillaries and mitochondria adapt over weeks of repeated sub-occlusive flow — there's no shortcut by going harder. This is *the* most common user pushback the coach handles (see "When user asks…" below).

### 3. The flash-pump trap

Going from cold to high intensity creates a "flash pump" — rapid intracellular acidosis before the aerobic system primes up. Once flashed, the session is largely lost: Hörst (podcast #26) notes that intracellular acidosis impairs mitochondrial function for hours, degrading same-day climbing performance.

Prevention is universal: 15-20 min progressive warm-up that primes all three energy systems before any high-intensity work. Coach surfaces this in any context where the user is jumping into hard work without warm-up — see `09_recovery_sleep.md` for warm-up protocol and `06_technique_movement.md` for silent-feet warm-up integration.

### 4. Capillarization and mitochondrial density

The two cellular adaptations ARC produces:

- **Capillarization (angiogenesis):** new capillary formation around forearm muscle fibers. More capillaries = faster O2 delivery and metabolite removal between contractions. Elite climbers' forearm capillary density is measurably higher (NIRS studies — Fryer, Baláš).
- **Mitochondrial density:** more mitochondria per fiber → more aerobic ATP at any given intensity → faster PCr resynthesis during rests between moves → more high-quality attempts in a session.

Both adaptations require **continuous sub-occlusive blood flow** for the stimulus. That's why ARC must be light. Higher-intensity intervals build mitochondrial *quality* (efficiency per mitochondrion) but ARC builds mitochondrial *volume* — both are needed. ARC is the foundation; PE work (`04_power_endurance.md`) sharpens it.

### 5. Re-oxygenation as performance metric

MacLeod 2007 (*J Sports Sci*): the rate at which the forearm re-oxygenates during rest phases predicts climbing endurance performance. Elite climbers don't necessarily desaturate less under load — they re-saturate faster between loads. This is the directly trainable mechanism ARC targets.

**G-Tox technique (D17 + Hörst):** during rests on a route, alternate arm position above and below heart, 5-10 s each, for 2 min. Gravity assists venous drainage from the working forearm. Hörst measured **+18.4% grip-strength recovery** vs hands-down resting. This is the cheapest intervention in climbing — zero training cost, ~15-20% measurable recovery improvement.

The coach surfaces G-Tox in any context where rest behavior matters: route projecting, route endurance sessions, PE attempts between sets, deep rests on a long lead.

### 6. Critical Force — acknowledged, not prescribed

The Critical Force (CF) concept is the climbing-specific analog of Critical Power: the highest sustainable intensity that the aerobic system can fully cover. Above CF → fatigue accumulates, eventual failure in finite time. Below CF → theoretically sustainable indefinitely.

**Baláš 2024** (*Eur J Appl Physiol* 124:2787) validated a 4-min all-out test on finger flexors for CF determination. NIRS data confirmed steady-state oxygenation at sub-CF intensities. CF measured at 20.1 ± 5.7 kg in their sample; end-force at 16.8 ± 5.2 kg. Time-to-failure at CF intensity averaged 440 ± 140 s — not infinitely sustainable as theory predicts.

**CAMP4 2025 reanalysis caveats:** CFmin (the lower-bound CF estimate) and CF720 (CF at 720 s extrapolation) differ; the 4-min all-out test tends to *overestimate* the true sustainable boundary; recommends combining with RPE verification and multiple-trial validation before prescribing training zones from a single test.

**Engine v1 stance:** the CF test is **not** in the standard battery. Reasons: (1) reliability questions from CAMP4 not yet resolved; (2) requires a Tindeq or Climbro force sensor most users don't have; (3) the engine's PE and endurance prescriptions don't depend on CF zones — they're phase-driven and EL-driven.

When the user asks about CF specifically: describe the concept and the current evidence honestly. If they've run their own CF test, the coach can *interpret directionally* ("your CF/BW ratio is in the advanced range; that aligns with strong route-endurance capacity") but never prescribe specific training intensity zones from a single CF value.

### 7. ARC alternatives when no climbing wall

Lead climbers asking "can I substitute ARC on a stationary bike + finger curls?" — answer honestly. ARC mechanism is **local** (forearm capillarization + mitochondrial density specific to the working musculature). General aerobic cycling builds *cardiac* aerobic capacity, which is largely irrelevant to climbing performance. Finger curls help marginally but lack the position specificity.

Practical fallbacks (see `18_equipment_fallback.md`):
- Easy traversing at any climbable surface (most effective substitute).
- Lifting edge with very light load + 7s on / 3s off pattern, ≥20 min (Lattice 2024-2025 — alternative protocol).
- Boulder pyramid below limit grade (3-4 grades down), high volume, low intensity (boulder-specific fallback when no rope wall).

Cycling + finger curls is a partial substitute, not an equivalent. Frame as: "you'll hold capillary base, not gain meaningfully — fine for travel weeks, not for the dedicated Base phase."

## How the engine applies this

- **D45 hard cap (L0):** ARC sessions are bounded at <25% MVC and pump scale ≤1-2. The closed-loop layer flags ARC sessions where user-reported pump exceeds 2 — that's a "too hard" signal, not a "too easy" complaint.
- **D44 hard floor (L0 §D71):** the Base phase is ≥6 weeks. Engine cannot generate a macrocycle with Base <6 weeks regardless of user request.
- **D15 progressive ARC:** the catalog includes progressive ARC duration prescriptions (week 1: 2×20 min, week 2: 2×25 min, … through week 6 at 2×40-45 min). Users early in Base see shorter durations; users late in Base see the full progression.
- **D17 G-Tox cue:** surfaces in route_endurance, route_projecting, and any session with explicit rest-on-route prompts.
- **D26 energy systems model:** the engine treats intermittent climbing as aerobic-dominant (60% per Maciejczyk 2021), which is why ARC is maintained at ~10-20% volume across non-Base phases instead of dropped entirely.
- **CF test deferred:** the engine does not prescribe training intensity zones from a CF test. Endurance axis uses self-reported route grade + repeater test as proxy until v2.

## When user asks…

**"My ARC feels too easy. Should I make it harder?"**

No (D45, hard cap). If it feels easy, it's working. The capillary and mitochondrial adaptations need ~6 weeks of continuous sub-occlusive flow. Pushing intensity converts the stimulus to a glycolytic adaptation that doesn't build the capacity you need. Results show up at week 4-6 in measurable route endurance, not at week 1 in subjective fatigue. Trust the protocol.

**"Why is my Base phase 6 weeks long? Can I skip ahead?"**

Cite D44 + Mujika 2012: mitochondrial biogenesis runs on a 6+ week clock that's time-locked, not effort-locked. Pushing harder doesn't shorten the timeline. Skipping Base costs the Build and Performance phases a smaller aerobic engine — diminished returns later, not now. The engine won't generate a macrocycle with Base <6 weeks. Don't relent.

**"My pulse is high during ARC, even though intensity is low. What's wrong?"**

Probably nothing. ARC arousal at the cardiovascular level is normal — the work is sustained and the brain is engaged. The forearm-specific stimulus is what matters; cardiac HR is a secondary signal. If you're sweating, breathing through the nose feels strained, and you can't talk in full sentences: that's a "too hard" signal — drop a grade or two. Otherwise, mild elevation is fine.

**"Can I substitute ARC with a stationary bike?"**

Partial substitute only. ARC mechanism is local (forearm capillarization + mitochondrial density). Cycling builds cardiac aerobic capacity, which is largely irrelevant to climbing performance. If you have *no* wall access (travel, illness with no gym), cycling + finger curls preserves a fraction of the capillary base — frame as maintenance, not progression. Get back to wall-based ARC as soon as practical.

**"Should the engine measure my Critical Force?"**

Not in v1. The CF concept is valid (Baláš 2024 validates the 4-min all-out test) but CAMP4 2025 raised reliability caveats: CFmin vs CF720 differ; single-trial CF tends to overestimate the true boundary; multiple-trial verification needed before prescribing training zones from a CF value. The engine's PE and endurance prescriptions don't depend on CF — they're phase-driven and EL-driven. If you've run your own CF test, the coach can interpret directionally but won't prescribe training intensity zones from it.

**"Why does ARC matter for bouldering? I never climb routes."**

The second job of the aerobic system: recovery between hard attempts. Limit-bouldering rest between attempts is aerobic-driven — your forearm clears H+ and Pi during the rest, and you get more high-quality attempts per session. Boulderers with better aerobic capacity get more grade-quality attempts per session, recover better between training days, and break plateaus that look like "strength plateaus" but are actually recovery-quality plateaus. Even pure boulderers get ~10-20% ARC maintenance across non-Base phases.

**"Why duration progression instead of intensity progression?"**

Mechanism. Capillary angiogenesis and mitochondrial biogenesis are both **continuous flow** adaptations — the longer the muscle stays in the stimulus window, the more adaptation per session. Above 25% MVC the flow stops and the adaptation flips to glycolytic. So progression is "more time at the right intensity", not "the same time at higher intensity".
