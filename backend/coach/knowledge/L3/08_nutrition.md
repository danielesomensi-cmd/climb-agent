## Quick reference

The coach's role on nutrition is **educational, never prescriptive**. The engine never recommends weight loss, body-composition targets, calorie targets, or specific diets — these are out-of-scope and unsafe coming from a training app (D64, hard rule, see [[L0_safety_hard_rules]]). What is in scope: explaining the rationale for fueling training adequately, the climbing-relevant macro framework (Hörst's 65:15:20 baseline), hydration impact on performance, and evidence-graded supplement context. The dominant risk in the climbing population is *under-fueling* (RED-S, low energy availability), not overfueling.

## Core findings

### 1. The two non-negotiables (L0 + framing)

Two hard frames the coach holds at all times:

**No weight-loss guidance, no body-composition commentary (D64, see [[L0_safety_hard_rules]]).** Joubert 2020 (498 international sport lead climbers) and Joubert 2022 (15.8% amenorrhoea prevalence in elite female climbers, frequently at relatively normal BMI) document widespread disordered-eating risk in the climbing population. The "lighter the better" culture compounds it. A training app giving weight advice is outside safe scope. If the user raises it, the coach pivots to fueling for performance and — if persistent or accompanied by RED-S signals — refers to a sports dietitian. See [[L1_coach_voice]] §4 for the protocol verbatim.

**Food first, but not always food only (Close et al. 2022).** The IOC consensus framework (Maughan 2018) plus Close: prefer whole foods; supplements are an additive tool with evidence-graded relevance, not a replacement for nutrition. The coach's supplement discussion is always grounded in this.

### 2. The Hörst baseline — 65:15:20 carb:protein:fat

Hörst Ch.11's working ratio for climbers. Two flavors by discipline:

| Climbing style | Carb : Protein : Fat | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Bouldering / cragging** (high-intensity, stop-and-go) | 65 : 15 : 20 | Anaerobic demands, lower total calorie count |
| **Alpine / long-route** (long, slow distance) | 55 : 15 : 30 | Higher total calorie demand; fat as slow-burn fuel |

The bouldering/cragging profile is the default for most climb-agent users. The alpine profile applies only when goals include multi-day or sustained-aerobic objectives.

**Protein:** Hörst's range is 1.2-1.5 g/kg/day for climbers, slightly below the ISSN's 1.4-2.0 g/kg/day for athletes generally (Smith 2017, Ranchordas 2019). The 1.2-1.5 range respects climbers' strength-to-weight reality without overshooting protein at the cost of carbohydrate. Hörst is explicit that successful training is much less a factor of consuming a lot of protein than of using the appropriate training strategy plus enough carbs to fuel that training.

**Fat:** 15-30% of calories depending on discipline. Minimum 15-25 g/day for essential fatty acid needs. Female athletes specifically: dietary fat deficiency has been linked to amenorrhoea (Hörst Ch.11).

**Carbohydrate:** roughly two-thirds of daily calories. **Low-carb diets (Atkins, Zone, Ketogenic) are inappropriate for most active climbers** — climbing's energy demands are mixed anaerobic alactic + glycolytic, both of which depend on glycogen. Inadequate carb intake forces muscle-protein breakdown for energy (the protein-sparing argument).

### 3. Glycemic Index strategy

Hörst Ch.11 makes GI strategic, not absolute:

**During the day / during a session:** favor **low- to medium-GI** foods (vegetables, whole grains, brown rice, milk, beans, lentils, balanced bars). Steady insulin → steady energy → no spike-and-crash. Critical for stop-and-go activities like climbing.

**Immediately post-exercise (0-2 hours):** switch to **high-GI** foods/drinks. Intense exercise primes muscles to reload glycogen rapidly; high-GI maximizes this window. The 0-30 minute post-exercise window is the highest-yield slot (see §6 below).

**Counter-intuitive GI examples climbers commonly mis-classify:** white rice, bagels, potatoes, cornflakes, sports drinks, jelly beans are all high-GI. Apples, peanuts, lentils, skim milk, whole wheat, balanced bars are low-GI. Sweet potato is medium. Granola bars are typically medium, not low.

### 4. The under-fueling problem

Climbing-specific data (Regulska-Ilow 2023, 106 sport climbers): low energy availability is documented across both genders and all levels, with significant differences across male climbing levels (p<0.001). Gibson-Smith 2024 (50 senior competition climbers): mean nutrition knowledge rated only "average" (53.5 ± 11.1%), with intentional 3-8% bodyweight cuts common over 2+ weeks.

**RED-S (Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport)** is the systemic consequence:
- Impaired metabolic rate
- Hormonal dysfunction (amenorrhoea in women, low testosterone in men)
- Decreased bone mineral density
- Weakened immune system
- Increased injury susceptibility
- Impaired psychological function

**Coach surface signals to escalate (D64 pushback in [[L0_safety_hard_rules]]):** chronic fatigue + declining performance despite adequate training, missed periods, frequent illness, mood/sleep decline, repeated finger/tendon injuries with no obvious mechanical cause. The coach doesn't diagnose RED-S — flags the pattern and refers.

### 5. Hydration — underused, high-impact

Hörst Ch.11: water may be the most important nutrient to get right; many climbers are chronically dehydrated.

**Performance impact** (Schoffstall 2001 cited in Hörst):
- 1-2% BW drop in water → loss of concentration, enhanced fatigue
- 1.5% BW drop → statistically significant drop in *maximum strength*
- 3% BW drop → headaches, cramping, dizziness

For a 70 kg climber, 1.5% is ~1 L. Easy to lose in a 2-hour gym session without proactive hydration. Hörst also notes dehydration as an injury risk factor: proper hydration facilitates nutrient transport, protects tissues, maintains joint lubrication. **Maintaining proper hydration is as important as a proper warm-up** for injury prevention.

**Practical protocol:**
1. Pre-hydrate ("camel up"): ~500 ml in the hours before session
2. During: minimum 240 ml every 1-2 hours
3. Normal conditions: 1-2 quarts over 8 hours minimum
4. Hot/humid: 3-4 quarts over 8 hours

Most climbers underhydrate. This is one of the highest-ROI nudges the coach can surface.

### 6. The post-exercise refueling window

Strongest evidence in the chapter. Burke 1999: waiting 2 hours after exercise to consume carbohydrates **reduces glycogen replenishment by 50%** compared with eating immediately. Delayed refueling slows recovery and may delay complete recovery by up to a full day.

**The 30-minute window (most critical):**
- High-GI foods/drinks substantially increase glycogen replacement rate (Richter 1984)
- Glycogen resynthesis ~40% faster with protein + carb together (Niles 1997)
- Optimal ratio: **4:1 carbohydrate to protein** (Burke 1999)
- For a 72 kg climber: ~100 g carb + ~25 g protein
- **Chocolate skim milk is an excellent recovery drink** — ~50 g carb + ~17 g high-quality protein per pint

**At 2 hours post-exercise:** full meal at ~65:15:20 macronutrient ratio. Low- to medium-GI foods now (the high-GI window has closed).

**Before sleep:** small carb+protein snack supports overnight glycogen resynthesis and tissue remodeling. Skim milk specifically is well-suited (low-GI carb, high-quality protein, tryptophan precursor for serotonin → supports sleep onset).

**Glycogen replenishment timeline:** complete restock takes **24 hours**. A 12-hour overnight break gives ~80% replenishment (assuming immediate post-exercise refueling). On back-to-back climbing days, expect to hit the wall sooner on day 2 — refuel aggressively throughout day 1. Carbohydrate intake during exercise extends glycogen supply 25-50% (Coyle 1984).

Cross-reference: `09_recovery_sleep.md` §2 (the Refuel medium-term recovery period).

### 7. Supplement evidence grading

Following IOC consensus + Close 2022. Climbing-specific research is sparse — most evidence extrapolates from general sports science.

**Strongest climbing-relevant evidence: vitamin C + hydrolysed collagen** (D67).
- Shaw 2017 (n=8): 15 g vitamin C-enriched gelatin 60 min before exercise → +20% procollagen markers. Small sample, individual variability, placebo was sugar (not protein) → promising but not definitive.
- Lattice Training 2023 review: reasonable evidence collagen supports connective tissue healing, joint pain, and functionality.
- Vitamin C scoping review 2022: combined products (collagen + vitamin C + mucopolysaccharides) showed substantial pain reduction in Achilles tendinopathy (69%), tennis elbow (83%), supraspinatus tendinopathy (75%) with structural improvements.
- Hooper's Beta 2022 critique: collagen companies over-cite Shaw 2017; reanalysis reached more cautious conclusions. Promising, not definitive.

**Suggested protocol (educational only):** 10-15 g hydrolysed collagen + vitamin C, 30-60 min before targeted loading. The coach mentions this when asked about tendon health or supplements — never proactively as a prescription.

**Moderate evidence — beta-alanine.** 4-6 g/day for 2-4+ weeks raises muscle carnosine (intracellular pH buffer). Most effective for 1-4 min exercise efforts → theoretically relevant to boulder problems and crux sequences. Side effect: paresthesia (tingling), mitigated by divided doses. No climbing-specific RCT exists. Mechanism-plausible; not a v1 prescription.

**Caffeine.** Well-established general ergogenic aid. Hörst's climber-specific framework:
- Optimal range: 3-9 mg/kg (200-600 mg for typical climber)
- Strategic periodization for performance days: maintain moderate daily intake → *decrease* in days before performance → *return to normal moderate dose* on performance day. Superior to "doubling up" on performance day (which risks jitters, GI distress, palpitations).
- Caveats: jittery on delicate face climbs requiring fine motor control; afternoon/evening consumption affects sleep (4-6 hr half-life); high doses lower blood pH which can hurt glycolytic performance.

**Weak/conflicting for climbing — creatine.** The most-researched sports supplement generally, but climber-specific picture is complicated:
- Loading protocol (10-20 g/day for 5-6 days) causes 1-3 kg water retention → worsens strength-to-weight ratio, especially in leg muscles
- Cell volumizing may accelerate intramuscular blood-flow occlusion under contraction → faster pump, earlier failure on longer routes
- Hörst's climber-specific alternative: **small dose 2-5 g/day** sipped through workout day. Modest power and recovery benefits without weight gain or pump issues.
- The coach explains the trade-off honestly when asked; never proactively recommends.

**BCAAs, ZMA, vitamin D, magnesium** — only beneficial if deficient (test before supplementing). BCAAs largely unnecessary if total protein intake is adequate (ISSN: prefer whole protein sources). Multivitamin is a reasonable baseline; Mg + Zn (ZMA, Brilla 1998) showed strength benefits in some studies. Vitamin D supplementation only if blood test confirms deficiency.

**Most other supplements** — testosterone boosters, nitric oxide boosters (most), the catalog of "performance enhancer" snake oil. Hörst's blunt summary: outside caffeine and small-dose creatine, anything that truly aids performance significantly is either illegal or on the WADA prohibition list.

### 8. The 3-month rule for sustained protocols

Most nutrition changes — protein timing, hydration habits, GI awareness, supplement protocols — take 4-12 weeks of consistency to show effects. Hörst is explicit: most climbers can realize 10-20% improvement in training, recovery, concentration, energy, and overall climbing performance with sound dietary practices.

The coach frames nutrition changes as 8-12 week experiments, not quick fixes. This protects against the user abandoning a sound protocol after 2 weeks of "no difference."

### 9. Periodized nutrition (alignment to training phases)

Stellingwerff 2019 proposes a framework for periodized nutrition aligned to training phases:
- **Base / ARC** (high-volume endurance): higher carbohydrate emphasis
- **Build / strength phases**: protein adequacy critical (timing around sessions)
- **Performance / competition**: glycogen-loading strategies, pre-climb meal planning, between-attempt fueling

The engine does not prescribe phase-specific calorie targets. The coach can describe the principle when asked — more volume = more fuel needed; phase of training affects what the body uses most.

## How the engine applies this

- **No weight or body-composition advice ever (D64, hard rule).** See [[L0_safety_hard_rules]]. The engine uses body weight ONLY as input for ratio calculations (finger strength %BW, pull-up %BW). It never displays a "target weight," "ideal body fat," or similar.
- **RED-S pattern flagging (D64):** the engine watches for chronic fatigue + declining performance + (when surveyed) missed periods / frequent illness. When flagged, the coach refers to sports dietitian — does not diagnose.
- **Educational fueling messaging (D66):** during onboarding and phase transitions, the engine surfaces a "fuel your training" educational note — more volume = more fuel — without specific caloric prescriptions.
- **Sleep + recovery integration:** post-session feedback and rest-day guidance include the 4:1 carb:protein post-exercise window cue and the 30-min window emphasis (cross-references `09_recovery_sleep.md` §2).
- **Collagen + vitamin C mention (D67):** in injury-prevention or tendon-health contexts, the coach can describe the evidence-graded protocol (10-15 g hydrolysed collagen + vitamin C, 30-60 min before loading) — never as prescription; always with the "evidence is promising not definitive" caveat.
- **Cardio is NOT a weight-management tool:** the engine never recommends cardio for body composition. Consuegra Ch.8 documents that cardio is inefficient for fat loss (max ~111 g/hour at extreme intensity). The coach mentions this when relevant to head off a common user misconception.

## When user asks…

**"How much protein should I eat?"**

Hörst's range for climbers: 1.2-1.5 g/kg body weight per day. For a 70 kg climber that's 84-105 g/day. Slightly below the general athlete recommendation (1.4-2.0 g/kg) because climbing isn't a hypertrophy-driven sport — adequate protein matters more than maximum protein, and extra protein at the cost of carbohydrate hurts training capacity. Timing: ~25 g protein within the post-exercise window (combined with carbs in a 4:1 carb:protein ratio for fastest glycogen restoration). Whey protein has the highest biological value, but skim milk, chicken, fish, eggs, dairy, and legume-grain combinations all work. If you eat little meat or dairy, a daily protein shake is a low-friction addition.

**"Should I cut weight to climb harder?"**

That's outside what I'm here to help with — see the safety rule. What I *can* help with: making sure you're fueling well enough to actually train and adapt. Climbing has a documented under-fueling problem (Regulska-Ilow 2023, low energy availability across all levels), and chronic under-fueling produces the opposite of what the user usually wants — declining performance, hormonal dysfunction (amenorrhoea in women, low testosterone in men), brittle tendons, depressed mood. Strength-to-weight ratio has two terms; making the bottom smaller while making the top weaker is a losing trade. If body composition specifically is the concern, a sports dietitian who works with climbers is the right resource.

**"What should I eat after climbing?"**

The 30-minute window matters most. ~100 g of high-GI carbohydrate + ~25 g protein in a 4:1 ratio (Burke 1999). Chocolate skim milk is the cheap-and-effective option — one pint roughly hits those numbers. Or a sports drink + protein shake. Or a banana + whey. Waiting 2 hours instead of eating immediately cuts glycogen replenishment by ~50% — delayed refueling is the most common avoidable training mistake. Then 2 hours later: full meal at ~65:15:20 (carbs:protein:fat), with low- to medium-GI foods this time (vegetables, whole grains, lean protein). Before sleep: small carb+protein snack (skim milk works) supports overnight rebuilding.

**"Are supplements worth it?"**

Mostly no. The two with credible climbing-relevant evidence are collagen + vitamin C (10-15 g hydrolysed collagen + vitamin C, 30-60 min before loading; promising for tendon health, evidence not yet definitive) and small-dose creatine (2-5 g/day sipped through training day; modest power and recovery benefits without the weight gain of full loading). Caffeine is well-established generally; if you use it, the climber-specific strategy is to maintain moderate intake, decrease in the days before a performance day, return to normal on performance day — better than doubling up. BCAAs are unnecessary if you hit total protein from food. A daily multivitamin is cheap insurance. Everything else marketed as "performance enhancer" is mostly snake oil — Hörst's blunt summary, and the IOC consensus agrees. Food first.

**"I feel weak and tired all the time even when I'm sleeping enough. What's going on?"**

Several possibilities, and ruling them out matters more than guessing. Three to consider: (1) under-fueling — chronic low energy availability is documented across climbing populations and produces exactly this pattern. Hörst's calorie-needs guidance shows that an active day for a 72 kg climber is ~3,100 calories; many climbers eat well below that without realizing. Track for a week. (2) Hydration — a 1.5% body-weight water deficit cuts maximum strength measurably. Pre-hydrate, drink through training. (3) RED-S — if there's chronic fatigue + declining performance + (in women) menstrual irregularity or (in men) low motivation/libido + frequent illness, the pattern is consistent with relative energy deficiency in sport. I can't diagnose it, but those are the signals to bring to a sports dietitian or sports physician — RED-S is treatable but it needs proper assessment, not guessed at from an app.
