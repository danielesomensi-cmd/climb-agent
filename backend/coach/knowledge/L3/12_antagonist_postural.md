## Quick reference

Hörst's pyramid: **Mobility & Stability → Strength → Power.** Build the base before the apex. Antagonist + stabilizer training "may be the single most important section in the entire book" (Hörst Ch.6) — and it's the section climbers most reliably skip. The engine treats antagonist work as non-optional structural content (D58, D60), prescribes it **2×/week** as Hörst recommends (mandatory, not opt-in), and combines it with **15–30 min/day of foam rolling + mobility** (rest-day or pre-bed). The mechanism: years of pulling-only training produce predictable imbalances (tight pecs + tight biceps + weak lower trap + weak rotator cuff + weak forearm extensors) that drive most climber shoulder and elbow injuries. The fix is small in time investment, large in injury-prevention return, and the only intervention with consistent SR evidence of preventing climber shoulder and elbow injuries (Quarmby 2023).

## Core findings

### 1. The pyramid — why ordering matters

Hörst Ch.6 (Figure 6.1) frames training in three tiers:

- **Base:** Mobility & Stability — must be built first.
- **Middle:** Strength.
- **Apex:** Power.

The mantra: *"To maximize potential and reduce injury risk, develop stability before strength, and strength before power."*

Why the order matters mechanically: power is generated by the prime movers but transmitted through joints stabilized by smaller, often neglected muscles. When the stabilizer is weak, the prime mover's force output is **internally rate-limited** — the central governor de-rates output to protect the unstable joint. Hörst Ch.6 calls this out specifically: "weak rotator cuff muscles are a common, yet unrecognized, limiting constraint in your maximum pulling strength, lunging power, grip strength, and contact strength. If you're a hard-training climber frustrated by lack of strength/power gains on the rock, it may be that your central governor is limiting power output due to afferent feedback from your weak, unstable shoulder joints."

The pyramid is the design principle behind the planner's phase ordering (`[[01_periodization]]`) and behind making antagonist + mobility content structural rather than optional.

### 2. The two imbalances climbing reliably produces

**Forearm imbalance.** The finger flexors get hours of training every week (every climb is finger flexor work). The finger extensors and the pronator teres get essentially nothing unless trained deliberately. The result: a chronically strong flexor pulling against a chronically weak extensor, and a biceps (supinator) pulling against an undertrained pronator. The clinical expressions are medial epicondylitis (pronator/flexor attachment), lateral epicondylitis (extensor attachment), and the chicken-wing chronic loading pattern that drives both (see `[[11_injuries_shoulder_elbow]]` §3).

**Shoulder + upper-back imbalance ("climber's posture").** Hörst Ch.6 Figure 6.2 names it directly: tight pectorals + weak lower trapezius + tight biceps + weak rotator cuff (external rotators especially) + tight upper trap. Visual signature: rounded shoulders, upper-back kyphosis ("climber's back," Förster 2009). Mechanical consequence: the humeral head elevates, the scapula fails to depress/rotate properly for overhead movement, and every overhead reach loads soft tissue (subacromial bursa, supraspinatus tendon) it shouldn't be loading. This is the proximate mechanism for impingement, SLAP-tear risk under dynamic load, and shoulder instability.

**The corrective principle (D59 hypertonic / inhibited framework):**

| Hypertonic (release + stretch) | Inhibited (strengthen) |
|---|---|
| Pectorals | Lower trapezius |
| Upper trapezius | Middle trapezius / rhomboids |
| Biceps | Triceps (deltoid posterior) |
| Forearm flexors | Forearm extensors / pronator teres |
| Lats (often) | Rotator cuff (external rotators especially) |

The hypertonic side gets foam rolling + targeted release + light stretching (between sessions, not pre-performance — see CUE-02). The inhibited side gets targeted strengthening **2×/week**. The asymmetric prescription is the point: stretching the hypertonic side without strengthening the inhibited side leaves the original imbalance intact and may even worsen it by creating more range for the weak side to fail through.

### 3. The exercise families (catalog-level, not prescription-level)

Hörst Ch.6 organizes the 38 catalogued exercises into seven families:

1. **Foam rolling / SMR** (8 exercises) — upper back + shoulder, lower back + glutes, hip flexors + IT band, pronator pin-and-stretch, wrist extensors pin-and-stretch, general forearm, elbow voodoo floss, pectoral release with ball.
2. **Upper-body stretches** (9 exercises) — arm circles, finger curls, finger isolation, finger flexor, finger extensor, posterior shoulder, rotator cuff towel stretch, anterior shoulder/chest/biceps, lat/triceps.
3. **Lower-body stretches** (9 exercises) — buttocks/lower back, hamstrings, adductor, groin, piriformis, hip flexors/quads, calf, abdominal, obliques/hips/back.
4. **Wrist stabilizer antagonist** (7 exercises) — rubber-band finger extension (warm-up), reverse wrist curls, wrist extension isometric, wide pinch with wrist extension, pronator isolation (sledgehammer), pronator isolation with band, reverse arm curls.
5. **Rotator cuff isolation** (2 exercises) — dumbbell internal rotation (subscapularis), dumbbell external rotation (infraspinatus + teres minor).
6. **Scapular stabilizers** (4 exercises) — T exercise (mid trap + rhomboids), Y exercise (lower trap), scapular push-up (serratus anterior), scapular pull-up (lower trap + lats).
7. **Antagonist push** (3 exercises) — shoulder press, bench press / push-ups, dips.

Hörst flags two as especially high-value and especially neglected:

- **Scapular pull-up** — "the best climbing exercise nobody does" (Ch.6). Develops scapular depression + retraction + the kinesthetic awareness to hold scapular position during pulling under fatigue. Direct injury-prevention return.
- **Shoulder press** — "the most central antagonist exercise" because the shoulder-press motion is almost exactly the opposite of climbing pulls. Trains the deltoids, triceps, and additional rotator-cuff + scapular stabilizer work.

Exercise-by-exercise prescription detail (sets × reps × resistance × tempo, plus alternatives and progressions) lives in the engine's exercise catalog. The principle the user needs from this file is which families matter and why — not which dumbbell to grab on which day.

### 4. The prescription pattern

**Antagonist + stabilizer strength: 2×/week, mandatory.**

Hörst's framing is absolute: "Every climber should train their stabilizers and antagonists twice per week. Furthermore, the harder a person trains and climbs, the more indispensable these exercises become."

The structure of a typical antagonist session (Hörst Ch.6 §7):

- **1 light warm-up set** of each wrist stabilizer exercise (EX-ANT-01 through EX-ANT-03).
- **2 strength sets** later in the workout — first set ~20–25 reps to near-failure, second set heavier ~10–15 reps.
- Rotator cuff isolation (internal + external rotation), 2 sets each, ~20–25 reps, 3-min rest between sets.
- Scapular stabilizer work (T, Y, scapular push-up, scapular pull-up), 1–2 sets of 10–20 reps each.
- 2 antagonist push exercises (shoulder press, bench press / push-ups, or dips), 2 sets of 15–20 reps.

**Time investment: 20–40 min per session, 2 sessions per week = 40–80 min/week.**

A "moderate resistance, high rep" prescription is deliberate. Hörst Ch.6: *"perform a high-weight hypertrophy or maximum-strength program is unnecessary and, in fact, could be detrimental if it causes unwanted gain in muscle mass."* Climber-relevant strength gain is neural and tissue-quality, not hypertrophic — the strength-to-weight ratio matters and added body mass without proportionate climbing-specific strength is net-negative.

Weight ceilings Hörst gives (worth knowing):

- Shoulder press: don't progress beyond ~40% bodyweight total weight lifted long-term.
- Bench press: 30% bodyweight as starting point, ~75% as long-term ceiling.

**Daily mobility + foam rolling: 15–30 min/day, separate from antagonist sessions.**

This is the lower-cost, higher-frequency habit. Foam rolling (SMR) + dynamic + static stretching, integrated as:

- Part of pre-climbing warm-up (with the static-stretching constraint below).
- A before-bed routine.
- Rest-day flexibility work.

Sequence rule: **roll first, stretch second.** Hörst Ch.6 Tip 7: *"Use foam rolling in conjunction with stretching for a synergistic effect — roll first, stretch second."* Foam rolling lengthens tissue and breaks adhesions; subsequent stretching then operates on a tissue that can actually elongate.

**The CUE-02 constraint — pre-performance static stretching.** L0 rule: no heavy or >30 s static stretching of forearm flexors (or any major working muscle) in pre-performance warm-up. Static stretching ≥30 s reduces grip strength up to 60 min after (GTO inhibition + decreased musculotendinous stiffness). Pre-climb, favor **dynamic warm-up + light Armaid/SMR + light traversing.** Save deeper static stretching for post-climb or rest days. This applies to the antagonist sessions too — heavy stretching mid-session of the muscles you're about to lift with is counterproductive.

### 5. The "must do, just do" framing

Hörst Ch.6's strongest exhortation: *"Time commitment is 20 to 40 minutes, twice per week. No excuses — just do it!"*

The reason for the directness is that this is the content climbers most reliably skip. The cost-benefit case is almost laughably one-sided:

- **Time cost:** ~60 min/week.
- **Equipment cost:** ~$100–200 one-time (a few dumbbells, a TRX, an Armaid). No gym membership required.
- **Benefit:** the only intervention with consistent systematic-review evidence of reducing climber shoulder + elbow injuries (Quarmby 2023). Reduces clinical-grade impingement risk, medial + lateral epicondylitis risk, and to a meaningful degree SLAP-tear risk under dynamic load. Removes the unrecognized stabilizer-weakness ceiling on max grip and pulling output.

The engine encodes the cost-benefit asymmetry structurally: D58 puts postural-correction exercises into every active phase, D60 makes wrist-extensor work a default, and the planner allocates antagonist sessions twice per week as standard content rather than as an opt-in. A user can decline to do the work — the engine respects autonomy (L1) — but the default is "you train this; if you want to skip, that's a deliberate choice and the coach will name what you're trading."

### 6. Common modifications and edge cases

- **Equipment-light households.** Most antagonist work needs minimal equipment: a TRX or rings, a couple of dumbbells (5/10/15 lb to start), a rubber band or Powerfingers, foam roller. The catalog includes equipment substitutions where possible (e.g. exercise band alternative to dumbbell rotator-cuff work, push-ups in place of bench press).
- **Active injury in the antagonist circuit.** If the user has a current shoulder/elbow injury, the antagonist session needs modification — not removal. Generally: lower load, pain-free range only, and physio-cleared exercises. The coach defaults to "ask your physio" rather than re-engineering a session unilaterally.
- **Female / youth / older climbers.** The 2×/week prescription holds across populations (Hörst Ch.6 doesn't gender-stratify it). For youth <16, antagonist load stays light — same volume, same exercises (where age-appropriate), lighter resistance. For climbers 40+, recovery between antagonist sessions may need to extend (3-day spacing rather than 2-day); the principle doesn't change. Detail in `[[14_female_age_youth]]` once written.
- **Warm-up integration.** The first set of wrist stabilizer work (EX-ANT-01 to EX-ANT-03) doubles as warm-up content before climbing. This is efficient — the same exercise that warms up the forearm extensors *is* the prophylactic loading the extensors need anyway.

## How the engine applies this

**D58 — Postural correction exercises in every active phase.** The planner allocates antagonist + stabilizer + postural sessions 2×/week throughout Base, Strength/Power, and Power Endurance phases. Deload weeks may reduce intensity but maintain frequency. Exercise selection rotates through the seven families (§3) to cover all the inhibited muscle groups across a mesocycle rather than hitting the same three exercises every session.

**D59 — Hypertonic / inhibited muscle reference table.** The engine uses this framework to assign asymmetric prescription: hypertonic muscles get foam rolling + post-climb stretching + Armaid; inhibited muscles get progressive resistance work. The user-facing impact is that the same "back & shoulder" complaint gets different exercise prescriptions depending on which side of the imbalance the engine identifies as primary (typically inhibited side strengthening + hypertonic side release).

**D60 — Wrist extension protocol for epicondylitis prevention.** Reverse wrist curls + wrist-extension isometric + pronator isolation appear as default antagonist components, not as conditional content gated on a current elbow complaint. The empirical basis: chronic flexor/extensor imbalance is the universal climber-population finding, and lateral epicondylitis is a chronic-load injury whose prevention starts long before symptoms (Hörst Ch.13 §5.2).

**CUE-02 — No heavy forearm-flexor static stretching pre-performance.** Enforced in warm-up generation. Pre-performance warm-ups use dynamic content (arm circles, finger curls, light traversing), foam rolling, and light Armaid work — not heavy static holds of the forearm flexors. Heavier static stretching is scheduled for post-climb or rest-day mobility blocks.

**Foam rolling + mobility as daily content.** A 15–30 min daily mobility routine is offered as a parallel track to the 2×/week antagonist sessions. Users can schedule it as part of warm-up, before bed, or rest-day. Compliance is the limiting factor — the engine encourages consistency rather than perfectionism (any amount of daily mobility > zero).

**No power before stability — phase-ordering enforced.** The macrocycle structure (Base → Strength/Power → Power Endurance → Performance → Deload, `[[01_periodization]]`) embeds the pyramid principle: power-phase content is gated by the prior Base work, which includes a heavy dose of mobility + stability + antagonist + corrective. A user who skipped Base and wants to jump to limit boulder + campus is, in practice, attempting to build power on an unstabilized base — and the engine pushes back on that.

## When user asks…

**"Do I really need to do antagonist work twice a week? It's the boring part."**

> Hörst's framing — and the Quarmby 2023 systematic review — is unambiguous: strength training is the only intervention with consistent evidence of preventing shoulder and elbow injuries in climbers. The cost is small: roughly 60 minutes a week, equipment you can pick up for $100–200. The return is large: you avoid the climber's-posture imbalance that drives most chronic shoulder and elbow issues, and you remove an unrecognized stabilizer-weakness ceiling on max grip and pull output. Hörst calls weak rotator cuff "a common, yet unrecognized, limiting constraint" — fitter shoulders often translate directly to fitter climbing. If you skip it, what you're trading is real: the planner can't keep you progressing through your 40s and 50s without the corrective work doing its job. Boring is fine; the engine takes care of variety across exercises so you're not doing the same three forever.

**"What's the minimum I can get away with if I'm time-crunched this week?"**

> Honest minimum: one session of ~20 min that hits scapular pull-ups + Y exercise + reverse wrist curls + a few push-ups. That's not the full prescription but it's *something*, and the gap between zero and 20 min is much bigger than the gap between 20 min and 60 min. The pyramid principle (Hörst Ch.6) is "stability before strength" — if you're cutting time anywhere, cut climbing volume, not stability work. The other thing you can compress is the daily mobility: even 5 min of foam rolling on lats + pecs + IT band + a couple of static stretches before bed gets most of the benefit. Don't let perfect be the enemy of done.

**"I have a sore right shoulder — should I still do the antagonist session?"**

> Modify rather than skip. If the soreness is the muscle-fatigue kind, an antagonist session at lower load on the unaffected side + bilateral push exercises at reduced load is reasonable. If it's "something feels wrong in the joint," that's a different conversation — get an evaluation, and in the meantime the engine drops overhead loading on that side entirely. The general principle Hörst applies: avoid any exercise that causes sharper pain or lingering/worsening pain after a workout. If the soreness is at the rotator cuff area or under the acromion, you're in territory where guessing wrong costs you a month of recovery — physio first, then we rebuild the session.

**"Should I stretch before climbing?"**

> Dynamic + light static, yes. Heavy static stretching of the forearm flexors specifically, no — that's an L0 rule (CUE-02). Heavy or >30-second static stretching of the working muscles reduces strength output for up to 60 min after (GTO inhibition, reduced musculotendinous stiffness). The pre-climb warm-up that works: 5–10 min general aerobic, foam rolling (especially lats, pecs, IT band, lateral forearm), dynamic content (arm circles, finger curls), light traversing graduating to limit attempts. Save deep static stretching for post-climb or rest day. The mobility-rich climbers you see at the gym doing splits before bouldering are not actually warming up well — they're impairing the strength they're about to use.
