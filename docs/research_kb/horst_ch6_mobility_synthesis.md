## 1. Core Philosophy

**Pyramid of Training (Figure 6.1):** Three tiers from base to apex:
- **Base:** Mobility & Stability ← *must be built first*
- **Middle:** Strength
- **Apex:** Power

**Key mantra:** "To maximize your potential and reduce injury risk, it's essential to develop stability before strength, and strength before power."

**Why this chapter is critical:** The ubiquity of injured climbers (an alarming number needing surgery) is evidence of the prevalence of over-enthusiastic training, climbing too much (with too few rest days), and a failure to properly train arm and shoulder mobility and stability before engaging in high-end climbing and advanced climbing-specific training (such as fingerboard and campus board training).

**Time investment:** Fifteen to thirty minutes per day into a selection of these exercises and you will be on your way to moving — and feeling — better, both on and off the rock.

---

## 2. Flexibility vs Mobility — Key Distinction

**Flexibility** = the range of motion around a joint (e.g., climbers need flexibility around the hips for high-stepping, stemming, and aggressive drop-knee positions). Influenced by joint mechanics, tendon, fascia, and musculature. Responds to dedicated training.

**Mobility** = how well a joint moves through the appropriate range of motion in specific physical activities. Influenced by many factors including motor control, chronic movement patterns, soft tissue adhesions and injury, and — yes — flexibility.

**Common problem spots for climbers:**
- **Shoulder joint:** tight, weak, sore, and/or injured muscles diminish mobility and compromise functional movement → "Climber's Posture" (Figure 6.2 = tight pectorals and weak lower trapezius contribute to rounded shoulders and rounding of the upper back)
- **Forearm muscles:** weak antagonist muscles and soft tissue dysfunction contribute to poor movement patterns, undue compensatory strain, and injury
- **Hips and lower back:** often more a result of excessive time sitting, long-term effects can impact back, core, and hip function

**Bottom line:** These common problem spots can be largely corrected given daily mobility and flexibility work.

## 3. SECTION A — Foam Rolling / Self-Myofascial Release (SMR)

**Principle:** SMR will help lengthen muscle fibers, release trigger points, increase circulation, and perhaps even encourage fascial remodeling. Make SMR a daily habit → improve mobility and lower the risk of certain overuse injuries such as medial and lateral epicondylitis.

**Method:** Address tight muscles and hunt out trigger points. Pause 15–30 seconds on any painful spot (trigger point); let the tissue release before moving on.

---

### EX-SMR-01: Upper Back and Shoulder Roll
**Category:** Foam rolling / SMR
**Target muscles:** Trapezius, rhomboids, teres major/minor, infraspinatus, posterior deltoid; latissimus dorsi
**Equipment:** Foam roller
**Photo:** [img-3] — two positions shown (upper back roll, then tilted for lats)

**Protocol:**
1. Begin with foam roller under your upper back, arms crossed over chest, knees bent, feet flat on floor
2. Slowly roll up and down along the thoracic spine
3. Pause 15–30 seconds on any painful spots (trigger points) in your trapezius and rhomboids
4. Next, tilt about 30 degrees to roll the latissimus dorsi and up across the posterior deltoid and infraspinatus
5. Dwell on any sore or tight spots for about half a minute
6. Return to neutral position, extend hands overhead (elbows locked, hands butt contact with floor)
7. Slowly roll up and down along the thoracic spine again

**Benefit:** Excellent for improving thoracic extension and addressing the posture issues and shoulder strains common among climbers

---

### EX-SMR-02: Lower Back and Glutes Roll
**Category:** Foam rolling / SMR
**Target muscles:** Erector spinae, quadratus lumborum (QL), gluteus maximus, gluteus medius
**Equipment:** Foam roller
**Photo:** [img-3] — lower body positions shown

**Protocol:**
1. **Lower back:** Position foam roller perpendicular to the lumbar spine; roll slowly up and down along the often-tight erector spinae muscles; add a slight roll to each side to find the quadratus lumborum (QL — connects pelvis to spine, common source of lower back pain)
2. **Gluteus maximus:** From a sitting position with straight legs and arms extended behind you; roll the gluteal muscles of the buttock
3. **Gluteus medius** (sides of buttocks, just below the hip — produces external thigh rotation when the knee is flexed): Bend one leg, lean to the opposite side; roll just a few centimeters up and down and side to side to quickly locate the trigger point; dwell for a minute and feel the tightness release

**Why important:** Many climbers discover extremely tight gluteus medius muscles — this isn't surprising since it's the gluteus medius that produces external thigh rotation when the knee is flexed (as it is in just about every climbing move)

---

### EX-SMR-03: Hip Flexors and IT Band
**Category:** Foam rolling / SMR
**Target muscles:** Hip flexors (front of upper thighs and pelvis), iliotibial (IT) band
**Equipment:** Foam roller
**Photo:** [img-4] — two positions shown (hip flexors prone, IT band side-lying)

**Protocol:**
1. **Hip flexors:** Assume a prone position with foam roller under your hips; only toes and forearms contacting the floor; roll just a few centimeters up and down along the hip flexors; tilt slightly to the side to attack the hip flexors only on that side (doubles the pressure); dwell up to a minute on each group (side) of hip flexors
2. **IT band:** Lie on your side with the foam roller under the upper outside of one leg; support much of your weight on the floor-side forearm, and position the other foot on the floor for balance; slowly roll along the outside of the leg, tracking the roller from just below the hip to just above the knee

---

### EX-SMR-04: Pronator "Pin and Stretch"
**Category:** Foam rolling / SMR / trigger point
**Target muscles:** Pronator teres (inside of elbow, just above the medial epicondyle)
**Equipment:** Thumb of opposite hand, or Armaid
**Photo:** [img-4] — arm extended showing pronator location

**Protocol:**
1. Locate the pronator teres: extend an arm straight to the front and then make a tight fist while you pronate the hand completely (as if pouring water from a jug)
2. The pronator teres will bulge on the inside of the elbow just above the bony medial epicondyle
3. Relax your fist, use the thumb of the opposite hand, press deeply into the pronator muscle (the "pin") and hold for 30 seconds — will likely be quite painful, a sign you should also do the pronator strengthening exercise
4. Repeat the "pin" with your thumb, but this time slowly pronate and supinate your hand to achieve a deeper massage and stretch of the pronator
5. Alternatively: use an Armaid on your pronator (provides even better leverage than your thumb)

**Coaching note:** If it is very painful, that's a sign you should also do the pronator strengthening exercise (pronator isolation)

---

### EX-SMR-05: Wrist Extensors "Pin and Stretch"
**Category:** Foam rolling / SMR / trigger point
**Target muscles:** Finger and wrist extensor muscles (lateral forearm), brachioradialis
**Equipment:** Armaid (preferred) or thumb of opposite hand
**Photo:** [img-5] — Armaid use on lateral forearm shown

**Protocol:**
1. Using an Armaid (or your thumb), probe the soft tissue of the lateral forearm to locate any sore spots
2. When you find one, apply steady pressure and hold for 30 seconds
3. "Pin and stretch" the trigger point by making a fist and flexing your wrist as you apply increasing pressure on the sore spot
4. Climbers with numerous trigger points on the lateral forearm should invest at least 5–10 minutes per day addressing these developing problem spots

**Why important:** The finger and wrist extensor muscles are a common problem spot, especially among climbers who are chronic crimpers. Persistently tight extensor muscles often develop painful knots and trigger points at one or more locations from the middle lateral forearm up to within about one inch of the bony lateral epicondyle. Daily forearm stretching of the extensor muscles is essential (see Finger Extensors Stretch), but addressing these tight muscles with an Armaid will lessen tension, release trigger points, and ultimately reduce your chance of someday developing lateral tendinosis.

---

### EX-SMR-06: Forearm "Pin and Stretch" — General (Armaid/Rubbit)
**Category:** Foam rolling / SMR
**Target muscles:** All forearm flexor and extensor muscles; medial and lateral epicondyle attachment areas
**Equipment:** Armaid or Rubbit
**Photo:** [img-5] — Armaid and Rubbit shown

**Protocol:**
- With a braced finger, or better yet an Armaid or Rubbit, hold firm pressure on a sore spot (trigger point), then stretch the muscle
- Feel the tension or trigger point release over the course of a minute or two of therapy
- Locate other sore spots and similarly "pin and stretch" them until they submit
- This can be a painful process but constitutes "good pain" — do not overdo it, or ever apply this technique directly to a tendon or epicondyle

**Hörst's strong recommendation:** Armaid and Rubbit are the two best massage tools he's used and he considers them so critical for maintaining forearm health that he takes one on all his climbing trips.

---

### EX-SMR-07: Elbow Voodoo Floss
**Category:** Tissue mobilization / circulation
**Target:** Cold, stiff muscles and joints; connective tissue; chronically sore or previously injured elbow
**Equipment:** Voodoo floss band (tight rubbery band, available from RogueFitness.com)
**Photo:** [img-6] — elbow wrapped with voodoo floss shown

**Protocol:**
1. Begin wrapping the voodoo floss about 4 inches below the elbow and tightly wrap all the way to about 4 inches above the elbow (50% overlaps)
2. Tuck the end of the band under the previous wraps
3. Goal: wrap tight enough to occlude blood flow — your hand will turn purple or white; shoot for a tightness of 8 on a scale of 1 to 10
4. Now flex and extend your elbow for about 20 slow repetitions
5. Remove the voodoo floss after about one minute

**Benefit:** Long-term voodoo flossing on a chronically sore or previously injured elbow will help improve tissue quality, increase range of motion, and enhance beneficial circulation. Also an excellent method of warming up the elbows before training or climbing.

---

### EX-SMR-08: Pectoral Release with Lacrosse Ball
**Category:** Foam rolling / SMR
**Target muscles:** Pectorals, upper trapezius
**Equipment:** Lacrosse ball or tennis ball (more forgiving); or foam roller (less precise)
**Photo:** [img-6] — standing against wall with lacrosse ball shown

**Protocol:**
1. Stand upright and place the ball between your pectoral muscle and a wall or vertical column
2. Easily move your torso position to direct the ball onto any tight spots
3. Pause with the ball on the sore spots and wait for the tissues to submit

**Why important:** Tight pectorals contribute to the poor posture and shoulder dysfunction that many climbers exhibit (see Figure 6.2). Releasing tight pectorals (and the upper trapezius) and strengthening the lower trapezius and rotator cuff muscles will go a long way to improving functional movement and reducing your injury risk.

## 4. SECTION B — Upper-Body Stretches

**Critical warm-up rule:** Pre-workout stretching must always be preceded by a warm-up activity that will increase muscle temperatures and spread synovial fluid through the joints and tendon sheaths. Static stretching alone can injure a cold muscle (Shrier 1999, 2000). **Minimum warm-up before stretching:** 5–15 minutes of low-intensity exercise (jogging, easy climbing on vertical walls with good holds), or foam rolling.

**Pre-stretch warm-up sequence:** At the very least, 25 jumping jacks followed by 20 arm circles and 20 finger flexors (see below), then a few minutes of self-massage to the finger tendons, palm of your hand, and forearm muscles and biceps.

---

### EX-STR-01: Arm Circles (Dynamic Warm-Up)
**Category:** Dynamic stretch / warm-up
**Target:** Shoulder joints; arms; fingers — circulation
**Equipment:** None
**Photo:** None — simple movement

**Protocol:**
1. Stand with arms out to the side and parallel to the floor
2. Begin moving your arms in small circles
3. Gradually increase the size of the circle until you feel slight tension in the shoulders — go no larger with the circles beyond this point
4. Do NOT wildly whip your arms or perform a rapid windmill-like motion
5. Complete 20 smooth, moderately paced arm circles
6. Then proceed to Finger Curls

**Timing:** Before engaging in any of the upper-body static stretches that follow

---

### EX-STR-02: Finger Curls and Massage (Dynamic Warm-Up)
**Category:** Dynamic warm-up / circulation
**Target:** Forearm muscles; lubricating synovial fluid in finger joints; tendon pulleys
**Equipment:** None
**Photo:** None

**Protocol:**
1. Stand with arms relaxed by your sides
2. Close your hands to make relaxed fists, then quickly open your hands and fan out the fingers as if you're flicking water off your fingertips
3. Continue for 30–40 repetitions
4. Use a pace of about 2 repetitions per second
5. Now perform a minute or two of massage on both sides of your fingers and hands — will encourage blood flow and help warm the tendons and tendon pulleys

**Use:** Must-do warm-up for climbers — increases circulation to forearm muscles and spreads lubricating synovial fluid in the joints of the fingers

---

### EX-STR-03: Finger Isolation Stretch
**Category:** Static stretch / warm-up
**Target:** Finger tendons and joints (one finger at a time)
**Equipment:** None
**Photo:** [img-7] — hand position shown

**Protocol:**
1. Either sitting or standing, position one hand palm-up at chest level
2. Curl the fingers of the other hand into a loose fist
3. Place the index finger of the other hand across the tip segment of the finger being stretched
4. Position the thumb under the finger near the base (hand) knuckle
5. Bracing with the thumb below, gradually apply pressure with the index finger to further close the bent finger and push it away from the palm
6. Stop when you feel mild tension in the joints
7. Hold the stretch for 10 seconds
8. Release the finger for a few seconds, then repeat the stretch for another 10 seconds
9. Repeat the stretching sequence with all 8 fingers
10. Stop immediately if you experience any pain

---

### EX-STR-04: Finger Flexor Stretch
**Category:** Static stretch
**Target:** Forearm muscles that flex the fingers; grip tendons
**Equipment:** None
**Photo:** [img-7] — two positions shown (palm facing wall, palm facing up)

**Protocol:**
1. While standing, bring your hands together in front of your waist
2. Straighten the arm to be stretched and lay the fingertips into the palm of the other hand
3. Position the hand of the stretching arm so the palm is facing the other hand with the thumb pointing inward
4. Pull back on the fingers of the straight arm until a mild stretch begins in the forearm muscles
5. Hold for 10 seconds
6. Release the stretch and then turn the stretching arm 180 degrees so that the stretching arm is now positioned with the palm facing upward and the thumb pointing out to the side
7. Again, using your other hand, pull your fingers back until a stretch begins in the forearm muscles
8. Hold for 10 seconds
9. Repeat the stretch, in both positions, with the other arm

---

### EX-STR-05: Finger Extensors Stretch
**Category:** Static stretch
**Target:** Extensor muscles of the lateral forearm; brachioradialis
**Equipment:** None
**Photo:** [img-8] — arm extended position shown

**Why important:** These muscles are especially strained when crimping with a chicken-winged arm position, and so daily stretching (and Armaid use) is essential for lengthening the tissues and releasing tension that can eventually contribute to lateral epicondylitis.

**Protocol:**
1. With nearly straight arms, cross your hands in front of your body and interlace your fingers, palms together
2. While maintaining mild tension throughout the length of both arms, pull with one hand to flex the wrist of the other hand until you feel the stretch develop in the finger/wrist extensors along the outside of the forearm
3. Hold the stretch for about 20 seconds
4. Then pull with the other hand to create a stretch along the other arm
5. Perform the stretch twice on each arm

---

### EX-STR-06: Posterior Shoulder Stretch
**Category:** Static stretch
**Target:** Infraspinatus; trapezius; shoulder capsule
**Equipment:** None
**Photo:** [img-8] — arm across chest position shown

**Protocol:**
1. While in a standing position, bring one arm across your chest until the hand rests on the opposite shoulder
2. You must have your elbow bent (around 90 degrees) and maintain a shoulder back position — **do not roll your shoulder forward**
3. With the other hand, grasp behind the bent elbow from below
4. Pull on the bent elbow until you feel tension in the shoulder and upper back
5. Hold the stretch for 10 seconds
6. Release for a few seconds before repeating for 20 seconds more
7. Repeat the stretch with the other arm

---

### EX-STR-07: Rotator Cuff Stretch (Towel/Dowel Stretch)
**Category:** Static stretch
**Target:** Rotator cuff (external rotators — infraspinatus, teres minor)
**Equipment:** Towel or dowel
**Photo:** [img-8] — standing with towel behind back shown

**Why:** A must-do before every climbing and training session. Specifically involves internal rotation of one arm in the bottom position (stretching the external rotators) while placing the other (top) arm in external rotation and shoulder flexion.

**Protocol:**
1. Roll up a towel and hold one end above and just behind your head
2. Grab the other end of the towel with the opposite hand (palm facing backward) behind your back
3. Pull up on the towel with the top hand until you feel the light stretch
4. Hold this position for 10–20 seconds
5. Perform the stretch 2 or 3 times on each side

---

### EX-STR-08: Anterior Shoulder, Chest, and Biceps Stretch
**Category:** Static stretch
**Target:** Biceps, pectorals, anterior shoulder — all common tight spots
**Equipment:** None (floor)
**Photo:** [img-9, img-10] — floor position shown

**Hörst's personal favorite** upper-body stretch — addresses all his common tight spots.

**Protocol:**
1. Sit on the floor with feet flat and knees bent about 90 degrees
2. Position your arms just behind your hips with the elbows straight, palms flat on the floor, and fingers pointing back
3. Slowly walk your hands away from your hips until you feel mild tension in your shoulders, pecs, and biceps
4. Be sure to keep your shoulders back (do not let them roll forward) — think about extending your lumbar spine and pushing your chest forward to provide a nice stretch into the pectorals
5. Hold this position for 20 seconds
6. Walk your fingers back a bit farther to enhance the stretch
7. Hold this position for 20–30 seconds before releasing

---

### EX-STR-09: Latissimus, Shoulder, and Triceps Stretch
**Category:** Static stretch
**Target:** Latissimus dorsi; posterior shoulder; triceps — overhead arm position common to climbing
**Equipment:** None
**Photo:** [img-9, img-10] — elbow pull behind head shown

**Protocol:**
1. Stand erect with arms overhead and bent at the elbows
2. Grab one elbow and gently pull it toward the back of your head until you feel a stretch in the back of the upper arm
3. Hold the stretch for 10 seconds
4. Release for a few seconds then perform a secondary stretch for about 20 seconds
5. You can extend this stretch down through the latissimus dorsi muscle by leaning slightly sideways in the direction of the pull
6. Repeat the stretch with the other arm

---

### Tips for Safe and Effective Flexibility Training (8 points)

1. Always engage in 5–15 minutes of jogging, jumping jacks, easy climbing, or foam rolling before beginning flexibility training — stretching a cold muscle can lead to injury
2. Stretch in a slow, gradually progressive manner — should produce mild discomfort, but never sharp pain
3. Perform a primary stretch of 10–30 seconds; release for a few seconds before performing a secondary stretch of 20–30 seconds
4. Direct slow, deep breathing throughout the stretch — inhale through your nose and exhale through your mouth
5. Maintain a neutral back position — neither rounded nor hyperextended — to maximize the stretch and avoid injury
6. Limit "gain" stretching to the legs and hips — be conservative in stretching the shoulders
7. Use foam rolling in conjunction with stretching for a synergistic effect — roll first, stretch second
8. **Refrain from excessive stretching of the forearm flexors prior to climbing**, since this may affect the nervous system in a way that reduces your maximum strength and power for up to one hour. Favor light stretching, Armaid use, and sports massage prior to performance climbing

## 5. SECTION C — Lower-Torso and Leg Stretches

**Why for climbers:** Increased hip turnout, wider stemming, and better ease at high-stepping will markedly improve center-of-mass positioning and quality of movement — conservation of energy. Daily lower-body stretching is a smart thing to do, not only as part of a pre-performance warm-up, but also as part of some before-bed mobility work and rest-day flexibility training.

---

### EX-STR-10: Buttocks and Lower-Back Stretch
**Category:** Static stretch
**Target:** Hip flexion; high-stepping facilitation
**Equipment:** None (floor)
**Photo:** [img-11] — lying on back, knee to chest shown

**Protocol:**
1. Lie flat on your back with both legs straight
2. Bend one leg and grasp it behind the thigh or around the upper shin
3. Pull it toward your chest
4. Hold the stretch for 10 seconds, then release for a few seconds
5. Pull the bent leg toward your chest again for a secondary stretch of about 20 seconds
6. Repeat with the other leg

---

### EX-STR-11: Hamstrings Stretch
**Category:** Static stretch
**Target:** Hamstring muscles (back of thigh) — restricting stem and high-step movements
**Equipment:** Optional fitness band or towel
**Photo:** [img-11] — leg raised with straight knee shown

**Protocol:**
1. Lie flat on your back with one leg straight and the other bent with the foot flat on the floor
2. Lift the straight leg upward, grab it behind the thigh or calf, and pull gently forward until you feel the stretch down the back of the leg
3. (Alternatively: keep the non-stretching leg flat on the floor)
4. Hold this stretch for 10 seconds, then release it slightly for a few seconds
5. Pull the leg once again for another 20–30 seconds
6. Be sure to maintain a straight leg throughout
7. Perform this stretch equally on both legs
8. Can also work this stretch by looping a fitness band or towel over your foot and regulating the stretch by pulling on the band

---

### EX-STR-12: Adductor Stretch
**Category:** Static stretch
**Target:** Adductor muscles (inner thigh/groin); hip turnout; stemming ability
**Equipment:** Wall
**Photo:** [img-11] — wall adductor stretch shown (feet up, legs spread)

**Protocol:**
1. Lie on the floor with your buttocks about six inches from a wall and your legs extending straight up it with a 90-degree bend at your hips
2. Slowly separate your legs by sliding your heels out to the sides
3. Concentrate on relaxing throughout your body and allow gravity to extend the split until you feel mild tension in your legs and groin
4. Hold this position for 20–30 seconds
5. Try to split your legs farther apart; if needed, press on your thighs to apply some downward pressure to extend the stretch
6. Hold this position for a minute or two

**Note:** Wearing socks will reduce friction between your heels and the wall, helping maximize the range of the stretch

---

### EX-STR-13: Groin Stretch
**Category:** Static stretch
**Target:** Groin; hip turnout; near-vertical climbing positions
**Equipment:** None (floor) + optional partner
**Photo:** [img-12] — sitting with soles together, elbows pressing knees shown

**Protocol:**
1. Sit upright with your legs flexed and knees out to the sides so that you can bring the soles of your feet together
2. Grasp your ankles and rest your elbows on the inside of the thighs
3. Press down with your elbows until you feel mild tension in your groin and inner thigh
4. Hold this stretch for 10 seconds, then release it for a few seconds
5. Apply pressure for a secondary stretch of 20–30 seconds
6. Next, lie flat on your back while keeping your feet together: relax and allow gravity to pull your knees toward the floor for another minute or two
7. To enhance the stretch: have a partner apply light downward pressure on your knees

---

### EX-STR-14: Piriformis Stretch
**Category:** Static stretch
**Target:** Piriformis (deep beneath gluteal muscles) — positions foot on holds up and out to the side; also relief for mild sciatica
**Equipment:** None (floor)
**Photo:** [img-12] — kneeling pigeon-type position shown

**Why for climbing:** The piriformis assists in lifting and rotating the thigh laterally. In climbing, used to position your foot on a hold that's up and out to the side. If you have trouble reaching out your foot to engage distant edges, a tight piriformis is likely part of the problem.

**Protocol:**
1. Kneel on the floor with your hands positioned under your shoulders
2. Shift your upper-body weight onto your arms so you can position one leg in front of you with the knee acutely flexed so that the outside of the foot contacts the floor near the opposite hip
3. Slide the other leg backward until it's nearly straight with the kneecap and toes touching the floor
4. Maintaining a straight torso, lower your hips toward the floor to increase the stretch
5. Hold this position for 10–20 seconds, then raise your hips slightly to release the stretch for a few seconds
6. Lower your hips again for a secondary stretch of 30–60 seconds
7. Repeat the stretch with the other leg
8. **Warning:** Perform this stretch with caution, and ease up at the first sign of any pain in the knees, hips, or lower back

---

### EX-STR-15: Hip Flexors and Quadriceps
**Category:** Static stretch
**Target:** Hip flexor muscle group (including powerhouse psoas); quadriceps
**Equipment:** None
**Photo:** [img-12, img-13] — kneeling lunge position with arm extended shown

**Why:** The hip flexor muscle group is commonly tight — not only because of the critical role they serve in running, hiking uphill, and lifting the legs in climbing, but also because they can readily shorten during extended periods of sitting. The hip flexors, including the powerhouse psoas, are a prime muscle group to target with foam rolling and stretching.

**Protocol:**
1. Kneel on the floor with one leg forward and bent at near 90 degrees, while the other leg is nearly straight and extended behind you
2. Resting your hands on the knee for balance, shift your hips forward while maintaining a flat or slightly extended spine — this should create some tension in the hip flexors atop the straight leg
3. Hold this position for 10 seconds, then raise your hips slightly to release the stretch for a few seconds
4. Shift your hips forward again, and if you can maintain balance, extend your arms straight overhead — this will enhance the stretch and perhaps elicit a "pop" from the lumbar zone
5. Hold the stretch for 30 seconds
6. Release the stretch and then flex your rear leg so that you can grab it with the opposite hand
7. Do a final stretch in which you both shift forward and pull gently on the rear foot — provides an excellent stretch of the quadriceps

---

### EX-STR-16: Calf Stretch
**Category:** Static stretch
**Target:** Calf muscles of the lower leg
**Equipment:** Small step or object
**Photo:** [img-13] — heel-drop on step shown

**Protocol:**
1. Elevate the front part of the foot on a small step or other object, keeping your heel on the floor
2. Simply lower your hip level and flex your knee forward to elicit a good stretch
3. Hold the stretch for 20 seconds
4. Release it for a few seconds
5. Then resume the stretch for another 30 seconds
6. Repeat the stretch with the other leg

---

### EX-STR-17: Abdominal Stretch
**Category:** Static stretch
**Target:** Abdominals; ability to quickly relax the abs and extend the torso
**Equipment:** None (floor)
**Photo:** [img-13, img-14] — cobra position shown

**Protocol:**
1. Begin in a prone position with your arms bent and palms flat on the floor next to your shoulders
2. Slowly press your shoulders away from the floor until you feel mild tension in your abdominals
3. Keep your legs and pelvis in contact with the floor for the duration of this stretch — it helps to contract your buttocks in order to maintain the position and reduce stress on your lower back
4. Hold the stretch for 10 seconds and then return to the starting position for a few seconds
5. Press up for a secondary stretch of 20–30 seconds
6. Relax and allow the curve of your spine to extend up through your upper back and neck
7. Look forward, but not up toward the ceiling
8. Do not be overly aggressive with this stretch — proceed with caution, especially if you have a history of back problems
9. Stop immediately if you experience any pain in the lower back

---

### EX-STR-18: Obliques, Hips, and Back Stretch
**Category:** Static stretch
**Target:** Hips, torso, back — torso-twisting, hip-kneeing movements common to steep sport climbs and boulder problems
**Equipment:** None (floor)
**Photo:** [img-14] — seated spinal twist shown

**Protocol:**
1. Sit erect on the floor with one leg straight and the other bent and crossing over the opposite knee
2. Slowly turn your body toward the side of the bent leg until you feel mild tension in your lower back, hips, and the side of your torso
3. Maintain a level head position and fix your eyes on the wall to the side of the bent leg
4. Hold the stretch for 10–20 seconds
5. Return to the starting position for a few seconds
6. Perform a secondary stretch for 20–30 seconds
7. If needed: enhance the stretch by levering your elbow against the thigh of the bent leg
8. Repeat the stretch in the other direction
9. Be sure to switch leg positions

## 6. SECTION D — Stabilizer and Antagonist Muscle Training

**Hörst's strongest claim in the book:** Training the stabilizer and antagonist muscles may be the single most important section in the entire book. While few climbers need additional motivation to train their finger or core strength, the majority of climbers spend too little time training the crucial muscles that support the wrist and shoulder during strenuous climbing moves.

**The consequence of neglect:** Left untrained, these "supporting cast" muscles, which stabilize the wrist and scapula, will eventually let you down in critical moments of fatigue — the result could be insidious slow-onset elbow tendinitis or shoulder impingement, or a more acute rotator cuff tear.

**Prescription:** Every climber should train their stabilizers and antagonists **twice per week**. Furthermore, the harder a person trains and climbs, the more indispensable these exercises become for preventing injury and ensuring longevity in the sport.

---

### SUB-SECTION D1: Training the Wrist Stabilizers

**The biomechanical problem:** The finger flexors suffer from a sort of functional insufficiency in that they are much weaker when the wrist is flexed and the muscles are short. Finger strength actually increases as the wrist extends! This is why fatiguing climbers lift their elbows into the chicken-wing position — it puts their wrist in a more extended position that enables their pumped forearms to grip a little longer. Done chronically, such chicken-winging stresses the extensor muscles and puts added stress on the tendon pulleys in the fingers. The result can be gradual onset of lateral epicondylitis and/or finger tendon problems.

**The fix:** Dedicate a modest amount of time to training the finger/wrist extensor muscles — in multiple ways, just as you train the finger flexors in a comprehensive manner.

**Minimum prescription:** At minimum, do one set of the following three exercises (with a light resistance) as part of your warm-up for climbing (or training), and then do two more sets with higher resistance toward the end of your workout.

### EX-ANT-01: Finger Extension Against Rubber Band
**Category:** Antagonist / warm-up
**Target:** Finger extensors
**Equipment:** Rubber band (or Powerfingers)
**Photo:** [img-15] — hand with rubber band shown

**Protocol:**
- Use of a rubber band (or similar) to extend your fingers against resistance
- Sufficient only for the purposes of warm-up and rehabilitation from injury
- Recommended: use Powerfingers to warm up before advancing to the exercises below

**Note:** This is for warm-up/prehab only — NOT sufficient as a standalone strength exercise for the wrist stabilizers

---

### EX-ANT-02: Reverse Wrist Curls
**Category:** Antagonist strength — wrist stabilizers
**Target:** Finger/wrist extensor muscles (lateral forearm)
**Equipment:** Dumbbell; bench, table, or thigh for forearm support
**Photo:** [img-15, img-16] — seated position with dumbbell, two positions shown

**Protocol:**
1. Fix your forearm in a stable, horizontal position using a bench, table, or on your thigh (if sitting)
2. Grasp the dumbbell with your hand overhanging the supporting surface
3. Begin with the dumbbell in the top (over-extended) position and lower it to the neutral (straight wrist) position, then curl the dumbbell back to the top position
4. Continue with slow, controlled reverse curls at a rate of about one rep per second
5. Recommend doing 2 sets with progressive resistance
6. **Set 1:** Select a weight that allows 20–25 repetitions, but stop the exercise before complete failure; rest 3 minutes
7. **Set 2:** A heavier dumbbell that allows only 10–15 reps
8. Initially: may need to use only a 5-pound dumbbell; advanced climbers may need as much as a 25–30 pound dumbbell for the high-resistance set
9. Do some mild stretching of the extensors during each rest period

**Note for elbow pain:** Anyone with elbow pain or recent history of lateral tendinosis should do only the negative phase of this exercise — use your free hand to grab the dumbbell-holding hand and aid its return to the top position (negative/lowering phase only)

---

### EX-ANT-03: Wrist Extension Isometric
**Category:** Antagonist strength — wrist stabilizers (isometric)
**Target:** Finger/wrist extensor muscles (emphasizes the over-extended position)
**Equipment:** Light dumbbell; clock with second hand or timing app
**Photo:** [img-16] — same setup as reverse wrist curl

**Protocol:**
1. Same setup as the reverse wrist curl, but using a lighter-weight dumbbell
2. The difference here is that there's no lowering phase
3. Simply hold the dumbbell in the up (over-extended position) for a long isometric contraction
4. Select a dumbbell light enough to allow an isometric hold in the over-extended position for 45–60 seconds
5. Do 2 sets with each arm, with a 3-minute rest between sets
6. Long term: strive to hold the over-extended position for a full 2 minutes
7. Good to do some stretching of the forearm extensors during the rest period

---

### EX-ANT-04: Wide Pinch with Wrist Extension
**Category:** Antagonist strength — wrist stabilizers (climbing-specific)
**Target:** Extensor muscles with fingers fully extended; wide pinch grip
**Equipment:** Wood blocks (2–3 pieces of 2×4 screwed together, two sizes) OR thick bumper weight plate
**Photo:** [img-17] — two variations shown: bumper plate pinch and wood block pinch

**Why essential:** Strengthens the extensor muscles with the fingers fully extended — mimics grabbing open-hand and wide pinch holds in climbing. The wrist extensors function a bit differently when the fingers are straight (extended) vs. when they're flexed (as in crimping or holding a dumbbell).

**Protocol:**
1. Stand upright with good posture
2. Pinch the wood block or bumper plate with a straight arm and extended wrist
3. Hold this position for 10–30 seconds

**For endurance (initial training):**
- Light weight that allows a full 30-second hold
- Do 3 with each hand, at least 1 minute rest in between

**For strength (longer term):**
- Use a heavier weight that allows only a 10-second hold
- Do 3 consecutive 10-second pinches with the same hand, resting only 30 seconds between each
- Do a total of 3 sets of 3 reps with each hand, resting for about 3 minutes between sets

### EX-ANT-05: Pronator Isolation (Sledgehammer)
**Category:** Antagonist strength — forearm pronation
**Target:** Pronator teres (important for maintaining muscle balance; prevents overuse of supinator/biceps)
**Equipment:** Ordinary 3-pound sledgehammer (or 16-inch length of 1-inch-diameter dowel with 2.5-pound weight on one end)
**Photo:** [img-18] — seated position with sledgehammer, two phases shown

**Why for climbers:** Arm-pulling movements naturally result in supination of the hand. If you perform a pull-up on a free-hanging set of holds, your hands naturally turn inward (supinate) as your biceps contract. Training forearm pronation is an important antagonist exercise for climbers to maintain muscle balance across the forearms.

**Protocol:**
1. Sit on a chair or bench with your forearm resting on your thigh, hand in the palm-up position
2. Firmly grip the sledgehammer with the heavy end extending to the outside and the handle parallel to the floor
3. Turn your hand inward (pronation) to lift the hammer to the vertical position — stop here
4. Now slowly lower the sledgehammer back to the starting position
5. Stop at the horizontal position for one second before beginning the next repetition
6. Continue lifting the hammer in this way for 20–25 repetitions
7. Choke up on the hammer if this feels overly difficult
8. Perform 2 sets with each hand

**Alternative:** Cut a 16-inch length of 1-inch-diameter dowel and mount a 2.5-pound weight on one end

---

### EX-ANT-06: Pronator Isolation with Exercise Band
**Category:** Antagonist strength — forearm pronation (alternative)
**Target:** Pronator teres
**Equipment:** Exercise band
**Photo:** [img-18, img-19] — seated with band under foot shown

**Protocol:**
1. In a sitting position, anchor one end of the band under your foot, then wrap the other end of the band around your hand
2. Remove all the slack between your hand and foot so that there's tension when holding the band palm-up with the back of your forearm resting on your thigh
3. Keeping your forearm in a fixed position, turn your hand inward (pronation) to reach the palms-down position
4. Return to the starting position and continue for 25 repetitions
5. Make sure there's enough tension in the band to provide moderate resistance throughout the full 180-degree range of motion
6. Do 2 sets with each hand

---

### EX-ANT-07: Reverse Arm Curls
**Category:** Antagonist strength — wrist stabilizers / elbow stabilizers
**Target:** Brachioradialis (elbow flexor, most active when hand is pronated — as in the majority of climbing moves); finger/wrist extensors as isometric stabilizers
**Equipment:** Barbell or bent E-Z curl bar (NOT dumbbells)
**Photo:** [img-19] — standing with EZ bar shown

**Why Hörst's favorite warm-up exercise:** While it obviously activates the biceps, more importantly it demands an isometric contraction of the wrist extensors and calls into play the often overlooked brachioradialis to stabilize and flex the elbow. The brachioradialis is most active when the hand is pronated, as in the majority of climbing moves — therefore strengthening the brachioradialis means a stronger wrist and a more stable elbow and forearm while gripping and pulling down on the rock.

**Protocol:**
1. Take an overhand grip and, with elbows fixed at your side, curl the bar upward to reach full elbow flexion
2. Continue for 15–20 repetitions, being sure to contract your core, glutes, and spinal erectors throughout the duration of the exercise
3. Do one set with light weight as part of your warm-up
4. Do another moderately heavy set or two with your antagonist training

### SUB-SECTION D2: Training Rotator Cuff and Scapular Stability

**The critical argument:** The rotator cuff is arguably the most stressed group of muscles in the body of a climber. While the forearms get all the attention and pump, the rotator cuff and scapular stabilizers are the real heroes as a climber repeatedly pulls with his arms from a wide range of angles, both statically and dynamically applying force of varying intensity.

**The alarming finding:** Weak rotator cuff muscles are a common, yet unrecognized, limiting constraint in your maximum pulling strength, lunging power, grip strength, and contact strength. So if you're a hard-training climber frustrated by a lack of strength/power gains on the rock, it may be that your central governor is limiting power output due to afferent feedback from your weak, unstable shoulder joints. This is why it's essential to develop stability before strength, and strength before power.

**Three subsections:**
1. Two exercises that isolate the four muscles that comprise the rotator cuff: supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, and the subscapularis
2. Four exercises that work the supporting cast of muscles, including the upper, middle, and lower trapezius, rhomboids, and serratus anterior, that move the scapula into proper position for overhead arm movements
3. Three pushing exercises that work the entire antagonist system (see Sub-Section D3 below)

---

### EX-RC-01: Dumbbell Internal Rotation
**Category:** Rotator cuff isolation
**Target:** Subscapularis (of the four rotator cuff muscles, only the subscapularis contributes to internal rotation)
**Equipment:** 5–15 lb dumbbell; rolled-up towel
**Photo:** [img-20] — lying side position, two phases shown

**Protocol:**
1. Lie on your side with your bottom arm in front of your waist
2. Place a rolled-up towel under your head to support your neck
3. Rest your other arm along your hip and upper thigh
4. Hold a 5–15 lb dumbbell in the hand of your other arm, positioning this forearm perpendicular to your body
5. Lift the weight up to your body and hold for a moment before lowering it back to the floor
6. The upper portion of your arm should remain fixed throughout the range of motion — think of the upper arm and shoulder as a door hinge that allows your forearm to swing "up and down"
7. Continue in a slow but steady motion for a total of 20–25 repetitions
8. Do 2 sets on each side, with a 3-minute rest between sets
9. Select a weight heavy enough to make you work, but not so great that you have to strain hard or compromise technique
10. Increase weight in 2–5 lb increments as needed

**Alternative:** Anchor an exercise band to a waist-high object; standing, grip the band and pull it across your body — key is to keep your elbow and upper arm in a fixed position and only move your hand and forearm across your body. However, dumbbell exercises are preferred by Hörst for serious training of the rotator cuff.

---

### EX-RC-02: Dumbbell External Rotation
**Category:** Rotator cuff isolation
**Target:** Infraspinatus and teres minor (primary external rotators) — also some help from deltoid
**Equipment:** 5–15 lb dumbbell; rolled-up towel
**Photo:** [img-20, img-21] — lying side position, two phases shown

**Why high priority:** Strengthening the external rotators should be a high priority, as this will provide additional protection of the cuff during above-the-head Gaston positions common to hard climbing.

**Protocol:**
1. Lie on your side with your bottom arm in front of your waist and a rolled-up towel under your head to support your neck. Alternatively, you can bend your bottom arm and use it as a headrest
2. Hold a 5- to 10-pound dumbbell in the hand of your top arm
3. Rest the upper arm and elbow on the top side of your body, and then bend at the elbow so that the forearm hangs down over your belly and the weight rests on the floor
4. Keeping your elbow and upper arm fixed, lift the weight upward toward the ceiling and stop when you feel the resistance ease as you move past the horizontal position
5. Return the weight to the starting position and continue for 20 to 25 repetitions
6. Do 2 sets on each side with a 3-minute rest between each set
7. Increase weight in 2- to 5-pound increments. Initially you may need to use as little as 5 pounds. With long-term training, you should be able to improve to using a 10-pound dumbbell; especially fit climbers may be able to use up to 15 pounds
8. This is a difficult exercise, and using proper technique is essential for isolating the external rotators. Err on the side of using too little weight, rather than too much

**Alternative:** With an exercise band anchored to a waist-high object, stand with your side (opposite the arm to be trained) facing the band anchor spot. Grab the free end of the band with your forearm across your belly and upper arm at your side. Keeping your elbow and upper arm in a fixed position, pull outward on the exercise band with your forearm opening up away from the body like a swinging door. Repeat this 25 times, adjusting resistance as needed by changing your distance from the anchor point or using a different exercise band.

---

### EX-SCAP-01: "T" Exercise (Scapular Retraction)
**Category:** Scapular stabilizer
**Target:** Middle trapezius and rhomboids — muscles that produce scapular retraction
**Equipment:** TRX Sling Trainer (preferred) or bench + light dumbbells
**Photo:** [img-21, img-22] — TRX standing position shown (starting and finish)

**Protocol (TRX version):**
1. Stand in a standing position with a TRX trainer
2. Grasp the handles with straight arms extended forward and palms facing each other
3. Contract your core, glutes, and leg muscles and lean backward to weight the TRX; you can also walk your feet slightly forward (farther forward is harder)
4. Maintaining a tight torso and straight arms, pull your hands apart until your arms are straight out to your side in a T position — think about squeezing your shoulder blades as close together as possible
5. Slowly lower back to the starting position and continue for 10 to 20 repetitions
6. Do 2 sets

**Protocol (bench version):**
1. Assume a prone position on a bench with your arms hanging straight down
2. Contract your core and glutes, and lift straight arms out to the side until raised just above horizontal
3. Feel your shoulder blades come together, and actively squeeze them at the top position
4. Continue for 20 repetitions
5. You can progress to using very light dumbbells (a few pounds max)

---

### EX-SCAP-02: "Y" Exercise (Lower Trapezius)
**Category:** Scapular stabilizer
**Target:** Lower trapezius — produces scapular depression and upward rotation, essential to avoid shoulder impingement when doing overhead movements
**Equipment:** TRX Sling Trainer (preferred) or bench + light dumbbells
**Photo:** [img-22] — TRX Y position shown (starting and finish)

**Why critical for climbers:** Among climbers, the lower traps are commonly a weak link and thus a potential contributing factor to shoulder injury. If you frequently engage in campus training and lunge moves, then a strong mid and lower trapezius are vital to facilitate proper scapular positioning (to avoid trashing your shoulders).

**Protocol (TRX version):**
1. Set up in the same way as when doing Ts, but the motion is up and outward to form a "Y"
2. Stop pulling when your arms come in line with your body — at this top position your arms should be just beyond shoulder width apart
3. Be sure to contract your core, glutes, and legs to maintain rigidity throughout the range of motion
4. Think about drawing your scapula downward as you reach the top of the Y position
5. Do 2 sets of 10 to 20 repetitions with a 3-minute rest in between

**Protocol (bench version):**
1. Lie prone on a bench with your arms straight and hanging down under the bench below your face
2. With a stiff core and tight glutes, lift straight arms up into the Y position with your hands just beyond shoulder width at the top position
3. No matter which method of Y training you select, do 2 sets of 10 to 20 repetitions with a 3-minute rest in between
4. You can progress to using a 1- or 2-pound dumbbell in each hand

---

### EX-SCAP-03: Scapular Push-Up
**Category:** Scapular stabilizer
**Target:** Serratus anterior — vital for scapular stability and protraction
**Equipment:** None (floor)
**Photo:** [img-22, img-23] — plank position, two phases shown (protraction and retraction)

**Protocol:**
1. Assume the classic plank position with elbows under your shoulders, a neutral spine, and tight abs and spinal erectors
2. Keeping your torso stiff and straight, allow your chest to sag downward between your arms
3. Now drive your elbows and forearms into the floor to return to the top position — feel your scapula slide around your rib cage as the serratus anterior contracts
4. The range of motion is only a few inches, but it's a great isolation exercise if you do 20 or so repetitions
5. This movement is not meant to be heavily loaded, so there's no need to use additional weight
6. Do 1 or 2 sets

**Alternative:** Can also be done from a straight-arm push-up position instead of the plank

---

### EX-SCAP-04: Scapular Pull-Up
**Category:** Scapular stabilizer
**Target:** Lower trapezius and latissimus dorsi — develops the ability to depress, rotate, and retract the scapula; kinesthetic awareness of scapular position
**Equipment:** Pull-up bar
**Photo:** [img-23] — hanging position, two phases shown (shrugged shoulders → scapulae depressed)

**Why perhaps the best climbing exercise nobody does:** Keeping your shoulders healthy and developing proper movement patterns in pulling motions demands the ability to depress, rotate, and retract the scapula. Regular use of this isolation exercise will develop better kinesthetic awareness of your scapula position and enable you to climb harder and longer with good form, despite growing fatigue. Furthermore, being able to quickly and forcibly engage the lower trapezius and lats will empower you to keep your scapula in proper position when campus training and lunging.

**Protocol:**
1. Strong climbers can do this exercise with full body weight, but Hörst suggests learning with less resistance by keeping your feet on the floor and flexing your knees enough to hang with straight arms from a pull-up bar
2. Either way, the range of motion is only a few inches — from a full hang with somewhat shrugged shoulders, the desired action is to draw the scapula down and together
3. The best learning cues are to try to "bend the bar" and think about doing a reverse shrug (i.e., shoulders drawn downward). Do this and you'll feel your head shift backward and your chest raise upward, as your scapulas pinch together
4. Hold the top position for 1 second, then return to the starting position
5. Do 8 to 12 reps, keeping straight arms and tight spinal erectors and glutes throughout
6. At first you may find this to be a difficult exercise (a sign that you've found a critical weakness to correct!), but resist the urge to overdo it
7. Add a second set to your workout only after you've mastered the exercise

### SUB-SECTION D3: Antagonist Push Training

**Rationale:** The previous sections presented the must-do isolation exercises for strengthening your rotator cuff and scapular stabilizers. This chapter concludes with three vital multi-joint exercises that target the large climbing antagonist muscles, including the three heads of the deltoids, the pectorals, and the triceps. The importance of doing these exercises cannot be overstated, considering the need to balance out all the pulling you do in climbing with some quality push movements.

**Training philosophy:** As in training the stabilizer muscles, you want to train the antagonists with a moderate resistance and relatively high number of repetitions. Keep in mind that performing a high-weight hypertrophy or maximum-strength program is unnecessary and, in fact, could be detrimental if it causes unwanted gain in muscle mass.

**Equipment recommendation:** While the three following exercises can all be performed on standard health club equipment (Cybex, Olympic weights, and so forth), Hörst would advise against purchasing a health club membership just to gain access to the necessary machinery. Instead, he recommends a one-time investment in a few pairs of dumbbells (only $100 to $200) — they'll last you a lifetime. Alternatively, you might ask the climbing gym you patronize to purchase a few dumbbells or a set of free weights. It's also possible to do some of these pushing exercises with a sling trainer such as the TRX.

---

### EX-PUSH-01: Shoulder Press (Dumbbell)
**Category:** Antagonist push — multi-joint
**Target:** Deltoids (all three heads), triceps; also extra work for the cuff and scapular stabilizers
**Equipment:** Dumbbells; bench (seated)
**Photo:** [img-24] — seated with dumbbells, two phases shown (starting and end)

**Why most central antagonist exercise:** The shoulder-press motion is almost exactly opposite that of pulling up while climbing — therefore, no exercise is more central to antagonist-muscle training.

**Protocol:**
1. Sit on a bench with good upright posture and feet flat on the floor
2. Begin with bent arms, palms facing forward, and the dumbbells positioned just outside your shoulders
3. Press straight upward with your palms maintaining a forward-facing position
4. As your arms become straight, squeeze your hands slightly inward until the dumbbells touch end-to-end
5. Lower the dumbbells to the starting position
6. The complete repetition should take about 2 seconds
7. Strive for smooth, consistent motion throughout the entire set
8. Continue this motion for 15 to 20 repetitions
9. Rest for 3 minutes and perform a second set

**Weight progression:**
- Women should start with 5-pound dumbbells and advance to 10- or 15-pounders when they can do 20 reps
- Most men can begin training with 15- or 20-pound dumbbells and then progress to 25 and 30 pounds as they are able to achieve 25 repetitions
- Over the long term it's best not to progress beyond about 40% of your body weight (total weight lifted), since frequent use of heavier weights may build undesirable muscle bulk

---

### EX-PUSH-02: Bench Press (or Push-Ups)
**Category:** Antagonist push — multi-joint
**Target:** Pectorals, anterior deltoid, triceps; also useful for maintaining stable, healthy shoulders
**Equipment:** Olympic bar or dumbbells; bench — OR no equipment (push-ups)
**Photo:** [img-24, img-25] — lying on bench, two phases shown (starting and end)

**Key principle:** Use only moderate resistance — begin with a total weight equal to about 30% of your body weight and progress up to about 75% (no need to go higher). For example, a 160-pound climber would begin training with two 25-pound dumbbells (50 pounds total) and progress up to training with, at most, 50-pound dumbbells or 120 pounds with an Olympic bar.

**Protocol (bench press):**
1. Lie flat on a bench with bent legs and your feet flat on the floor
2. Using an Olympic bar or two dumbbells, begin the exercise with your hands just above chest level and palms facing your feet
3. If you're using a bar, your hands should be a few inches wider than your shoulders
4. Press straight up with a slow, steady motion
5. If using dumbbells, squeeze your hands together to touch the ends of the dumbbells together upon reaching the top position
6. Return to the starting position, pause for a moment, and then begin the next repetition
7. With a bar, be careful not to bounce the bar off your chest
8. The goal is slow, controlled movement that takes about 2 seconds per repetition
9. Continue for 15 to 20 repetitions
10. Rest for 3 minutes before performing a second set

**Push-up alternative:**
1. Begin with your hands shoulder width apart and build up to doing 2 sets of 25 repetitions
2. If necessary, move your hands closer together to increase training resistance
3. Conversely, beginners should do the push-ups with their knees on the floor until they are able to progress to the normal feet-on-floor position

---

### EX-PUSH-03: Dips
**Category:** Antagonist push — multi-joint
**Target:** Upper arms, shoulders, chest, and back — sport-specific benefit (dip motion is similar to the mantle move in climbing)
**Equipment:** Parallel bars, rings, Pump Rocks, or two heavy chairs; alternatively kitchen counter corner or gymnastics rings
**Photo:** [img-25] — parallel bars/rings, two phases shown (straight arm start, 90-degree bend)

**Why sport-specific:** The dip motion is quite similar to the mantle move in climbing and thus provides a very sport-specific benefit. A set of free-floating Pump Rocks or gymnastics rings are Hörst's personal favorite, as they provide a more dynamic (and difficult) workout.

**Protocol:**
1. Position yourself between the parallel bars, rings, or similar apparatus
2. Jump up into the straight-arm starting position with your hands drawn in near your hips
3. Slowly lower until your arm is bent 90 degrees — **do not lower beyond this point!**
4. Immediately press back up to the starting position
5. Continue this up-and-down motion, with each repetition taking about 2 seconds
6. Strive to complete 8 to 20 (hard) repetitions
7. Perform 2 or 3 sets with a 3-minute rest between each set
8. Don't rush or bounce through this exercise, and **never lower beyond a 90-degree arm bend**

**If unable to do at least 8 dips:** enlist a spotter to reduce the resistance as needed so that you can reach this goal. The spotter should stand behind you and lift around your waist or, more easily, pull up on your ankles (bend your legs and cross them at the ankles to facilitate this).

## 7. Chapter Scheduling Summary

**Daily (15–30 min):** A selection of SMR foam rolling exercises + upper-body and lower-body stretches. Make this a daily habit — either as part of pre-climbing warm-up, a before-bed routine, or rest-day flexibility training.

**Twice per week (mandatory):** All stabilizer and antagonist exercises (wrist stabilizers, rotator cuff, scapular stabilizers, and push exercises). The harder a person trains and climbs, the more indispensable these exercises become. Minimum: 1 set light resistance as part of warm-up + 2 sets heavier as part of workout.

**Pre-climbing warm-up integration:**
1. 5–15 min general warm-up (jogging, jumping jacks, easy climbing)
2. Foam rolling (selected areas)
3. Dynamic warm-up (arm circles, finger curls)
4. Light static stretching (but **avoid excessive forearm flexor stretching** — reduces grip strength up to 1 hour)
5. 1 light set of wrist stabilizer exercises (EX-ANT-01 through EX-ANT-03)

**Rest-day flexibility focus:** Deeper static stretching of all areas, especially lower body (hips, groin, piriformis, hip flexors). This is the time for longer holds and more gain-oriented stretching of the legs and hips.
