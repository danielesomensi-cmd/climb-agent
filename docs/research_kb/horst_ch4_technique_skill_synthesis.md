## 1. Core Premise

Hörst's opening argument: moving over stone is the essence of climbing, and therefore no subject can be more central to improving your climbing than training skill and developing technique. Yet strength training tends to get all the attention and hype.

**Key claim:** Mental training and improving skill and strategy come before the subject of strength training because most climbers can benefit greatly from focused training in these areas. If you want to put yourself on the fast track to the higher grades, strive to understand and apply the information in this chapter as much as or more than any other chapter in the book.

**The irony of practice:** The majority of climbers spend little, if any, time on dedicated practice of the vast spectrum of techniques inherent to our sport. Most climbers unknowingly service their lust for ticking routes by constantly climbing for performance. Of course, becoming proficient requires focused practice of new skills — yet some climbers just don't want to spend time practicing on routes below their maximum grade.

## 2. Motor Learning Theory

### 2.1 Types of Skills

**Skill** = the capability to bring about an end result with maximum certainty and minimum time and energy. Climbing skills possess both motor and cognitive components.

Two important distinctions:
- **Discrete skills**: single movement with a definite beginning and end — a mantle, lunge, high step, down pull, lieback, deadpoint
- **Serial skills**: string many discrete skills together → complex skilled action. Like a gymnast performing a routine, a climber must successfully execute specific moves but also possess the skill to link all moves into a complete ascent

**Implication:** For a redpoint ascent, you can't just practice individual moves — it's equally important to practice connecting the moves, since linking is a new skill in and of itself.

### 2.2 Skill Transference

Skill transference = how practice of a skill in one activity carries over to enhance performance of another. Research and anecdotal evidence confirm that transference is either **small or completely absent**, even between seemingly similar activities.

**Critical implications:**
- Practice at crack climbing will improve crack climbing skill but NOT face climbing ability
- Engaging in any type of "cross-training" activity (Hacky Sack, snowboarding, slack-lining) is a waste of time for the purpose of improving climbing skill (though these may develop cognitive attributes like focus)
- You must climb to improve climbing

### 2.3 Sensory Information Sources

**EXTEROCEPTION** — sensory information from outside the body:
- Vision is the primary source for physical activities like climbing — provides data needed to discover holds and unlock sequences, plus wealth of less conscious input on spatial and temporal aspects of movements
- Touch: provides essential data on rock texture and quality of contact with hands and feet — every moment on a climb, your conscious or preconscious mind monitors this tactile input, enabling subtle adjustments of grip and foot position
- Hearing: provides novel environmental data (warning of approaching rockfall; confirming that a carabiner gate has closed)

**PROPRIOCEPTION** — internal sense of body position and movement in space (also called kinesthesis):
- No matter what you do physically, proprioception provides the brain with a high bandwidth of sensory data from the nerves in all muscles, tendons, and joints, as well as the vestibular apparatus of the inner ear
- This vast amount of sensory feedback from limbs and inner ear is processed **unconsciously** in simple tasks (walking, pull-ups, easy climbing)
- More complex tasks require **conscious attention** to proprioception — the awareness and diligent use of this information separates master climbers from the mass of climbers

**Proprioceptive cue awareness varies on a continuum:**
- Beginners: coarse, limited — may sense basic quality of a foot placement, whether in balance, how pumped they are getting
- Elite: exquisitely subtle and well-defined — recognize a steady stream of valuable movement cues from the body's internal sense organs

**Key proprioceptive practice habit:** When practicing a new skill or working a move on a hard boulder problem, ask yourself: *How does it feel when I do it the right way (most efficiently) compared with when I do it the wrong way?* Making this distinction empowers you to detect flawed execution and make corrective adjustments on the fly.

**For crux moves:** Many falls off crux moves that you have rehearsed and seemingly wired — or off easier moves when on-sighting — are the result of poor attention to proprioceptive cues. **Bottom line:** The more subtle the level of proprioception you can perceive, the better you will be able to climb given your current skill level and physical abilities.

## 3. Three Stages of Motor Learning

Motor learning = the process by which we acquire physical skills. Learning occurs in three identifiable and overlapping stages.

### 3.1 Cognitive Stage

- Involves a lot of thinking about technique and moves, along with repeated trial-and-error attempts to execute novel moves
- More **visual than feel-oriented** — helps to be shown a new skill and then verbally guided through execution
- Early attempts are clumsy, inefficient, jerky — expending energy and strength in wasteful ways
- This is normal for first few weeks/months as a climber, as well as when you make first attempts at a new type of climbing (crack, slab, pocketed face) or on a route that's especially hard for you

**Coach input:** Most valuable in this stage. Coach can demonstrate new skills and proper ways of movement, provide verbal cues to guide proper posture, body positioning, tactics, and problem solving. Feedback should be positive and highly specific, yet not overwhelming in volume — focus on correcting major flaws, not tweaking minor technical details.

**Critical warning:** Success by way of thrashing up a climb is a failure when it comes to learning — it wires bad habits of movement into the brain, thus developing bad technique rather than good technique.

**Two keys to effective cognitive-stage learning:**
1. Let go of judgments relating to poor performance (view errors as clues for improvement)
2. Don't rush or overwhelm yourself by trying many different types of climbing during initial sessions — strive to develop good technique by repeating moves and entire routes until you can climb them smoothly and efficiently

### 3.2 Motor Stage (Associative Stage)

- Less a product of self-conscious effort and thought than one of automatic increases in efficiency and organization of the activity by the nervous system and brain as a response to continued practice
- The brain wires new neural associations as a result of regular practice of a class of skills and multiple attempts at a specific movement
- The climber exhibits more **fluid, coordinated, and economical movement** — energy expenditure decreases; natural momentum of the body and limbs is used to advantage
- **Hallmark:** Marked increase in economy of movement

**Goal in this stage:** Moves must be done efficiently with keyboard to spare, not eked out in desperation. Early, crude success should NOT be accepted as "good enough" — having demanding goals has been shown experimentally to produce both better performance and faster gains. The goal should be to perfect movement and dominate at a certain grade, not just get by at it.

**Coach input:** Far less than during the cognitive stage. The climber must be allowed to self-diagnose errors and make a first attempt at correction. **Two important keys to rapid learning in the motor stage:**
1. Broadening the skill set through exposure to new types of moves and climbs
2. Extensive use of proprioceptive cues to increase awareness and feel of technically correct movement

The climber should be encouraged to talk through (and even write down) what they feel when performing specific moves. Coach may need to draw out this information by asking: "How does the move feel when you do it correctly versus when you do it poorly?"

### 3.3 Autonomous Stage

- The final stage — many climbing moves are automatic and require very little conscious attention
- Movement has reached a stable and polished form — you can often do other things while in this state (carry on a conversation, decipher and send a moderate route in perfect form, on-sight)
- This apparent ease of execution is one aspect of the flow state, or "zone"
- **In climbing, reached only through dedicated, disciplined, long-term practice**
- Usually takes more than ten years to reach a consistent autonomous stage of mastery
- Even such elite climbers always have room for learning and refining skills — the very best embrace a lifelong, ever-learning mind-set

**Coach input here:** Focus on questioning the climber about how they feel, with the goal of enhancing awareness of lingering (often very subtle) technical and mental constraints on performance, and helping develop an effective long-term training strategy.

## 4. Motor Programs and Novel Moves

### 4.1 Motor Programs

Discrete climbing skills are directed by **motor programs** wired into long-term memory. These motor programs:
- Define and shape movement
- Become more stable, elaborate, and long lasting as you progress through the three stages of motor learning
- With consistent, quality practice: become highly precise and largely unconscious, freeing attention for other matters such as finding the next handhold or remembering the sequence

**Highly skilled climber:** Can look at a section of rock, see the moves, and know what skills will be needed. He might view a short boulder problem, see a lieback move that leads to a backstep move, followed by a two-foot deadpoint move to a good hold — the motor programs for "lieback," "backstep," and "deadpoint" yield a sort of movement script directing essential details of movement.

**Less skilled climber:** May lack the experience and cognitive skills to "see" the necessary moves; may possess less refined motor programs; will need numerous attempts to feel out the moves and gather proprioceptive feedback.

### 4.2 Schema Theory and Novel Moves

The hallmark of an expert climber = ability to on-sight climb at a high level on a wide variety of terrain and rock types — executing novel moves with a high rate of success.

**How novel moves are executed:** The climber hasn't performed a deadpoint from a backstep position to a hold exactly 24 inches away before. However, if they have thrown deadpoint moves from several different distances and from different body positions and rock angles, they can execute the novel move on-sight. All different deadpoint moves call the same "deadpoint" motor program into use, and the motor program is scaled to fit the novel situation according to a learned set of rules, called a **schema**.

**Schema** = a set of rules, developed and applied unconsciously by the central nervous system, that enables you to adjust a motor program for different environmental conditions (hold location, rock angle, friction properties) by changing parameters of muscle force, body position, and speed of movement.

**Practical implication (crucial for climbing development):** Upon learning a new climbing skill, practice it in cracks of different sizes, on different wall angles, and on rock with different frictional properties. This expands your use of the schema-rule to effectively ascend similar features at almost any crag. Conversely, climbing only a few cliffs and favoring a specific type of climbing = fewer motor programs and less refined schema-rules → these motor programs may not apply well at the outer limits of difficulty and will leave you climbing at a much lower grade or flailing on routes of the grade you're accustomed to sending at your home area.

### 4.3 Feedback Types

**EXTRINSIC FEEDBACK** — information provided by an outside source (belayer, coach, videotape) that augments internal sensory feedback:
- Inherent part of cognitive-stage learning
- As you progress to intermediate level (motor stage): accept and ask for a lesser amount of extrinsic feedback
- Advanced climbers need very little extrinsic feedback in daily climbing and training activities
- Most common form: **beta** — highly specific instruction on the proper sequence for a given climb
  - Extremely helpful in developing hold recognition and sequencing skills during the formative stage
  - Beyond the cognitive performance stage, continued use of beta will HANDICAP the development of problem-solving skills and other advanced cognitive abilities
  - Long-term reliance on beta leads to a false sense of ability — your climbing performance will degrade significantly when beta is not available
  - **Bottom line:** Beyond first few months as a climber, make it a practice to shut off beta from your partner; the exception is those rare cases when beta is essential for safety or speed

**INTRINSIC FEEDBACK** — sensory information via your five senses and proprioception gleaned from muscles, tendons, joints, and the vestibular apparatus:
- Most obvious: what you visually observe as the gross results of your efforts (falling off a move, seeing that a hold is not what you expected it to be, discovering that a sequence you visualized won't work)
- Subtler: the intrinsic feedback of "feel" (proprioception) — beginners lack the subtle senses of touch, body position, and levels of muscular tension
- As quality of movement improves (the motor stage) and you gain greater control over fear and excessive muscular tension, the subtler proprioceptive cues begin to reveal themselves
- **Growing your abilities demands** that you learn to utilize the high bandwidth of internal feedback in place of extrinsic feedback in self-analyzing and self-correcting movement error

**Beneficial practice:** Talk through and write down proprioceptive cues you discover for specific types of moves and crux sequences of project routes — this will foster uncommon sensory awareness, accelerate motor learning, and put you on the fast track to the higher grades.

### 4.4 Rate of Improvement and Ultimate Skill Level

**Law of Practice:** Performance improves rapidly from its baseline level when the activity is first practiced and continues to improve in gradually decreasing amounts as ability approaches some ultimate (personal) skill level.

**For climbing:** The learning curve also rises rapidly as a result of initial practice sessions. Because of the high complexity and wide range of skills inherent to the sport, you can continue to improve for many years, even decades.

**Specialist vs. generalist trade-off:**
- Focusing practice and training in one type of climbing (e.g., gymnastic bouldering or overhanging crimpfest) = you can quickly progress to a high level in that specific style, since only a small subset of total climbing skills is required
- The trade-off: limited motor programs will be a handicap when attempting routes outside your area of specialization
- **Bottom line:** Becoming a proficient, all-around climber is a long-term proposition. The most intelligent approach for training beginners = emphasize **learning skills over getting wickedly strong during your first few years** in the sport. Developing refined schema-rules for the many different climbing skills will take many years of climbing at a wide range of areas and rock types.

## 5. Practice Strategies to Accelerate Learning

**Universal prerequisite for learning a new skill:** Must not try to get around learning it by relying on your strengths in other areas. The more a new move feels at first, the easier it will be to execute — and perhaps even intuitive — as the result of intelligent practice.

**Key principle:** Learning new skills is difficult in states of fatigue, stress, fear, and urgency — a fresh, relaxed, and confident approach yields rapid acquisition of new skills. Consequently: practice new skills **early in your training sessions** and employ liberal hangdogging for blocked practice of crux moves. Interestingly, you can solidify skills you already possess by practicing them in a fatigued state.

### 5.1 Modeling Advanced Climbers

Best used in a climbing gym where you can observe the movements, positions, techniques, and tactics of a more advanced climber and then immediately give them a try on your own. Make a mental picture of what you want to attempt; use that vision as a starting point; experiment, modify, and make the move your own.

**Also model at the crags:** Note the tactics and strategy used by high-end climbers — how do they work crux sequences? Where do they find rests? At what pace do they climb? How do they go about equipping routes?

**Important warning:** Although modeling technical skills is a powerful weapon, copying an elite climber's strength and power training program is usually a big mistake. Elite climbers have spent years conditioning their muscles and tendons to withstand extreme levels of stress — to train as they do without this long-term preparation could be disastrous.

### 5.2 Blocked Practice

Identical repetitions of a specific move = the most popular method of practicing a hard climbing move because it produces rapid learning of the skill in that specific situation.

**The critical limitation:** Upon development of "feel" and early success at a new skill, a radical change is needed. Further blocked practice will have little value and may even result in a false sense of confidence and poor use of the skill in novel settings. **Beyond the first few successful trials of a new skill, blocked practice is for blockheads** — you must graduate to variable and random practice.

### 5.3 Variable Practice

The tried and proven way to achieve instant and proficient use of the skill in any new situation you come upon.

**Example:** After learning the drop-knee move on a vertical indoor wall with large, positive holds → change the "route" conditions slightly and attempt the same drop-knee move again → modify the route by changing the hold spacing and wall angle to further expand use of the skill → continue this progression to the point that you could perform the drop-knee move in a variety of random settings. Such variable practice will refine schema-rules to direct effective execution of the drop-knee motor program over a wide range of conditions (angle, hold size, rock type, frictional properties).

### 5.4 Fatigued Skill Practice

Beyond the initial successful trials of a skill, practice should be performed with variable conditions and levels of fatigue and **never again "blocked."**

**Session structure:**
1. First 30 minutes: train new skills while fresh
2. Move on to chalking up some mileage on a variety of routes
3. After an hour or so, when moderately fatigued: attempt several reps of recently acquired moves and sequences
4. As fatigue increases: finish up with some reps of sequences or boulder problems you have more completely mastered

**Key principle:** In a two-hour gym workout, this rule emphasizes the benefit of squeezing in a greater volume of climbing "practice" ascents over doing just a few "performance" ascents with extensive rest between. The long rests and performance climbing may make you look better, but the greater volume of practice will make you climb better.

### 5.5 Random Skill Practice

The ability to on-sight a sequence of novel moves on "foreign" rock is the ultimate goal of skill practice time. The best work-out approach is a randomized free-for-all of skill types — widely used in other sports, should not be overlooked by climbers as optimal training for the unknown.

**Two approaches:**
1. On an indoor wall: attempt to link a sequence of very different bouldering moves — contrive an unusual sequence of moves that will call a wide range of skills into use; make several attempts at sending it
2. Team up with a partner for a round of the **Stick Game** — take turns pointing (with a broomstick or laser pointer) each other through an unusual sequence of widely varied skills and movements

**Another random practice method:** Climb a series of widely differing routes in rapid succession. A commercial gym with many different wall angles, a few cracks, and a roof is ideal. Team with a partner and toprope ten to fifteen routes of different character over the course of an hour or so. The first route may be a vertical face, the next a slab, the third a finger crack, the fourth an overhanging pumpfest, the fifth a hand crack, the sixth a roof route, and so on. Rapid recall of a wide range of motor and cognitive skills is like taking skill-fortifying steroids!

### 5.6 Ten Tips for Rapid Learning of Skills and Developing Good Technique

1. Engage in regular climbing "practice" — go climbing with the intention of learning new skills and improving quality of movement, with little regard for absolute difficulty. Climb on as many different types of rock, wall angles, and areas as possible to build diverse skills and true climbing expertise
2. Practice new skills and techniques early in the session while you are physically and mentally fresh
3. Use blocked practice to accelerate learning during the initial trials of a new move, skill, or sequence. After two or three successful repetitions, cease blocked practice in favor of variable and randomized practice
4. Employ variable practice to expand command of newly acquired skills over a wide range of conditions (angle, hold size, rock type, and so forth). Vary the "route" conditions more than you expect would be the case in a real-life climbing situation
5. Practice known skills while in varying states of fatigue to increase your mastery of them and to build long-term retention
6. Use random practice — climb a random series of moves back-to-back to enhance proficiency of serial skill performance; another random practice strategy is to climb several very different routes back-to-back to mandate recall of many different motor programs
7. Model the techniques and tactics of advanced climbers. Stretch your imagination for climbing movement and strategy by observing and mimicking the techniques and tactics of top climbers
8. Aspire to dominate at a climbing grade, and not just get by. Focus practice on routes at or just below your maximum difficulty, and resist the urge to constantly work routes beyond your ability level
9. When working a route, resolve to find the best way to do a move or sequence, and resist the urge to just thrash up the route and deem that acceptable. As a practice method, climb a route several times to identify and learn the most effective and efficient moves and tactics
10. Maintain a long-term perspective toward learning to climb. No matter how fast you improve or how hard you climb, realize that you can still improve technique and learn new skills — even after ten, twenty, or more years!

### 6.1 Precise Foot Placements That Carry Your Weight

**The first fundamental:** Given that your legs are stronger than your arms, the legs should do the majority of the work. Exceptions: severely overhanging routes, which demand greater use of the arms.

**Two aspects of sound footwork:**

1. **Spotting the foothold and positioning your feet on the best part of each hold**
   - Directing foot placement demands attention to detail beyond that given to hand placements
   - Whereas handholds are easy to inspect, the greater eye-to-foot distance commonly leads to less-than-ideal foot placements
   - Furthermore, feet don't provide the same degree of feel as the hands → quality of each foot placement more difficult to assess
   - **Developing good footwork is an attribute you must make happen via constant foot focus and practice**

2. **Proper alignment of center of mass directly over a foothold**
   - Balance, stability, and application of force are optimized when CoM is positioned directly over your feet, forming a line perpendicular to level ground
   - On less-than-vertical wall or slab: requires a hip position out from the wall and over the foothold — keep your body position straight and your legs as much as possible over your feet
   - On near-vertical surface: simply keep your body position straight and over feet as much as possible
   - When the wall overhangs: impossible to position weight over feet → the Twist Lock technique takes over

### 6.2 Grip Handholds Lightly and Let Arms Play a Secondary Role

In a sport where anxiety and fear often rule, it's understandable that many climbers hang on with their hands for dear life → overgripping of handholds and unnecessary muscling of moves → rapid fatigue, pumped forearms, eventual need to hang on the rope.

**The practice:** Make each hand contact a conscious process. Consider where the best place is to grab the hold — not always on the top. Focus on using a light touch that yields soft forearms. Your goal: try to use each hold with a light touch, then increase gripping force only as much as required for the move at hand. This process of minimally gripping each handhold takes just a split second, yet it is a master skill that separates the best from the rest. With practice it will actually become an unconscious act as you cruise up hard routes with high efficiency.

**Arms and pulling:**
- Think of your arms as points of contact that simply prevent you from falling backward off the wall — arms maintain balance while the legs drive movement
- On a slab or near-vertical: let the leg muscles carry the load; arms mainly provide balance
- On a ladder: legs do all the work; arms mainly provide balance
- Exception: pull quickly through strenuous moves in order to get back onto good footholds that can carry your weight
- **Final tip for strenuous situations:** Maintain straight arms as much as possible — this way, much of your weight is supported by the skeletal system of your upper body and not by your muscles

### 6.3 The Left-Right Rule for Stable Movement

Maximum stability and ease of movement comes from the pairing of a left hand and right foot (or a left foot and right hand) in harmonious action.

**Ladder analogy:** Ascending a ladder with opposing hand-foot combinations (left hand pulling, right foot pushing at the same time) is so intuitive it's almost impossible to climb a ladder any other way. Using non-opposing combinations would immediately begin to barndoor or rotate sideways off the ladder.

**On climbing walls:** Much more complex — position and shape of hand- and footholds wreak havoc with your intuitive sense of movement. Therefore: in seeking to reposition your hands and feet on the wall, it's helpful to ponder which holds will provide the best opportunity for a left-right combination. Easy climbs often provide a pulling right hand that can combine with a pushing left foot (or vice versa). More difficult climbs tend to be more devious — advanced climbers will often be able to intuit the best way to proceed; beginners must be willing to try difficult sequences multiple ways to discover and learn the best solution.

### 6.4 Move with High Economy

**Technical paramount:** Climb with **high economy** — the mantra for every time you touch rock. High economy = discovering the way to do each move, and an entire route, with minimal energy expenditure.

**Catlike movement** is your technical model: smooth, quiet, leg-driven movements, with an unhesitating shift to arm-pulling, dynamic movement when it is required to most efficiently execute a difficult move.

**Five attributes of economical movement:**

**QUIET FEET**
- Feet that regularly pop off footholds or skid on the wall surface = typical of lackluster footwork and poor economy
- For many climbers, noisy footwork is just the way they climb — a habit developed over a long period and a flaw that will prevent them from ever reaching their true potential
- Goal: learn to climb with good foot technique even in the toughest times — concentrate on each foot placement, keep your foot steady and firm to the hold, and stand up on the foot with confidence as you proceed smoothly to the next hold or foothold

**RHYTHM AND MOMENTUM**
- Climbing should have a natural rhythm that utilizes momentum and inertia
- Ladderlike rhythm = step, reach, step, reach → good for easy terrain
- Better rhythm for effective movement: step, step, reach, reach → allows legs to direct and drive the movement
- Many other useful rhythms: step, step, reach, reach and step, step, reach and step, reach, reach
- Every unique sequence possesses a best rhythm of movement — you'll eventually learn to intuit this on the fly
- **Momentum:** Use it to help propel successive moves. Especially important on difficult climbs with large spacing between holds — smooth, continuous motion is critical when climbing through crux sequences. Many people do the opposite (engage crux sequences with measure and caution) — not only losing upward movement but also having the inertia of stillness to overcome

**SMOOTH MOVES AND RELAXED BODY**
- Smooth, fluid movement = another sign of high economy — a climber appears to flow up a route when only the muscles necessary for stability and upward motion are contracting and all other opposing (antagonist) muscles remain relatively passive
- Climbers wrought with anxiety, fear, or the need to perform find themselves contracting most all their muscles head to toe in an attempt to hang on or fight through a crux → tension in antagonist muscles forces prime movers to work harder → inefficient movements → rapid energy expenditure
- **Two-pronged approach to smooth movement:**
  1. Think about contracting only the muscles necessary for engaging the rock, maintaining stability, and directing movement (forearms, shoulders, core, thighs, calves)
  2. Switch focus to antagonist muscles and scan for unnecessary tension — while taking deep, slow breaths, visualize this tension escaping like air from a balloon → return focus to executing the next climbing movement → alternate focus back and forth between directing movement and directing relaxation

**PACE**
- Pace becomes increasingly important as a route gains in steepness and difficulty
- When climbing near your limit: intention must be to move as briskly as possible without any drop-off in technique (skidding feet, botching sequences)
- Reduce pace at the first sign your technique is suffering
- Identify obvious rest positions ahead of time, then make it a goal to move from one to the next as fast as possible
- Knowing just the right pace on a given route is a sense you will develop with experience

**STEADY BREATHING**
- Steady breathing is essential to sustain the life in your climbing — restricted breathing is akin to tightening your muscles, constraining your thinking, and fostering failure
- Many climbers have a tendency to shift into shallow, rapid breathing as fatigue and mental anxiety grow; worse yet, some climbers unknowingly hold their breath at times of high stress
- **Pre-climb breathing protocol:** Before every climb, take a moment to close your eyes and take several slow, deep breaths. Feel your belly expand outward as you slowly inhale, then allow the air to escape sparingly through pursed lips in a slow ten-second count. Visualize a wave of relaxation washing across your body — this sets the stage for optimal performance
- **Mid-climb:** Use every rest position as an opportunity to reset your breathing cycle with a few slow, deep belly breaths — like topping off your gas tank, do it frequently and you'll rarely hit empty

### 6.5 Optimize Use of Rest Positions

Finding efficient rest positions is as important as finding the best way to do a crux sequence. If you miss a good rest stance, you miss an opportunity to physically recover as well as to mentally "read" and prepare for the next section of the climb.

**Rest position search protocol:**
1. Begin with on-ground visualization of the route (Ch.3) to estimate the location and body position of rest stops
2. Upon reaching a rest stop: assume a body position that will allow the most fatigued muscles to rest (usually forearms, biceps, and calves)
3. An optimal rest position: feet in rest-step position, legs straight, hips over the legs, or in a position midway between the feet (if on holds more than shoulder width apart)
4. On a climb less than vertical: your upper body can relax completely, and in some cases you might even be able to assume a no-hands rest position

**On vertical to overhanging climbs:**
- Weighting the feet more difficult — still want to place as much weight as possible on the footholds, but a significant amount of weight will remain on your arms
- Absolutely vital: hang with **straight arms**, so that bones are providing the support, not the muscles of the upper arm
- Also advantageous: if you can obtain a heel hook or knee bar from which you can hang a high percentage of body weight — forearm muscles will need to contract very little to maintain a grip on the handhold
- Best strategy: attain a stable stance and shake out alternate arms every five to ten seconds → both arms take turns resting
- When hanging at the rest begins to cost more energy than you can recover: time to begin climbing toward the next rest spot

**Key insight:** The best climbers are not always the strongest — their prowess instead comes from uncommon mastery of climbing economy and effective resting.

### 6.6 Clever Use of Opposing Forces

More difficult climbs demand you consider all the possible arm positions (side pull, Gaston, undercling) and figure out how to match one of these with an opposing foot placement.

**SIDE-PULL ARM AND OUTSIDE EDGE OF OPPOSITE FOOT**
- Staple move on almost every moderate to advanced climb
- Foothold selection often determines the effectiveness of this move
- In most cases: best to use the outside edge of the opposing foot, not the inside edge
- The key: rotate your hips so that the hip opposite the pulling hand is turned into the wall — face and chest rotate toward the side pulling hand
- This stable position allows you to step up high with the free hand and quite possibly use your free (non-opposing) hand as well
- **Struggling to reach a handhold?** Try using the outside edge of your shoe and a hip turn to maximize reach

**GASTON AND INSIDE EDGE OF OPPOSITE FOOT**
- The Gaston (aka reverse side pull) is the most unnatural and weak arm position, yet fairly common move needed to unlock many crux sequences
- Best opposed by the inside edge of the opposite foot
- Combining a Gaston with an outside-edging foot is strenuous but doable if absolutely needed
- Practice in a variety of ways to gain comfort and strength in its use

**UNDERCLING AND INSIDE OR OUTSIDE EDGE OF OPPOSITE FOOT**
- Frequently overlooked by less-experienced climbers; often essential for unlocking difficult sequences on steep terrain
- An underclinging hand helps maximize your reach with the free hand and positions your arm and body in a naturally strong position
- Typically undercling a hold somewhere near your torso while you press with an opposing foot
- This foot can edge with either the inside, outside, or toe portion of the shoe; the outside edge is best for maximizing your reach
- Remember: edging with the outside of your foot → best to turn the hip opposite the pulling hand to the wall
- In extreme situations: may need to use a foot smear to oppose the under-clinging hand
- A very powerful but important move to train on a bouldering or System Wall

**SIDE-PULLING LEFT AND RIGHT HANDS**
- Use of opposing handholds is a key move for unlocking a sequence that lacks any usable down-pull or underclinging handholds
- Most common: two opposing side pulls that you'll draw inward to create tension through arms, shoulders, and upper body
- Ideally: upgrade the foot that opposes the better of the two side pulls → sets up a stable left-right combination → enables you to release the other side-pulling hand to upgrade it to the next hold

### 6.7 Use Foot Flagging to Enhance Stability

Flagging = alternative technique for maintaining stability when a left-right hand-foot combination is not possible. When releasing your free hand to make a reach upward, you will immediately begin to barndoor (sideways rotation) — this is hard to fight and often results in a fall.

A simple flagging of the free leg significantly improves stability and balance by shifting center of mass more directly over the supporting foot and under the supporting hand.

**Two methods of flagging** (choice depends on whether supporting leg is edging on the inside or outside edge of the shoe):
- Example: edging on the inside of right foot pulling with right hand → to avoid barndooring, flag the left leg across the right leg
- If supporting leg edging with the outside edge: best to flag the free leg out left along the wall surface in an effort to find a balance point

### 6.8 Mantling and Hand-Foot Matching

**Mantling** = move often called upon to overcome a long reach between holds. May be able to press your entire palm onto the hold or just your fingertips.

**The left-right combination** of pushing and pulling hands provides great stability → able to upgrade one or possibly both feet.

In many cases: match a foot (same side as the mantling hand) on the same hold that you are mantling → often possible to shift center of mass over that foot and stand up.

**Severe climbs:** Frequently demand that you adroitly match your foot onto a tiny crimp or finger pocket hold — quick, precise execution essential to maintain forward momentum through such tenuous sequences.

### 6.9 Twist Lock and Backstep on Steep Terrain

The twist lock and backstep are the bread-and-butter moves of a steep-wall connoisseur. As a climbing wall tilts back past vertical:
- Increasingly difficult to place a high percentage of weight on your legs
- Greater portion of body weight must be supported by the arms
- **Twist lock and backstep together:** Draw your body in toward the surface of the overhanging wall → changes the force vector on the handholds, making them feel more positive and secure → draws-in of the body places more weight onto the footholds

**Proper execution requires practice and significant core strength.** (See Ch. 7 for exercises for strengthening these core muscles.)

**Use of Twist Lock:**
- Typically used to ease the upgrading of a hand on an overhanging section of wall
- Example: left hand on a good hold, want to reach up high with right hand → turn right hip to the wall before making the reach upward (proper positioning) → use outside portion of right foot on a hold somewhere below or in back of your body → feet press in unison while left arm pulls down and in toward your torso, creating the twist lock
- Finding just the right body position is the key → when you do, you'll notice a surprising lack of effort needed to reach up and acquire the next right hand-hold

**Learning note:** Initially limit practice to boulder problems that overhang about 20 degrees past vertical; as you acquire skill, expand use onto even steeper boulder problems and overhanging toprope climbs.

### 6.10 Creative Footwork: Heel Hooks, Toe Hooks, Knee Locks

**HEEL HOOK**
- Major go-to move for turning the lip of a roof, topping out on a boulder problem, or copping a quick shakeout while on steep terrain
- In these cases: hands likely on holds at or above face level; intend to place one heel on a hold off to the side roughly between hip and shoulder level
- **Which heel to use:** Depends on (1) availability of a decent-size hold on which to place your heel, and (2) location of the next hand-hold that you hope to acquire — best made with the hand on the same side as the heel hook
- Pull on the heel hook as if it were a third arm — often helps to think about pulling your heel toward your rear end; this will maximize use of the leg muscles and help shift center of mass toward the heel hook
- Meanwhile: other foot should inside-edge or smear on the wall to help contribute to the upward motion
- As you gain elevation: make a quick reach to the next handhold, then switch your heel hook into a standard step-down foot placement

**TOE HOOK**
- Foot move used mostly in pulling overhangs or in navigating roofs
- Simply hook as much of the top/laces side of your shoe as possible on a large protruding hold
- Sometimes: toe hook onto a hold with a bent leg, then straighten that leg as your hands move out the roof
- Ideally: one foot toe hooking while the other foot pushes off a nearby hold — opposing push-pull combination enhances foot purchase on the holds and lowers the chance that your feet will be swinging off the roof
- Strive to keep your arms and legs in the straight position as much as possible so that your body weight is being supported more by bones than by muscles
- Done properly: can navigate a surprisingly large roof, with the limiting factor being forearm endurance

**KNEE LOCK (AKA KNEE BAR)**
- A boon on overhanging walls and roofs with large protruding holds — if you know the technique, can find a position to exploit this "thank-God" move
- On a severely overhanging climb with no obvious rest positions: finding a knee lock may be your only hope for a rest
- Look for a place where you can step your toe onto a hold and then lock in your knee against a larger opposing hold — provides surprising purchase; allows you to drop one hand at a time to shake out and chalk up
- Occasionally: knee lock so solid you'll be able to cop a rare no-hands inverted rest

### 6.11 Dynamic Moves When They Offer Greater Economy

Throughout the chapter Hörst stresses climbing with maximum economy — the goal of climbing a move, sequence, and entire route in a way that requires minimal energy. In most cases the hallmark of economical climbing is smooth, relaxed movement that utilizes the feet and legs over the muscles of the upper body.

The opposite of static style is **dynamic or explosive movement** — certain moves and sequences demand dynamic movement to achieve maximum economy. In particular: vertical routes with tiny handholds, and overhanging routes with long reaches, often require dynamic movements.

**The key:** Know which moves are best attempted dynamically versus statically — a recognition skill that takes years to fully develop. When working a route, it's best to attempt a crux in both a static and dynamic way to determine which style yields the most economical passage; otherwise you'll never know if there was a more effective method.

**DEADPOINTS**
- Allows you to make a rapid hand upgrade to a position despite the fact you can't hang on to the wall statically with a single-hand contact point
- The "magic of the deadpoint" = exploiting the apparent weight-lessness (moment of stillness) at the peak of a small upward motion to facilitate the delicate upgrading of a hand from one small hold to another
- **Executing a deadpoint:** Initiate a small droop downward (or a release outward in the case of an overhanging wall) immediately followed by a firm drawing-inward of the handholds toward your torso → drawing-in of your body is akin to the basketball player's jump → there will be an instant when the motion peaks and you'll be able to flash your hand up to snag the next hold
- A well-executed deadpoint is calculated and controlled such that it flows naturally in perfect economy
- In extreme cases: may need to execute several deadpoint moves in a row to climb through a series of small handholds you could never hang on to for a static movement

**LUNGES (DYNOS)**
- A full-on leap for an out-of-reach hold — arms and legs explode in unison to propel body upward toward the next good hold
- Typically end in one of two ways: latch on to the hold and regain control of your body, or fail to catch the target hold and end up falling
- Best viewed as a **last-resort move** that you only pull out of the bag when nothing else appears to work — strenuous and stressful; has led to many shoulder injuries
- **Execution technique:**
  1. Lock your eyes on the target hold and visualize exactly how your hand will hit — and stick — the hold
  2. Look down and concentrate on maximizing an explosive launch off your four points of contact
  3. In many cases: "cock" your lunge by drooping or bouncing before you catapult upward
  4. As you go airborne: your eyes will naturally return to a pinpoint focus on the target hold
  5. **Now stick it!**
- **Common mistake:** Ideally would be best to lunge only when climbing statically would require more energy. In fact, a perfectly executed lunge in just the right situation is a classic example of climbing with high economy, despite the apparent burly nature of the move

### 6.12 Crack Climbing

Crack climbing involves techniques and tactics far different from the skills outlined above → requires completely novel motor programs and schema-rules.

**FINGER AND HAND JAMMING:**
- Just how your hands engage a crack depends on the size of the fissure
- Narrowest cracks: accept little more than the tips of your fingers — place jam with index finger on the bottom and elbow out to the side; when you pull on that arm, the elbow will rotate downward and produce a twisting of the fingers that anchors them into the crack
- Larger finger cracks (around an inch wide): fingers tend to slide down instead of jamming solidly → look for constrictions or bottlenecks along the crack; attempt to place jams in or just above these narrower spots
- **Hand jams** (one to three inches wide): execute in the thumb-up or thumb-down position. Vertical cracks often better climbed thumb-down; cracks that slant to the side may be more easily climbed with the lower hand jamming thumb-up and the high hand jamming thumb-down
- In finger jamming: scan hand cracks for constrictions or bottlenecks that will provide the most bombproof jams imaginable — cracks with little variance are more challenging; key is to cup your hand inside the crack to generate outward pressure and friction on the inside
- **Fist jamming** (optimal for about four inches wide): insert your hand with the palm facing into the crack and then make a fist — the width of your fist increases, making it stick like an oversize cork

**FOOT TECHNIQUES:**
- Two primary foot techniques: feet inside the crack, and feet on the rock face
- In climbing thin finger cracks: have little choice but to search for edges on the rock surface on which you can edge or smear
- Cracks wider than about one inch: exceedingly solid foot placements by means of **foot jamming** — turn your foot sideways so that the sole of the shoe is facing inward and the knee is bent outward, then insert as much of your foot as possible into the crack. Depending on crack size: limited to jamming just the toe portion (narrow hand cracks) or its entire front half (fist cracks). Once secured, your knee will naturally rotate back to center as you stand up on the foot

**LIEBACK TECHNIQUE:**
- Unique method of climbing cracks located in the vertex of a dihedral or corner
- Fairly strenuous: places your body in a sort of rowing position with arms pulling and legs pushing in a powerful opposition
- Feet smear on one wall of the corner while your fingers cling to the edge of the crack and arms remain as straight as possible
- Move upward by simply walking your feet up the wall and sliding (leapfrogging) your hands up the crack
- With practice: learn to position hands and feet to provide the greatest leverage at the lowest possible energy expenditure — moving quickly, as in all kinds of strenuous climbing, is fundamental to maximizing performance

**CRACK-CLIMBING STRATEGY:**
- Effective crack-climbing strategy is nearly identical to optimal face-climbing strategy — climb briskly and efficiently; utilize your legs as much as possible in generating locomotion; pause for long periods only at definitive rest stances
- In formative days of crack climbing: use the security of a toprope to experiment with different techniques and subtle variations of the basic jamming skills
- Don't be satisfied with just thrashing up a crack to the top — strive to learn the best way to do each move with the goal of climbing each crack in good style and with minimal energy burn
- As a practice method: climb single-pitch crack routes a few times in a row to refine your skills and learn the subtle finesse moves that will eventually make you a master crack climber

## 7. Smart Training Drills (Section beginning — to be completed with final 2 pages)

Having performed the technical evaluation in Table 4.1 and the self-assessment in Ch.2, you should now have a clear picture of your technical weaknesses. But knowledge is not enough — to improve you must act! **Dedicate a portion of two climbing sessions per week to improving your weaknesses** and you will become a better climber.

**The distinction between practicing versus performing:** Many climbers handicap themselves by constantly focusing on performance as they succumb to the natural tendency to climb as close to their limit as possible. Climbers with horrible footwork flailing repeatedly on a steep route so that they can eventually tick some impressively difficult route — while they may ultimately succeed at sending their "5.hard" climbs, they gain little in technical ability from this process, and in fact they further groove their bad habits and poor technique. Hörst calls such practices "stupid training."

**Intelligent training alternative:** Dedicate a block of practice time early in the workout when mentally and physically fresh. During this practice period or day (if at the crags), the goal is to seek out routes that will target their technical weaknesses. Since performance is not a goal, there is no hesitation to hang on the rope and experiment with moves, body positions, and sequences that feel awkward or...

### 7.1 Traverse Training

The traverse drill is performed on a bouldering wall or low traverse — carefully spotting each foot placement, concentrating on shifting your center of mass over the leading foot, relaxing your grip as much as possible, and learning to move quickly and confidently through thin, tenuous sequences. Strive to remain calm and relaxed at all times, and refocus on your feet anytime you sense you're losing control.

**Variations to increase intensity and benefit:**
- Do a complete traverse using only two fingers (index and middle, or middle and ring fingers) of each hand — forces you to maximize the weighting of your feet and is also an excellent way to increase your finger strength
- Challenge yourself to do a complete traverse using only open-hand finger positions (especially beneficial if you naturally favor the crimp grip)
- Creative elimination traverses: "side-pull only," "undercling only," or "cross-through only" traverses
- Beginner and intermediate climbers have much to gain from performing these drills on a regular basis

### 7.2 Toproping and Hangdogging

Toproping and hangdogging are the ideal formats for practicing difficult moves near your limit or when diving into unfamiliar terrain like pocket or crack climbing. A relaxed, low-stress environment is critical to rapid learning of new skills. Climbing on toprope or on lead, bolt-to-bolt, represents a low-stress setting where you can experiment with tricky, awkward moves without the risk of a serious fall and injury.

**When attempting a route that is continuously hard or with multiple cruxes:**
1. Break it down into smaller sections or chunks — reduces the mental burden by allowing you to view and solve the route in parts
2. Much like working a boulder problem: employ blocked practice to work a sequence repeatedly
3. Once a problem is solved and programmed to a high likelihood of success: move on and begin work on the next chunk
4. Upon solving all the chunks (and after a good rest): your next goal should be to combine chunks — e.g., on a route broken into four hard sections, try to link the top two chunks, then move down and link the top three chunks
5. Such incremental learning will wire you for a successful redpoint or toprope ascent after another rest or on your next outing or day at that area

### 7.3 The Stick Game (aka Send Me)

Great for learning to quickly assess and execute a novel, unknown move on-sight — a vital skill when on-sight climbing at the crag or in competition.

**Protocol:**
- Best played on an indoor wall
- Requires at least two players
- Players take turns pointing out (with a broomstick or laser pointer) impromptu boulder problems for the other to ascend
- Begin by identifying the starting hand- and footholds for the climber; then, as she pulls up on them, the course setter points to the next hold to be used
- Continue until the climber falls or the problem is done
- Commonly played with open feet — the climber can use any foothold she likes

### 7.4 First Touch

Great practice drill for would-be competition and on-sight climbers — though anyone can benefit.

**Protocol:**
- An indoor facility with a wide range of toprope routes is the ideal setting
- You must climb a route by using each handhold in the exact way that you first grab it — no readjusting or changing your grip after you first touch it
- By climbing many routes in this fashion (and obeying the guidelines completely): learn to examine holds more closely both from the ground and while climbing → increases the likelihood you'll use them optimally from the first touch
- On lead: this skill saves time and energy, both of which increase your odds of on-sighting a route

### 7.5 Tracking and Elimination

**Tracking:** Climb a route by tracking your feet on the exact same holds used by your hands — like climbing a ladder, where you press down on a rung with your right hand and make room to step on that same rung with your right foot. As a type of elimination drill, this will make a climb much harder than it would be if climbed with all the holds on the route. If you normally practice skill by climbing 5.10 or 5.11 routes, do this drill on routes in the range of 5.8 to 5.9.

**Creative elimination drills:**
- Begin eliminating certain hand- and footholds from routes you have ruthlessly wired — little to be gained from blocked practice of the same tired routes
- Eliminate the five biggest holds from the route, or limit yourself to grabbing the holds only as side pulls or underclinas, or with only two fingers — makes for good fun, and is an excellent way to enhance skill practice and overall ability

### 7.6 Downclimbing Routes

When leading or toproping indoors: downclimb as much of the route as possible rather than lowering off without trying. Benefits beyond the obvious one of doubling the pump:
- You become a more observant and focused climber on the way up — since you know you're going to downclimb, you know you're going to need that information
- Since poor footwork is a leading handicap for many climbers, downclimbing demands intense concentration on footwork
- At first you will find downclimbing difficult, awkward, and very pumpy — but that's the MO when first attempting anything new that's worthwhile
- As your hold recognition improves and you learn to relax and fluidly reverse the route: downclimbing a route often feels easier than sending it in the first place — because your eccentric (lowering) strength is greater than your concentric (pulling) strength, and because you lead with the feet while downclimbing, learning to maximally weight them and conserve energy
- **A killer drill — not to be overlooked by any serious climber**

### 7.7 Speed Training

When the rock gets steep and the moves hard, there's no more important weapon to have in your arsenal than being able to climb fast and precisely. **Climbing quickly is primarily a function of skill, not strength or power** — in fact, the less strength and endurance you possess, the more important this skill becomes.

**Protocol:**
- No benefit to climbing faster if you begin to botch sequences or if your technique degrades
- Practice speed climbing on routes you have completely wired and, likely, at a number grade or two below your personal best
- Climb several laps on the route (rest between attempts), each incrementally faster than the previous
- Attempt to climb about 10 percent faster on each successive lap, but back off the accelerator at the first sign that your technique is suffering
- Perform this drill a few times a week for several months

**Payoff:** You'll find yourself naturally moving faster when climbing on-sight or redpoint at the crags. This new skill alone could push your redpoint ability a full grade higher over the course of a single season — **a much greater gain than you'd achieve from strength training alone**.

## 8. Tips for Developing Superior Technique and Movement Skills (Chapter Final Summary)

1. **Hire a climbing coach** — obtain the objective evaluation and sage guidance of a climbing coach to get on the fast track to climbing excellence. Even just one session per month will be extremely beneficial
2. **Warm up properly** before every climbing session. A progressive warm-up reduces injury risk and modulates your neuromuscular system for optimal learning and performance. Spend twenty to thirty minutes on easy climbs and various warm-ups
3. **Embrace a practice perspective** and resolve to always have fun. Optimize learning of new skills by maintaining a curious, carefree disposition and having fun regardless of climbing outcomes
4. **Make it your mission to develop a high level of self-awareness.** Tune into your body's proprioceptive feedback and learn to really "feel" each move, including the quality of your contact with the holds, the location of your center of mass, and the various levels of tension or relaxation in your muscles. Use this information to discover the most efficient way to do each sequence
5. **Regularly experiment with new techniques and on new terrain.** Challenge yourself on a wide range of wall features to expand your skill set and develop confidence. Avoid always climbing known routes and training in the same ways
6. **Push yourself, but know when to call it a day.** Spend about one-third of your climbing time on routes beyond your limit to effectively stretch your boundaries. However, for the majority of your climbing, work on routes that allow for focused practice and improving your weaknesses
