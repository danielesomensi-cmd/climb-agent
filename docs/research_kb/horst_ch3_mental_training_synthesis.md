## 1. Core Premise

Hörst opens with the Güllich thesis: the brain is the most important muscle for climbing. Güllich — one of the strongest pullers of his era — opened grades of maximum difficulty primarily through mental and physical synergy, not strength alone. Poor mental control can instantly sabotage physical and technical abilities.

**Key framing:** All performance operates from the inside out. Beliefs, focus, fears, confidence, preparation, and problem-solving ability form the foundation from which you will either succeed or fail.

**Training claim:** Mental skills should be practiced with the same dedication and resolve as a strength program. Some climbers see near-instant breakthroughs; others need weeks of consistent practice to reach a critical value. This depends on current degree of tone or atrophy of their "mental muscle."

**Aggregate effect:** All mental skills are interlaced and produce a powerful synergy when used together — Hörst calls this using your **Mental Wings**.

## 2. Ten Mental Strategies ("Mental Wings")

### Strategy 1: Separate your self-image from your performance
- Tying self-image to climbing performance creates obsessive pressure to perform perfectly → intense anxiety, fear of failure → makes best performance difficult or impossible
- **Process orientation:** detach from outcome, focus only on things immediate to the act of climbing (warm-up, mental rehearsal, gear selection, move and sequence at hand)
- Accept route feedback without frustration or judgment; liberate yourself to try new things, take chances, fall
- Paradox: by not needing to climb well, you will climb better

### Strategy 2: Surround yourself with positive people
- Three options: climb alone, climb with upbeat/positive people, climb with cynical/negative people
- Negative people's aura adversely impacts climbing and enjoyment whether you recognize it or not
- Bottom line: climb with positive individuals or solo — both are rewarding

### Strategy 3: Stretch your comfort zone
- Goals must exceed current grasp; you must be willing to push beyond comfort zone both mentally and physically
- Means climbing onward despite mental and physical discomfort; challenging fears head-on; attempting what looks impossible based on past experience
- This process stretches abilities to new levels, redefines belief systems, reshapes personal vision of what is possible

### Strategy 4: Anticipate and proactively manage your risk
- Climbing has both physical risks (falls, injury) and mental risks (failure, criticism, embarrassment)
- MO: assess all possible risks before each climb; determine ways to reduce risk (belay differences, spotters, crash pads); anticipate how you will respond to new emerging risks as you climb
- For mental risks (fear of failure): see Strategy 1 (self-image detachment)

### Strategy 5: Fortify your confidence
- Self-confidence is based on self-image and thoughts minute by minute
- Pondering past failures, allowing self-demeaning self-talk, or dwelling on chance of falling → deflates confidence and sows seeds of failure
- **Pre-climb confidence protocol:** Take mental inventory of past successes; review and believe in your skills and strengths; acknowledge your preparation and investment in training
- Result: more energized and confident as you engage the rock

### Strategy 6: Use visualization to foster a peak performance zone ("The Zone")
- The zone = state where everything comes together for a perfect ascent, seemingly effortless and automatic
- The trick: create this state **on demand**, even in stressful conditions (heat of competition, before hard redpoint)
- **Two-phase visualization protocol:**
  1. Use disassociated visualization (on-TV perspective) to reenact the positive feelings of a past good performance in a similar situation — about 60 seconds; use all senses; make pictures crisp, bright; feel the emotion and feeling of success
  2. With positive emotions now internalized, begin associated mental rehearsal of the upcoming climb — feel the moves play out successfully; create a mental blueprint for action
- Open eyes; take on the route one move at a time

### Strategy 7: Use preclimb rituals to create an ideal performance state
- The minutes and moments before you climb form the foundation for performance (sand or stone?)
- Quality of foundation is influenced by quality of preclimb rituals: scoping route, visualizing sequence, preparing gear, warming up, even how you put on your shoes
- **Develop rituals from past experience:** What did you think or do before your best ascents? What did you eat/drink? How did you warm up? How long did you rest?
- Once rituals become tried and true: stick to them

### Strategy 8: Control stress and tension before they control you
- Tension kills performance — it is a physical manifestation of mental stress
- Results in: poor motor control (inefficient movement), unproductive emotions, quite often failure
- **Two in-climb tension management methods:**
  1. Direct thinking away from pressure-producing thoughts; focus only on the process of climbing; engage the route completely; stay in the moment
  2. Use rest positions to break from climbing and direct thoughts inward for a tension check → use the 6-step ANSWER Sequence (see §5 below)
- ANSWER Sequence clears tension and returns to center in less than one minute — perfect for every mid-route rest position

### Strategy 9: Engage in positive self-talk
- Inside our heads: a "critic" voice and a "doer" voice
- The doer voice compels action, keeps us positive, helps us perform effectively
- The critic voice: useful in a few situations (evaluating weaknesses, risk management) but otherwise mostly non-useful
- **Goal:** Doer's voice rules the mind ~95% of the time
- **Self-talk reframe technique:**
  - *"This route looks impossible"* → *"This route looks challenging"*
  - *"I feel nervous"* → *"I feel energized"*
  - *"I'll probably fall"* → *"I think I can do this, but if I fall it's okay because I'll get it next time"*
- Sever ties to negative, critical people — their communication pollutes thoughts and attitude

### Strategy 10: Love climbing, no matter what
- Resilience to criticism and bad results; unwavering belief that success will come with time and effort
- Real winners never dwell on setbacks or accept defeat
- Climbing is about the journey, not the summit — love the process unconditionally

## 3. Controlling Your Emotions

### 3.1 The Mind-Body-Emotion Triangle

Hörst's model (Figure 3.1): Mind (think) ↔ Body (act) ↔ Emotions (feel). Each node influences the others bidirectionally.

**What you feel → influences how you think and what you do. How you think and what you do → affects how you feel.**

Negative emotions cascade:
1. Climber leaves ground cautiously, looking apprehensive — trying not to make mistakes
2. As moves get harder, breathing becomes shallow and irregular
3. Negative emotional energy rises → increased muscular tension and mental stress
4. Building stress disrupts coordination, balance, footwork → movements become tight, mechanical, inefficient → begins to overgrip
5. Becomes afraid to fully commit; hangs out too long on marginal rests
6. Fight-or-flight triggered → adrenaline burst → may thrash through a few more moves; more commonly grabs rope or quickdraw
7. Death grip sets in, muscles flame out
8. Falls; hangs on rope engaging in critical thinking that further raises tide of negative emotions

**Key insight for climb-agent:** This cascade is highly recognizable in the Q30 self-assessment items (Ch.2): items 4 (anxious at crux), 8 (stall before crux), 10 (Elvis leg), 13 (excuses before climbing), 16 (grabs quickdraw) all map directly to steps in this cascade.

### 3.2 The Energy-Emotion Matrix

Four quadrants defined by two axes:
- Energy axis: Low (0) → High (10)
- Emotion axis: Negative (−5) → Positive (+5)

| Quadrant | Energy | Emotion | State |
|---------|--------|---------|-------|
| I | High | Negative | Fight-or-flight, tension, forced effort |
| II | High | Positive | **Peak performance zone** |
| III | Low | Negative | Depression, apathy, withdrawal |
| IV | Low | Positive | Calm, reflective — good for learning/tests |

**Climbing-specific guidance:**
- Almost always best to be in **Quadrant IV (low-energy, positive)** — creative thinking, learning, fine motor skills all demand a relaxed, positive state
- Exception: psyching up for a powerful boulder problem or short powerful sport route → **Quadrant II (high-energy, positive)** is ideal
- **Never Quadrant I** — high energy + negative emotion = tension, overgripping, death grip

**Practical tool:** Mentally score your current energy (0-10) and emotion (−5 to +5) → ask "What quadrant am I in?" → take action to move toward optimal quadrant for the task

### 3.3 Changing Your Emotional State

Using the mind-body-emotion bidirectional relationship:
- **Physical interventions:** Extended posture, crack a smile, pump fist in the air, deep belly breaths — these create rapid emotional changes
- **Mental interventions:** Replay great events or climbs from the past; think about accomplishments; ponder good health and fortune; visualize future goals that excite you

**Tips for Optimizing Emotions:**
1. Regularly tune into thoughts and self-talk — what quadrant are you in?
2. Supplant negative thinking with positive, productive thoughts — direct solution-oriented thinking
3. Use your body to change your state: roll shoulders back, take deep belly breaths, crack a smile
4. Leverage emotional resources by reviewing past successes — vividly relive past great climbs and life events, feel the positive emotions charge you up

## 4. Dealing with Fear

### 4.1 Reasonable vs. Unreasonable Fears

Hörst distinguishes explicitly:
- **Reasonable fears** — keep you alive: fear of groundfall without protection, objective dangers
- **Unreasonable fears** — derail performance: fear of falling on a well-protected route, fear of physical discomfort, fear of failure, fear of embarrassment

**Key insight:** It is the unreasonable fears that must be eliminated. Reasonable fears must be heeded and mitigated.

**Subconscious preprogrammed fears:** Root of many "dumb things" that just seem to happen — falling after the crux when the route is in the bag, botching a wired sequence when feeling in control. These may be the result of unchallenged inner fears, not lack of ability.

### 4.2 The Four Primary Climbing Fears

**Fear of Falling**
- Inherently, it is not really falling we fear but not knowing what the fall will be like
- This explains why the first fall is the scariest; subsequent falls are much less stressful
- **Progressive fall practice protocol:**
  - Find a steep climb with bomber protection
  - Use a good rope (double-check knot and buckle)
  - Start with two-foot falls → build to about twelve-footers (gear just below feet)
  - More experienced climbers: mentally replay past inconsequential falls during the preclimb warm-up; believe fully that falls on this climb will be no different (if that is indeed the case)

**Fear of Pain**
- Causes you to give up long before your body has reached its physical limitations
- The pain of climbing a continuously strenuous route is akin to running a mile at full speed — it freaking hurts
- Fortunately, the pain is brief; perseverance pays dividends
- Decision: push into the discomfort zone a bit further each time → pain threshold will be redefined, as will your limits on rock

**Fear of Failure**
- Deep-seated, often instilled during childhood — binary classification of everything as success or failure
- In climbing: attack becomes less aggressive than required → second-guessing sequences in the midst of doing them → breathing becomes shallow → paralysis by analysis
- **Three methods to eliminate fear of failure:**
  1. Focus on what is *probable* instead of what is *possible* — consider probable, realistic scenarios based on past experiences, not worst-case scenarios
  2. Focus all attention on the *process* of climbing, not the pondering of possible outcomes — concentrate on things immediate to your performance: precise foot placements, relaxing grip, moving quickly to next rest
  3. Adopt the attitude that *it's okay to fall* (safe fall) and *falling won't bother me, I'll just get back up and give it another go* — by willingly accepting this fate, you dissolve the fear of failure

**Fear of Embarrassment**
- Occasional bad-performance days are inevitable — accept them, analyze them, bury them
- With this attitude you will be free to try chancy moves and risk occasional mistakes
- In the long run, you'll often look like a hero; never like a zero if you attempt things boldly
- Better to embrace bold attempts and occasionally fail than to accept mediocrity

### 4.3 Tips for Managing Fear (Summary)
1. Analyze your fears — real or imagined? Take action to mitigate legitimate risks
2. Overcome imagined fears with reason — redirect thinking in productive ways; dismiss illusionary fears
3. Focus on the process; detach from possible outcomes — let the climb unfold one move at a time
4. Predetermine that you will accept failure should it happen — you are not defined by successes or failures; you are defined by how you react to success and failure
5. (References deeper treatment in *Maximum Climbing: Mental Training for Peak Performance*)

## 5. Relaxation Training and Centering

### 5.1 Progressive Relaxation

Origin: American physician Edmund Jacobson (1940s). By fully relaxing muscles, you relax the mind. Learned by deliberately tensing and relaxing specific muscle groups → sharpens awareness of tension levels; ability to release tension on demand.

**Progressive Relaxation Sequence (10-step protocol):**
1. Go to a quiet location; sit or lie in a comfortable position
2. Close eyes; take five deep belly breaths (inhale slowly through nose to count of 5; exhale through mouth to count of 8)
3. Tense muscles in right lower leg for 5 seconds; feel tension in foot and calf; let go and relax completely. Repeat with left lower leg. Think: *My feet and lower legs feel warm and light*
4. Same sequence in muscles of upper leg (one at a time). Think: *My upper legs feel warm and light. Feel all tension dissolve*
5. Repeat in hands and lower arms — make tight fist for 5 seconds, then relax completely. Think: *My hands and forearms feel warm and light*
6. Repeat procedure in muscles of upper arm
7. Shift focus to many muscles of torso (chest, abdominal, back, shoulder areas)
8. Conclude with muscles of face and neck
9. In deep relaxation state: mentally scan from head to toe for any isolated pockets of remaining tension — visualize the tension escaping like air from a balloon
10. Open eyes and return to work or climbing with renewed sense of calm and focus. Or use this relaxed state for visualization

**Practical application:** Best done at midday break, as part of a long rest at the crag, or last thing before falling asleep. Initially ~15 minutes; with practice, complete relaxation in under 5 minutes.

### 5.2 Differential Relaxation

Relaxing **only the muscles not needed for the task at hand**, while all other muscles remain engaged.

**Climbing application:**
- Especially useful while hanging out and chalking at rest positions on the rock
- Scan body for pockets of tension or contracting muscles not needed for upward motion or stability
- Take a few deep belly breaths; visualize tension draining from the muscle like air escaping from a balloon
- Commonly tight areas to scan: forearms (overgripping?), shoulders, upper back, chest, abdominals, calves

**Elite climbers:** The best climbers actively control undue tension as a largely unconscious process — move with grace and fluidity, maximize economy of motion and energy use. Like a Porsche getting the high "miles-per-gallon" of a Honda.

**Key mechanism:** Unwanted contraction of antagonist muscles or over-contraction of prime movers interferes with even simplest movements. Climbers who try too hard become extremely rigid and mechanical — pitting muscles against each other → stress, fatigue, premature failure.

### 5.3 The ANSWER Sequence

Six-step centering protocol for use at every mid-route rest position. Initially takes a few minutes; with practice, executable in about ten seconds.

| Letter | Full name | Action |
|--------|-----------|--------|
| **A** | Awareness of rising tension, anxiety, or negative thoughts | Turn thoughts away from outer world toward inner world; perform tension check; nip negative changes before they snowball out of control |
| **N** | Normalize breathing | Deep, relaxed, regular breathing throughout; exception: Valsalva Maneuver to create core tension on a hard move |
| **S** | Scan for specific areas of muscular tension | Quick sweep of all muscles to locate pockets of tightness; forearms (overgripping?), shoulders, upper back, chest, abdominals, calves; consciously contract and then relax any tight muscle; visualize tension draining like air from a balloon |
| **W** | Wave of relaxation | Single deep breath; feel a wave of relaxation wash from head to toes |
| **E** | Erase thoughts of past events and possible future outcomes; focus on the present | Let go of last failed attempt or difficult sequence just fought through; refocus on the present moment entirely |
| **R** | Reset posture and flash a smile | Generate positive energy through postural reset; flash a smile → leaves you in peak performance state and ready to climb into the zone |

**Centering:** When centered = balanced, relaxed, confident. Being out of center = feelings of imbalance, tension, awkward movement, growing stress and anxiety. ANSWER Sequence is the tool to return to center.

**Use:** Make it as regular and automatic as chalking up — at every rest position, every marginal hold, every stance. With practice, the mind learns to detect growing tension even as concentration is focused outward on the climbing task.

## 6. Visualization Training

### 6.1 Why Visualization Works

The brain is not always capable of distinguishing between something that actually happened and something that was vividly imagined (Kubistant 1986). Repeated visualization tricks the mind into thinking you've been there and done that before — creates a mental blueprint for future actions. Mental movies enhance climbing by:
- Hardwiring sequences (moves, body positions, "feel")
- Increasing memory
- Fortifying confidence

**Critical requirement:** Visualization must be as detailed and accurate as possible. Any bad coding (wrong moves) or fuzzy detail (uncertain sequences) may lead to a botched sequence or fall when you climb the route for real.

**Neurological basis (Feltz 1983):** Vivid, associated visualization has been shown to cause low-level neuromuscular activity that helps enhance motor learning and maintain the feel of performing a skill. Referenced in context of injury use case.

### 6.2 Two Types of Visualization

| Type | Perspective | Best used for |
|------|-------------|---------------|
| **Disassociated** | On-TV / observer's point of view | Reviewing past poor performance; route finding; imagining possible sequences; risk management; fall risk assessment |
| **Associated** | Through your own eyes | Preprogramming a future ascent; redpoint or onsight preparation; all aspects of doing the climb |

**Key rule:** When projecting positive future events, always use **associated** visualization. Switch to **disassociated** only when visualizing things that might go wrong (risk management) — from on-TV view, see what falls you could take and what risks might be involved.

### 6.3 Uses of Visualization by Context

**Preprogramming a redpoint ascent:**
1. Begin with disassociated visualization of your last attempt — see yourself; note what needs to be corrected or refined
2. Switch to associated: create a perfect mental movie of the ascent as seen through your eyes
3. Imagine all important aspects: crux moves, gear placements/clips, rest positions
4. Create the feel of doing the moves AND the feeling of being relaxed and centered as you move into the crux
5. Make the movie positive and perfect in every way; always conclude with the feeling of reaching the top

**Preparing for an on-sight ascent:**
- No firsthand experience → very hard to create accurate associated movie → spend most time in disassociated perspective
- After studying from below: create images/movie of yourself climbing from on-TV perspective; see yourself dropping gear at obvious placements; hanging at what appear to be good rests; visualize any hazards unique to this climb
- If enough detail can be seen from ground: create **two** movies of possible sequences through the crux → if one obviously doesn't work when you get there, call up the second and continue without delay
- Finish by moving into associated state to imagine what you might feel and see as you climb the sequence from the disassociated view

**Preparing for competition:**
- Many events allow only a brief preview period → create a "rough-cut" movie
- Include: basic route path, location of obvious rests, whatever can be gleaned about moves or sequences
- Even without route preview: take a mental picture of the wall and project yourself climbing with grace and confidence to the top
- Most important: strive to eliminate any self-defeating images that might cross your mind in the hours and minutes leading to your ascent

**Climbing injured or tired:**
- If laid up due to finger injury or out for the season: can still climb in your mind
- Vivid, associated visualization causes low-level neuromuscular activity that helps enhance motor learning and maintain the feel of performing a skill (Feltz 1983)
- Also useful when pumped out on a route: instead of thrashing for the umpteenth time and risking injury, call it a day → spend time doing mental laps to solidify sequence knowledge without physical strain

### 6.4 Tips for Enhancing Performance with Visualization
- Use disassociated (observer) perspective for route finding, imagining sequences, risk management, reviewing failed attempts
- Use associated (climber's perspective) to mentally climb the route — feel the ascent evolve, including all physical movements and thought processes — the goal is to mentally practice virtually every aspect of the climb
- Make associated visualization as bright and detailed as possible — avoid any negative visualization
- Never rush through visualization — engage in a quiet area; goal is to visualize the complete ascent from bottom to top with no interruptions

## 7. Creating Laserlike Focus

### 7.1 The Nature of Focus in Climbing

Focus in climbing = a laserlike concentration of mental energy aimed at the most important task at any particular instant. Since every movement in climbing possesses a different most-important task, you must be able to redirect focus in an instant to the finger or foot placement most critical at that moment.

**The zoom lens analogy:** At any given moment, zoom in on the single task most critical to performance — toeing down on a small pocket, pulling on a manky finger jam, shifting center of mass to just the right balance point. Think about nothing else and you may fall at this critical task.

**Key skill:** The difficult part is learning to zoom in and out quickly from pinpoint focus to a wide-angle perspective — zoom out when hanging looking for next hold; zoom in as you reach toward it and latch on.

### 7.2 The Singular Focus Drill

**Protocol:** When climbing a route a couple of grades below your maximum ability (toprope or lead, gym or crag):
1. Attempt to climb the whole route by focusing solely on **one aspect** of movement
2. Example: complete focus on just hand placements — find the best way to grab each hold, use minimum grip strength necessary, feel how purchase changes as you pull on the hold
3. Place as little focus as safely possible on feet, balance, belayer — allow your intuitive sense to determine where feet go and how balance should shift
4. When focus wanders: simply redirect it to the predetermined task
5. This process of becoming aware of lost focus and returning it to the critical task = sharpened awareness; tantamount to gaining control of focus

**Progression:** Repeat regularly but change the focus each time (foot placements, weight shifts). Work on increasing the length of time you can maintain a singular focus — this builds **mental endurance**. As you become more skilled: convert into a **dynamic focus drill** where your focus constantly shifts to the most critical task at any moment.

**Ultimate goal:** The process of directing and redirecting focus becomes largely subconscious. On the rare occasions when focus wanders away from climbing, your well-trained mind will instantly recognize this loss and redirect focus onto the climb. This leads to the highly desired **flow state**.

### 7.3 Pinpointing Your Focus for a Climb

**Pre-climb focusing exercise** (perform after preclimb ritual, after being put on belay):
1. Stand at base of climb; assume an extended posture (shoulders back); close eyes
2. Place fingertips of dominant hand against the rock face — touching lightly, NOT gripping
3. Hand and arm should be completely relaxed
4. Take three deep belly breaths: inhale through nose to count of 5; exhale through mouth to count of 10
5. Let a wave of relaxation wash across your body
6. Narrow focus to the tips of your fingers touching the rock — concentrate singly on the sensation of your fingertips touching the rock
7. Feel the thermal energy exchange between fingertips and rock
8. Maintain relaxed, singular focus on this energy exchange for 30 seconds to 2 minutes
9. If focus wanders: simply redirect it to your fingertips
10. Soon your mind will become completely still: all attention pinpointed on tips of fingers
11. On reaching this state: open eyes and begin climbing

### 7.4 Six Enemies of Concentration

| # | Enemy | Description | Antidote |
|---|-------|-------------|----------|
| 1 | **Focusing on mechanics of well-learned skills** | Skills you possess high competence in should be turned over to the preconscious mind; consciously focusing on them results in mechanical, lower-efficiency movement | On all but most cruxy/tricky moves, engage foot- and handholds properly and allow the mechanics of climbing to take care of themselves |
| 2 | **Dwelling on internal feelings and sensations of fatigue** | Must occasionally check internal conditions; but dwelling on fatigue and strain magnifies these feelings and opens the door to stopping thoughts | Dissociate from the fatigue and pain (non-injury-producing "good pain"); dismiss phantom stopping thoughts; this empowers you to transcend previous limitations |
| 3 | **Entertaining nonproductive self-talk** | Negative self-talk directs attention inward to fretful or fearful thoughts | Direct positive, productive self-talk at all times; it will help maintain focus, aid execution, and sustain motivation |
| 4 | **Focusing on the past** | Engaging the vertical extreme demands being fully in the moment; looking into the rearview mirror quickly derails performance | Reflect on past experiences only as briefly necessary for strategic planning and risk management |
| 5 | **Focusing on the future** | Projecting into the future and pondering a possible performance outcome thwarts task-relevant concentration; entertaining future-oriented thoughts of failure generates pressure and anxiety that makes the unwanted outcome more likely | Detach from outcome-oriented thinking and engage the moment completely |
| 6 | **Visual and auditory distractions** | ADD era = ubiquitous distractions; achieving a quiet in-the-moment mental state is exceedingly difficult | Systematically eliminate distractions before engaging the route; aspire to master blocking out distracting people and sounds as you climb; reduce non-task-relevant stimuli in all aspects of life |

## 8. Tips for Creating Focus (Chapter Summary)

Hörst's 5-point summary for developing focus:
1. Train focus by using the Singular Focus Drill while lead climbing in the gym or toproping outside
2. Develop a detailed preclimb ritual that includes warming up, scoping the route, analyzing risk, visualizing the ascent, preparing your gear, putting on your shoes, and tying in to the rope — use the same ritual before every climb to consistently create a high level of focus
3. Use the Pinpointing Your Focus for a Climb exercise as your final task before beginning up the route
4. Strive for process-oriented thought and avoid dwelling on past outcomes or what's ahead — stay in the moment
5. If focus is scattered: acknowledge this and redirect attention to the move or action at hand; consider using the Pinpointing Your Focus exercise again

**Hörst's personal preclimb ritual example:** Scope route to determine best path and figure out key moves and rest positions → a few minutes of mental rehearsal and visualization, feeling the moves and preprogramming the sequence → put on shoes and tie in → complete preclimb ritual by taking a few slow, deep breaths, straightening posture, and cracking a smile. Total time: 5–15 minutes depending on difficulty and length.

## 9. Mental Wings Strategies for Problem Solving and Learning

Six strategies specifically for deciphering complex routes and difficult crux sequences — being able to quickly decipher perplexing cruxes while conserving mental and physical energy is described as a master skill.

### Strategy 1: Focus on problem solving, not performance
- Working a hard boulder problem or project route is the challenge of studying a complex problem and gradually seeing a beautiful, unique sequence take form — like piecing together a puzzle
- Best solve a rock puzzle by remaining focused on the task and having fun regardless of how long the puzzle takes to complete
- When working a boulder problem or crux: ponder the beauty of the rock puzzle and feel the joy of being engaged in the challenging process → shifts you out of the must-not-fail mind-set of frustration → turns focus away from the problem and onto finding a solution
- **Key principle:** The brain naturally magnifies whatever you focus on — so always be solution-oriented

### Strategy 2: Relax and remain positive
- Both problem solving and motor learning occur most rapidly in a stress- and anxiety-free state
- Controlling tension through deep breathing, positive visualization, and remaining process-oriented is crucial for accelerating these processes
- Eliminate thoughts of needing to flash the route or redpoint this attempt — these positions create stress and anxiety that may prevent the very thing you desire
- Instead: acknowledge that falling is part of the learning process; accept each fall as providing a clue for success — predetermine that you will accept a fall (and find the clue) if it happens
- Believe that success will come with creativity, effort, and patience → creates the optimal state for learning and succeeding most quickly

### Strategy 3: Chunk down the route
- Breaking a long, hard route into a series of short problems makes the climb less overwhelming and easier to learn
- Each short problem or chunk = a short-term goal to be reached or accomplished
- While failing on the project as a whole for several days, you experience short-term success as you solve individual chunks → keeps you energized, positive, on track
- **Critical rule:** When working through individual chunks, avoid becoming obsessed on any one of them — beating yourself up on a single chunk is self-defeating (you'll be too mentally and physically wasted to put meaningful work on the others)
- Move on to the next chunk upon onset of frustration or any judgmental self-talk ("I can't do this" or "I'll never figure this out")
- Allow these thoughts to burrow into your subconscious — they form the basis for reality
- Far better to solve the rest of the route before returning to the problem section
- **Sequence:** Work the most difficult chunks first, then begin linking sections from the top down

### Strategy 4: Engage both sides of the brain
- Left hemisphere: logical, practical, language, mathematical, linear matters
- Right hemisphere: creative, artistic, intuitive, situational, imaginary matters
- Most people are "left-brained"; intense situations with lots of information — like climbing — tend to bring the left brain into command
- Result: many climbers leave their right brain on the ground → handicap their problem-solving ability (linear thinking, blinders on, prevents finding key holds, sequences, or rests)
- **How to access the right brain:** Be in a relaxed state — resist the Type A behavior of rushing up to a route and quickly going for the send; instead get comfortable at the base of the climb, warm up slowly, make a relaxed study of the climb before tying in
- Goal: balanced approach to problem solving — able to think logically and practically as well as intuitively and creatively
- This balanced state is a hallmark of climbers who can perform at an exceedingly high level in a wide range of styles and settings (examples: Lynn Hill, Chris Sharma)

### Strategy 5: Employ multisensory learning
- Everything we learn comes through one of our five senses — the more senses used in learning, the faster and easier it becomes
- Climbers tend to use primarily the sense of vision before they leave the ground, and feel once they begin to climb
- The auditory sense can also be a powerful learning tool (Knudson 1997) — particularly when faced with a tricky boulder problem or when working to memorize a difficult sequence
- **Verbal beta technique:** Climbers who give names to key holds and moves then create verbal beta are using this technique — e.g., "high-step to the credit card edge, then deadpoint to the potato chip flake"
- Remembering an obscure sequence of tiny holds by feel or vision can be enhanced with descriptive verbal beta
- **Practice:** Talk yourself through sequences in addition to visualizing and feeling the moves — by talking the talk, you make it easier to climb the climb

### Strategy 6: Try something ridiculous
- The biggest block to learning is judgment — self-talk like *"Others use this sequence, so that must be the best way"* or discounting a novel/improbable sequence that flashes into your mind without trying it first = self-sabotage
- Your brain doesn't know what you can or cannot do until you tell it — don't prejudge sequences or your capabilities
- The best problem solvers are both creative and uninhibited — they never hesitate to try a novel solution entirely different from the known sequence
- **Practice:** Ignore the obvious solution (the one not currently working for you); attempt a few completely different, even ridiculous sequences
- Regardless of how improbable a given technique looks (heel hook, undercling, high step, knee bar): don't pass judgment on it until you make a few attempts with that technique
- Try a variety of body positions, foot flags, and lunges; don't ignore less positive holds like Gastons, side pulls, and pinches
- Eliminate the seemingly must-use hold (or at least try it with the opposite hand)
- Search for unchalked holds that might unlock the sequence
- Keep a constant watch for footholds that are off the main line of the route — a single missed foothold or high step can make the difference between "impossible" and "possible"

**Cross-reference note:** Hörst explicitly states at the end of this section that the mental and technical aspects of climbing are intimately connected, and that Chapter 4 will provide an in-depth study of motor learning and performance (how you acquire skill), as well as a primer on fundamental climbing techniques and several powerful strategies for developing superior technique.

## 10. Problem-Solving Tips and Success Strategies (Chapter Final Summary)

1. Focus on problem solving, not performance — view each climb as a puzzle that you will enjoy and learn from, no matter what the outcome
2. Relax and remain confident — use the ANSWER Sequence to maintain a relaxed state, and direct positive, productive self-talk to fortify confidence and sustain effective action in the face of difficult climbing
3. Chunk down the route into manageable sections — work the most difficult chunks first, then begin linking the sections from the top down
4. Engage both sides of the brain — which side is more dominant for you? Strive to bring both creative, intuitive (right brain) and analytical, practical (left brain) power to work for you
5. Employ multisensory learning by seeing, feeling, and talking yourself through tough sequences
6. When all else fails, try something ridiculous — seemingly impossible cruxes often require a trick move or non-intuitive sequence, so experiment with a variety of moves and techniques, no matter how improbable or inappropriate they seem to be
