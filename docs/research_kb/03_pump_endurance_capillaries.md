## Executive Summary

The "pump" — the progressive loss of grip force during sustained climbing — is the most common performance limiter in route climbing. Understanding its physiology is essential for training prescription. The pump is NOT caused by "lactic acid buildup" (a persistent myth) but by **intracellular acidosis (H+ accumulation)**, **inorganic phosphate buildup**, and **blood flow occlusion** during isometric forearm contractions.

**Key physiological facts:**
- At >50% MVC, forearm blood flow is completely occluded (Hörst T4C p.178; Lacrux/Völker)
- Climbing contraction:relaxation ratios are extremely unfavorable: 6.3:1.5s in lead competitions, 7.9:0.6s in bouldering (Draga 2024)
- The anaerobic-aerobic crossover occurs at ~60-75 seconds of sustained high-intensity climbing (Hörst podcast #23)
- Aerobic metabolism contributes ~60% of energy during intermittent finger flexor tests (Maciejczyk 2021)
- Elite climbers show superior ability to extract oxygen from forearm muscles (higher SmO2 desaturation rate) — a TRAINABLE adaptation (Fryer, Baláš)
- ARC training (20-45 min continuous easy climbing) is the primary method for capillarization and mitochondrial density improvement

## 1. The Physiology of the Pump

### 1.1 What Actually Causes the Pump

**The myth:** "Lactic acid causes the pump."

**The reality (current science):**
- Lactate and H+ are 99% dissociated at physiological pH — there is no "lactic acid" in the muscle (Hörst podcast #23)
- Lactate is actually a FUEL, not a waste product — it can be shuttled to other tissues (heart, liver) and used for energy
- The actual culprits of fatigue are:
  1. **H+ accumulation** (protons) → intracellular acidosis → impairs enzymatic function → reduces force production
  2. **Inorganic phosphate (Pi)** — cleaved from ATP during energy release — now thought to be the primary cause of reduced force in sustained exercise (Lattice energy systems guide)
  3. **Blood flow occlusion** — isometric contractions at >20% MVC impair blood flow; at >50% MVC blood flow is completely stopped (Hörst T4C)
  4. **Impaired PCr resynthesis** — without blood flow, the alactic system cannot replenish during contractions

### 1.2 The Occlusion Problem — Climbing's Unique Challenge

Climbing is uniquely problematic because:
- Forearm muscles work **isometrically** (no lengthening/shortening) → no natural "muscle pump" to assist blood flow
- Contraction intensities are often >50% MVC on hard holds → complete vascular occlusion
- The contraction:relaxation ratio in climbing is extremely unfavorable (4:1 to 13:1 depending on discipline)
- Between moves, the brief relaxation period (~1.5s in lead, ~0.6s in bouldering) is often insufficient for full re-oxygenation

**Elite vs. sub-elite difference:** Elite climbers show faster re-oxygenation during rest phases (MacLeod et al. 2007) and deeper muscle oxygen desaturation capacity (Fryer et al., Baláš et al. 2022). This is a trained adaptation reflecting higher capillary density and mitochondrial function.

### 1.3 The Flash Pump

A "flash pump" occurs when climbing hard without adequate warmup:
- Cold muscles have reduced blood flow and lower enzyme activity
- Jumping to high intensity forces immediate reliance on anaerobic systems
- Massive H+ accumulation occurs before aerobic systems are primed
- **Hörst (podcast #26):** Intracellular acidosis negatively impacts mitochondrial function — a flash pump can impair same-day climbing performance for hours
- **Prevention:** 15-20 min progressive warmup (easy climbing → moderate → near-limit) primes all three energy systems

## 2. Energy Systems in Climbing

### 2.1 The Three Energy Systems

| System | Fuel | Duration | Intensity | Climbing Application | Byproducts |
|--------|------|----------|-----------|---------------------|------------|
| **ATP-PCr (Alactic)** | Phosphocreatine | 0-15s | Maximal | Single hard move, campus move, power slap | None significant |
| **Glycolytic (Lactic)** | Glucose/glycogen | 15s-2min | Near-maximal | Sustained crux sequence, boulder problem | H+, lactate, Pi |
| **Oxidative (Aerobic)** | Glycogen, fat, lactate | >2min | Submaximal | Route climbing, ARC, recovery between moves | CO2, H2O |

### 2.2 Energy System Contributions in Climbing-Specific Tests

From Maciejczyk et al. 2021 (*Frontiers in Physiology*):
- **30s all-out finger flexor test:** 62% alactic, 18% glycolytic, 19% aerobic
- **Continuous 60% MVC test:** 54% alactic, 18% glycolytic, 28% aerobic
- **Intermittent 60% MVC test:** 27% alactic, 13% glycolytic, **60% aerobic**

**Critical insight:** The intermittent test (most similar to actual climbing) is predominantly aerobic (60%). This confirms that aerobic capacity is THE most important energy system for sustained climbing.

### 2.3 The Anaerobic-Aerobic Crossover

Hörst (podcast #23-24) describes a crossover point:
- At ~45-75 seconds of sustained high-intensity climbing, the glycolytic system begins to fail
- The oxidative system becomes the primary ATP source
- For most climbers, this crossover occurs at 60-75 seconds
- Elite climbers (Ondra-level) likely have a faster aerobic takeover and higher aerobic power

**Training implication:**
- Short power endurance (15-30s): train with high-intensity bouldering circuits
- Intermediate PE (40-60s): train with linked sequences, 4×4
- Long PE (1-3min): train with sustained route climbing at RPE 8.5-9
- Aerobic power (>3min): train with ARC, route laps, density work

### 2.4 Hörst's Energy System Training Model

From podcasts #22-26 (Training for Climbing):

| Energy System | Training Method | Intensity (RPE) | Duration | Rest | Frequency |
|--------------|----------------|-----------------|----------|------|-----------|
| **ATP-PCr** | Campus moves, power slap, max hang | 9.5-10 | 3-7s | 3-5 min | 2-3x/week |
| **Glycolytic (short PE)** | 4×4 bouldering, linked sequences | 8.5-9 | 15-60s | 3-5 min | 2-3x/week |
| **Glycolytic (long PE)** | Sustained route climbing | 8.5-9 | 1-3 min | 5-10 min | 1-2x/week |
| **Aerobic power** | ARC, alactic-aerobic intervals | 6-7 | 20-60 min | Continuous | 2-4x/week |
| **Aerobic capacity** | Easy climbing, active recovery | 4-5 | 20-45 min | Continuous | 2-6x/week |

**Key Hörst warning:** "Chronic acidosis (too much anaerobic lactic training per week) can decrease aerobic power and contribute to overtraining syndrome." Balance is essential.

## 3. The Aerobic Base — Capillaries and Mitochondria

### 3.1 Capillarization

Capillaries are the microscopic vessels delivering O2 and removing metabolites from muscle fibers.

**Why it matters for climbing:**
- Higher capillary density = faster O2 delivery = faster recovery between moves
- Higher capillary density = better metabolite clearance = delayed pump onset
- Elite climbers have demonstrably superior forearm capillary networks (NIRS studies)

**How to train it:**
- **ARC training** is the primary stimulus for angiogenesis (new capillary formation)
- Low intensity (30% MVC, RPE 4-6), long duration (20-45 min continuous)
- Must stay below anaerobic threshold — "talk test" or nose-only breathing
- The intensity must allow blood flow to continue (no complete occlusion)
- Progress by increasing duration, NOT intensity

### 3.2 Mitochondrial Density

Mitochondria are the "power plants" of aerobic metabolism inside muscle cells.

**Why it matters:**
- More mitochondria = greater aerobic ATP production at any given intensity
- More mitochondria = faster PCr resynthesis during rest phases between moves
- More mitochondria = ability to use lactate as fuel (lactate shuttle)

**How to train it:**
- Both ARC (low intensity, long duration) and high-intensity intervals stimulate mitochondrial biogenesis
- ARC builds "volume" (more mitochondria overall)
- High-intensity work builds "quality" (more efficient mitochondria)
- Both are needed; ARC forms the base, PE sharpens it

### 3.3 ARC Training — The Protocol

**ARC = Aerobic Restoration and Capillarity Training**

| Parameter | Guideline | Source |
|-----------|----------|--------|
| Duration | 20-45 min continuous climbing | Hörst, Bechtel, Climbing Doctor |
| Intensity | RPE 4-6, "talk test" pass, nose breathing OK | All sources agree |
| Grade | 3-4 number grades below onsight max (Hörst podcast #26) | Hörst |
| Pump level | Light/mild only — NOT a deep pump | Climbing Doctor, Hörst |
| Frequency | 2-4x/week (can do up to 6x due to low fatigue) | Bechtel: "3x is plenty, could do 6x" |
| Progression | Increase duration (20→30→40→45 min), not intensity | All sources |
| Method | Continuous laps, traverse, auto-belay, treadwall | Various |
| Phase | Primarily Base phase; maintenance in other phases | Hörst 4-3-2-1 |

**Common mistakes with ARC:**
1. Going too hard — "pump lust" (Hörst) — if you get a deep pump, it's too hard
2. Skipping it — most climbers skip the base phase because ARC is "boring"
3. Replacing it with 4×4 — 4×4 is glycolytic/PE training, NOT aerobic
4. Not progressing duration — staying at 20 min for weeks without extending

### 3.4 Recovery Between Moves — The Re-oxygenation Window

The brief rest between moves (shaking out, clipping, resting on good holds) is where the aerobic system does its critical work:
- PCr begins to resynthesize (50% in ~30s, 95% in ~3min — Hörst 7-53 rationale)
- Blood flow resumes when grip is released → O2 delivery, metabolite removal
- Elite climbers re-oxygenate faster (MacLeod 2007: re-oxygenation rate predicts endurance performance)
- **G-Tox technique** (Hörst): alternating arm position above/below heart while shaking, 5-10s each position, for 2 minutes → 18.4% increase in grip strength recovery (Climbing Doctor)

## 4. NIRS Research — Measuring Muscle Oxygenation

### 4.1 What NIRS Tells Us

Near-Infrared Spectroscopy measures:
- **SmO2** (muscle oxygen saturation) — how much O2 is available in the muscle
- **tHb** (total hemoglobin) — proxy for blood volume in the tissue
- **O2Hb / HHb** — oxygenated vs deoxygenated hemoglobin

### 4.2 Key NIRS Findings for Climbing

| Study | Key Finding |
|-------|------------|
| Fryer et al. 2016 | Forearm muscle oxidative capacity (measured by NIRS) predicts climbing performance |
| MacLeod et al. 2007 | Faster re-oxygenation during rest phases predicts endurance performance |
| Baláš et al. 2022 | Critical angle (climbing equivalent of critical power) correlates with muscle oxygenation breakpoint |
| Winkler et al. 2023 | NIRS parameters predicted 26.4% of intermittent finger endurance variance |
| Hermans et al. 2021 | HIT climbing causes significant SmO2 reduction; 24h insufficient for full SmO2 recovery |
| Feldmann et al. 2023 | SmO2 dynamics in elite climbers during finger-hang: SmO2min is consistent across load conditions |

**Practical takeaway:** SmO2min (the lowest oxygen level your muscles reach during sustained contraction) is an indicator of oxidative capacity. Elite climbers can push their SmO2 lower (better O2 extraction) and recover faster. These are trainable adaptations.

### 4.3 The Critical Force Model

The critical power/critical force concept applied to climbing:
- **Critical Force (CF):** The highest sustainable force output indefinitely (boundary between steady-state and non-steady-state)
- Above CF → fatigue accumulates, eventual failure in finite time
- Below CF → sustainable indefinitely (theoretically)
- **CF/BW ratio** is a climbing-specific endurance metric (currently used in climb-agent assessment)

**Baláš et al. 2024 validation:**
- 4-min all-out test on finger flexors validated for CF determination
- CF measured at 20.1 ± 5.7 kg in their sample
- End-force at 16.8 ± 5.2 kg (suggesting mean end-force may underestimate true CF)
- At CF intensity, mean time to failure was 440 ± 140s (not indefinitely sustainable as theory predicts)
- NIRS data confirmed steady-state oxygenation at sub-CF intensities

## 5. Training Protocols for Endurance

### 5.1 Protocol Hierarchy (from base to peak)

| Phase | Primary Method | Secondary | Duration | Goal |
|-------|---------------|-----------|----------|------|
| **Base** | ARC training (20-45 min) | Easy route laps | 4 weeks | Capillarization, mitochondrial density |
| **Build** | Density hangs (Nelson) + moderate route climbing | Repeaters 7/3 | 3-4 weeks | Local muscular endurance, aerobic power |
| **PE** | 4×4 bouldering, linked sequences, route intervals | Alactic-aerobic intervals | 3-4 weeks | Glycolytic capacity, lactate tolerance |
| **Peak** | Redpoint attempts, project climbing | Maintain ARC 1-2x/week | 2-3 weeks | Convert fitness to performance |

### 5.2 The 4×4 Protocol

The classic power endurance method:
- Choose 4 boulder problems (progressively easier: hardest first, easiest last)
- Climb all 4 back-to-back with minimal rest between (only walk to next problem)
- Rest 4-5 min between sets
- 2-4 sets per session
- Target: RPE 8.5-9 by end of each set, significant pump
- Hörst variation: "Choose problems with lots of moves" for sustained effort

### 5.3 Route Intervals

For lead climbing endurance:
- Climb a route section (20-40 moves) at near-onsight difficulty
- Rest 3-5 min (shake, breathe)
- Repeat 3-5 times
- Progress by increasing moves per interval or decreasing rest

### 5.4 Alactic-Aerobic Intervals (Advanced/Elite)

From Hörst podcast #26:
- Short bursts of maximal effort (5-10s bouldering moves) followed by easy climbing (30-60s)
- Total session: 15-30 min
- Target: train the aerobic system to rapidly clear metabolites after alactic efforts
- NOT for beginners — requires high base strength and power
- Best done on treadwall or system board for controlled intensity

## 6. Implications for climb-agent

### 6.1 Current Endurance Axis Validation

The endurance axis measures:
- Repeater test (7/3 × 6) → proxy for local muscular endurance
- Critical Force test → boundary of sustainable climbing intensity
- Route grade endurance → self-reported ability on sustained routes

**This is well-supported by the literature.** The intermittent finger flexor test format (like 7/3 repeaters) has the highest aerobic contribution (~60%, Maciejczyk 2021) and correlates well with climbing endurance.

### 6.2 ARC Training in the Engine

**Current implementation:** ARC sessions exist in the catalog for Base phase.

**Validation:** Correct placement. ARC is universally recommended for Base phase (Hörst, Bechtel, Lattice, Climbing Doctor). The protocol parameters (20-45 min, easy grade, no deep pump) are consistent with the science.

**Improvement opportunity:** The engine could prescribe **progressive ARC duration** (week 1: 2×20min, week 2: 2×25min, week 3: 2×30min) rather than fixed duration. This matches the progression model from the literature.

### 6.3 Energy System Phase Mapping

| climb-agent Phase | Primary Energy System | Training Focus |
|------------------|----------------------|----------------|
| Base | Aerobic capacity + aerobic power | ARC, easy climbing, density hangs |
| Strength & Power | ATP-PCr (alactic) | Max hangs, limit bouldering, campus |
| Power Endurance | Glycolytic + aerobic power | 4×4, route intervals, repeaters |
| Performance | All (competition simulation) | Project attempts, route rehearsal |
| Deload | Aerobic capacity (recovery) | Easy climbing, active recovery |

This maps well to the current Hörst 4-3-2-1 framework in the engine.
