## 1. Core Philosophy

**Impact estimate:** Most climbers can realize a 10 to 20 percent improvement in training, recovery, concentration, energy, and overall climbing performance if they dedicate themselves to improved dietary surveillance.

**Simplifying principle:** Performance nutrition is not that complex — if you make sensible food choices, eat moderately, and avoid excessive amounts of sugar, alcohol, and unhealthy fats, then you'll be eating more healthfully than most people.

**Hörst's personal testimony:** Credits sound nutrition for his ability to train ~48 weeks per year, avoid injury, recover quickly, and still climb at a high level in his fifties despite decades of climbing abuse to joints, tendons, and muscles.

## 2. Macronutrients

### 2.1 Protein

**Function:** Building and repairing tissue, immune system component, enzyme production — facilitates every reaction in the body.

**Daily requirements for climbers:** 1.2 to 1.5 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day. For a 72 kg (160 lb) individual, this translates to 86–108 g/day. This is higher than the FDA's 0.8–1.0 g/kg recommendation for sedentary adults, but well below the 2.0–3.0 g/kg advocated by some. Hörst (and most nutritionists) reject the massive protein-intake guidelines — successful training is much less a factor of consuming a lot of protein than of using the appropriate training strategy and eating enough carbohydrates to fuel training.

**Best protein sources:** Low-fat dairy (skim milk, yogurt), grilled chicken, fish, lean red meats. Whey protein powder mixed into skim milk, juice, or water is an excellent high-quality source. Incomplete proteins (plant-based) are also useful when eaten in combination — especially important for vegetarian athletes, who tend to be more protein-deficient.

### 2.2 Fat

**Function:** Essential fatty acids for cell membranes, hormone production, phospholipids. Dietary fat deficiency in female athletes has been shown to cause amenorrhea and may affect bone tissue maintenance.

**Daily requirements:** Minimum 15–25 g/day. As percentage of total calories: **15–30%** depending on climbing preference.
- **Cragging/bouldering** (anaerobic, low percentage of body fat desirable): restrict fat to **15–20%** of total calories
- **Alpine climbing** (endurance, higher total calorie demand): up to **30%** of calories from fat. Fat is more calorie-dense than carbohydrate and protein (9 cal/g vs 4 cal/g), and it's a good fuel for long, slow aerobic activities.

**Four types of fat:**
1. **Saturated fats** — animal products (milk, dairy, meats, poultry), some nuts (Brazil, macadamia). Excessive intake increases LDL cholesterol. A certain amount is needed for hormones and phospholipids.
2. **Monounsaturated fatty acids** — vegetable oils (canola, olive, peanut), avocado. Best for lowering LDL without reducing HDL (the "good" cholesterol).
3. **Polyunsaturated fatty acids** — fish (tuna, mackerel, salmon, trout), corn, sunflower, soybean oils. Omega-3 "polys" investigated for anti-inflammatory properties (arthritis, migraine, heart disease).
4. **Trans fatty acids** — hydrogenated oils (margarine, shortening, many processed foods). Produce effects similar to saturated fats and may be cancer-causing. **Avoid these.** Found in breads, cookies, snack foods, fried foods, and surprisingly even some "health foods" and energy bars.

**Practical advice:** Consume roughly equal amounts of saturated, monounsaturated, and polyunsaturated fatty acids, and minimize trans fat intake. Eliminate hydrogenated oils from your diet. Read labels — you'll be surprised how many foods you eat daily contain trans fatty acids.

### 2.3 Carbohydrate

**Function:** The most efficient and effective source of energy for muscles and brain. Critical protein-sparing effect — if you do not consume enough carbohydrates to meet energy needs, muscle protein will be broken down for energy. **Consequently, popular low-carbohydrate diets (Atkins, Zone, Ketogenic) are inappropriate for most active climbers.**

**Two forms:** Sugars (fruit, sugar, soda pop, jam, honey, molasses) and starches (breads, rice, pasta, vegetables).

#### Glycemic Index (GI)

**Definition:** Measures how ingestion of a particular food affects blood sugar levels compared to straight glucose. High-GI foods → rapid blood sugar spike → insulin spike → energy crash. Low-GI foods → more subtle, sustained changes.

**Why it matters for climbers:** Stable insulin levels are optimal for long-duration stop-and-go activities such as all-day climbing and training sessions. A steady insulin curve promotes muscle growth, discourages fat storage, and prevents the "yawning and feeling strangely weak" pattern from blood sugar swings. **This makes low- to medium-GI foods preferable for climbers in most situations.**

**Counterintuitive GI findings:** Many foods classified as "complex carbohydrates" (cereal, candy, some fruit juices, potatoes, white rice, bread, bagels) are actually high-GI foods. Low-GI foods include vegetables, whole grains, brown rice, and milk.

**Factors affecting GI:** More processed/easily digestible food → higher GI. Liquids → higher GI than solid form. High-fiber foods → slow insulin response, lower GI. Foods containing protein and fat → relatively low GI.

**The one exception — post-exercise window:** The one good time to consume high-GI foods (juice, soda pop, Gatorade) is immediately at the end of your workout or day of climbing. Intense exercise primes the muscles to immediately reload energy reserves as glycogen. **The optimal window for high-GI foods is the first 2 hours following exercise.** After that, favor low- to medium-GI foods for slow, steady refueling. (See Chapter 12 for more recovery strategies.)

**Daily carbohydrate requirements:** Should account for nearly **two-thirds** of daily calorie intake (~2/3 of plate = pasta, rice, potatoes, vegetables; ~1/3 = lean protein). Pair carbs with protein when snacking (e.g., bagel with skim milk, fruit with yogurt, peanut butter) — protein slows digestion and creates longer-lasting energy.

**Calculation by body weight:**
- Training 2 hours/day: ~7 g carbohydrate per kg body weight (e.g., 72 kg person → 500 g carbs → 2,000 cal from carbs)
- Full day strenuous climbing: 10–14 g/kg body weight

## 3. Water and Hydration

**Critical importance:** Water may be the most important nutrient to get right when climbing. Many climbers are chronically dehydrated.

**Performance impact (Schoffstall 2001):**
- 1–2% drop in water → loss of concentration, enhanced fatigue
- 3% drop → headaches, cramping, dizziness
- Just 1.5% drop in body weight from dehydration → statistically significant drop in maximum strength

**Injury connection:** Dehydration increases chance of joint or tendon injury. Proper hydration facilitates nutrient transport to cells, protects tissues from injury, and maintains joint lubrication. **Maintaining proper hydration is as important as a proper warm-up** for the purpose of injury prevention.

**Hydration recommendations:**
1. Pre-hydrate ("camel up"): drink 2 cups (~16 oz / 500 ml) of water before heading to the gym or crag
2. During activity: minimum 8 oz (240 ml) every hour or two throughout the day
3. Normal conditions: total 1–2 quarts over an 8-hour period (bare minimum — on a cold day with little perspiration)
4. Hot/humid conditions: roughly double → 3–4 quarts of water for a hot 8-hour climbing day
5. **Most climbers massively underhydrate** — carrying insufficient water to the crags detracts from climbing ability and increases injury risk

## 4. Optimal Macronutrient Ratio

**Climbing-style dependent:**

| Climbing Style | Carb : Protein : Fat | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| **Bouldering / Cragging** (high-intensity, stop-and-go) | **65 : 15 : 20** | Anaerobic demands, lower total calorie count |
| **Alpine / Long route** (long, slow distance) | **55 : 15 : 30** | Higher total calorie demand, fat as slow-burn fuel |

---

## 5. Diet and Nutritional Tips (Summary)

1. Strive for a **65:15:20** caloric ratio of carbohydrate, protein, and fat in your overall daily diet
2. Consume **1.2 to 1.5 grams of protein per kilogram** of body weight per day to facilitate maximum recovery and muscular regeneration
3. **Limit fat consumption to mono- and polyunsaturated fats** from vegetable and fish oils. Avoid saturated and trans fats, fried foods, and pre-packaged snack products
4. Consume **several small carbohydrate snacks** throughout the day to provide sustained energy, positive mood, and clear thinking. Favor **low- to mid-GI** foods
5. Drink an **8-ounce serving of water every hour or two** throughout the day — and twice this amount on hot days

## 6. Micronutrients

**Key finding:** As many as two-thirds of self-selected diets contain less than the recommended daily allowances (RDA) of certain vital vitamins and minerals. RDAs may represent low-end requirements for serious athletes.

**Recommendation:** Take a daily multivitamin — a remarkably cheap nutritional investment (~$15 for 3 months).

**Minerals of special concern for climbers:**
- **Magnesium and zinc:** Often consumed in less than recommended amounts. Several studies show statistically significant increases in muscular strength with supplemental Mg + Zn (ZMA supplement, Brilla 1998). Food sources: spinach, nuts, beans, lentils, whole grains, low-fat dairy (Mg); seafood, beef, cashews, pork, chicken (Zn).
- **Calcium and iron:** Vegetarians often fall short on iron. A multivitamin is the best way to obtain extra iron (absorption enhanced by vitamin C). Women may need extra calcium (supplement or 2–3 glasses of skim milk daily).

## 7. Sports Supplements

**Hörst's overarching view:** Up to 90% of sports supplements on the market are nothing more than modern-day snake oil. The handful of products that could potentially enhance training response and climbing performance are reviewed below.

### 7.1 Protein Powder

**Best form:** Whey protein — highest Biological Value (BV) of any protein source.

**Table 11.4 — Biological Values of Protein Sources:**

| Food | BV |
|------|-----|
| Whey | 104 |
| Whole egg | 100 |
| Milk | 91 |
| Egg white | 88 |
| Fish | 83 |
| Beef | 80 |
| Chicken | 79 |
| Soy | 74 |
| Beans | 49 |
| Peanuts | 43 |

**Practical advice:** Look for a blend of "whey isolate" and "whey concentrate" — highest quality. Costs ~$0.60–0.90 per 20 g serving. Ideal first thing in the morning and immediately after workout/climbing. Skim milk is also excellent (10 g protein per glass; a quart per day provides 40 g toward your total).

### 7.2 Sports Drinks

**Two main categories of active ingredients:** Electrolytes and fuel (carbohydrates).

**Electrolytes:** Potassium, magnesium, calcium, sodium, chloride — critical for concentration, energy production, nerve transmission, and muscle contraction. Electrolyte loss during climbing is quite slow, so a reasonable diet + multivitamin provides all you need for ordinary training or a day of climbing. Sports drinks with electrolytes may benefit during limited food supply situations (big-wall, alpine climbing for multiple days).

**Fuel sources in sports drinks:** Mainly carbohydrates (glucose, sucrose, fructose, maltodextrin, lactates).

**What to avoid:** Any drink whose first ingredient (after water) is glucose or high-fructose corn syrup — these have a high GI and will release sugar rapidly into the blood, causing a spike-and-crash pattern. In stop-and-go sports like climbing, this can leave you feeling "flat" and lethargic.

**What to choose:** Drinks that list **fructose** (not HFCS) or a **protein source** (such as whey) as the first or second ingredient. These provide slower, more sustained carbohydrate release ideal for climbers. Good choices: Accelerade (Pacific Health Labs), PowerBar Performance Recovery.

**When to use sports drinks:**
- **Long days (>90 min):** A fructose-based or protein-containing sports drink throughout the day at the crags, plus food, is beneficial
- **Short sessions (1–2 hours bouldering/gym):** No benefit from a sports drink — water is sufficient
- **Post-exercise:** Switch to a high-GI drink to reload glycogen ASAP (optimal window = first 2 hours)

### 7.3 Energy Bars

**Problem:** Many energy bars are designed for endurance athletes and have high GI (>70), delivering rapid sugar release.

**Better choice for climbers:** Balanced-style bars with a GI in the 40–60 range (e.g., Balance Bar, PowerBar Protein Plus). The higher protein and fat content slows sugar release and helps conserve glycogen and spare muscle protein.

**Recommended 40:30:30 macronutrient ratio** in bars for sustained energy during long climbing days.

**Best combination for a long day at the crags:** Balanced energy bars + plenty of water.

### 7.4 Creatine

**What it is:** A compound natural to the body, used in muscles to help create ATP (the energy source for brief, explosive movements — anaerobic alactic energy pathway). Present in animal foods (red meat); normal diet provides ~1 g/day.

**Scientific evidence:** By far the most effective, legal sports supplement. Proven to increase muscular strength in dozens of well-controlled studies (Toler 1997, Kreider 1998).

**The "creatine loading" protocol (10–20 g/day for 5–6 days):**
- Enhances short-duration, high-intensity performance
- Causes weight gain of 2–3+ lbs (water retention — "cell volumizing")
- **LIKELY COUNTERPRODUCTIVE for climbers because:**
  1. Creatine loads in ALL muscles of the body, not just "climbing" muscles — especially the legs (largest muscles)
  2. In a sport that requires a high strength-to-weight ratio, added weight is detrimental
  3. Cell volumizing may lead to faster occlusion of capillary blood flow within muscles during strenuous exercises → worse pump, faster muscle failure
  4. May have a positive effect on very brief powerful climbs (<15 seconds), but will very well hamper longer boulder problems and difficult roped ascents

**Hörst's recommended protocol for climbers (small-dose):**
- Add just **2 to 5 grams of creatine** to a quart of sports drink that you sip throughout your workout or climbing day
- This provides just a few grams more than a normal "dietary dose" — enough to hasten recovery of maximal power between exercises and boulder problems
- No loading side effects (no weight gain, no cell volumizing)

**The Truth About Creatine for Climbers (summary box):**
- **Small-dose (2–5 g/day):** May yield a slight increase in power and a small edge in recovery between strenuous exercises, boulder problems, and routes, without any significant undesirable side effects
- **High-dose loading (10–20 g/day):** Likely counterproductive for climbers due to weight gain (especially legs), increased pump, and reduced strength-to-weight ratio

### 7.5 Caffeine

**Effects:** Cognitive and physiological — many climbers consume caffeine morning and upon arriving at gym/crag.

**Potential negative impacts of high doses:**
- Jittery and nervous — not ideal for delicate face climbs requiring fine motor control
- Gastrointestinal distress, headache, heart palpitations
- Insomnia (half-life of 4–6 hours — afternoon/evening consumption affects sleep)
- High doses may lower blood pH (more acidic), negatively impacting glycolytic exercise performance (e.g., short, pumpy sport routes)

**Optimal dosing:** Research literature shows 3–13 mg caffeine per kg body weight, but low-to-moderate dosing of **3–9 mg/kg** seems optimal (most upsides with fewest negative side effects). For an average-sized climber, this equates to approximately **200–600 mg of caffeine** — use low end if caffeine-naive, upper range if regular, heavy consumer.

**Strategic caffeine periodization for performance:**
1. Maintain moderate daily caffeine intake normally
2. In the days leading up to a critical climb or competition, **decrease** daily dosing
3. On the performance day, **return to your normal moderate dose**
4. This is superior to simply doubling your caffeine dose on performance day (which risks all the negative side effects above)

### 7.6 Sports Supplements That Don't Work

- **Testosterone boosters:** Don't work — period
- **Nitric oxide boosters:** Most yield no effect; exceptions are citrulline malate and beet root concentrate, which may slightly support NO production and vasodilation
- **Everything else** touted as "performance enhancer" or "miracle cure" is likely snake oil
- Outside of caffeine and creatine, the two most effective sports supplements, any substance that truly aids performance in a significant way is either illegal or on the WADA prohibition list

## 8. Vitamin and Sports Supplement Tips (Summary)

1. Take a **daily multivitamin** each morning with breakfast
2. Consider consuming **extra protein** (especially if you eat little meat or dairy) by drinking a **whey protein shake** each morning and evening
3. When climbing, snack on **fruits, balanced-style energy bars, sports drinks** (choose those possessing some fructose and/or protein), and other **low-GI foods**. Consider adding **2 to 5 grams of creatine** (never more) to your bottle of sports drink to further enhance recovery
4. Small, well-timed doses of **caffeine and creatine** may provide an edge in both training and climbing
5. **Avoid mass-marketed sports supplements**, especially those with the wildest claims — most are frauds
