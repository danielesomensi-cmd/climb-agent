## 1. Core Philosophy

**Central thesis:** If you are not playing a proactive role in the recovery process, then you are definitely not training optimally or climbing up to your capability. Knowing how to limit fatigue and speed recovery is as important as knowing how to perform a drop-knee, lock a finger jam, or float a deadpoint.

**Why it matters for performance:** Being able to accelerate recovery means you will get more back during a mid-climb shakeout, perform better today AND tomorrow, and on all future outings. More rapid recovery between training sessions translates into more long-term strength gains (can work out more often while still getting adequate rest without risk of overtraining).

**Key insight:** Recovery ability is a function of age, sex, and level of conditioning. Regardless, you can recover more quickly by playing a proactive role — instead of just letting it happen.

## 2. The Basics of Fatigue and Recovery

### 2.1 Five Causes of Fatigue

**1. Depletion of ATP-CP**
- ATP and creatine phosphate (CP) are energy-rich phosphate compounds stored in muscle cells in small amounts
- Fuel brief, maximum-intensity activities (short vicious boulder problem, one-arm pull-up, 100m sprint)
- Supply limits action to between 5 and 12 seconds
- Explains why double dyno campus training for >10 seconds is near impossible, and why muscles give out after ~10 seconds pulling a maximal move on a route
- **Recovery:** ATP-CP stores become fully replenished in just 3 to 5 minutes of complete rest (Bloomfield 1994)

**2. Accumulation of Metabolic By-Products**
- Sustained high-intensity activity lasting 12 seconds to 2 minutes = anaerobic glycolysis
- Produces H+ ions (hydrogen ions) → intracellular acidosis → muscular discomfort → muscular failure
- The "pump clock" runs out in less than 2 minutes on sustained near-maximum crux sequences
- **Recovery:** Clearance time of H+, lactate, and other metabolic by-products = 10 to 60 minutes, depending on initial levels and whether rest is active or passive
- Strength/power-endurance training increases tolerance to acidosis and improves cellular/systemic buffering of H+
- Enhanced blood flow helps disperse excess lactate to lesser-stressed muscles (aerobic energy production) and to liver (converted back to glucose)

**3. Depletion of Glycogen**
- Steady, long-term exercise typically depletes glycogen stores in 90 minutes to 2 hours
- Climbing is stop-and-go, so a full 2-hour glycogen supply can be stretched to last nearly all day
- Carbohydrate feeding during exercise can extend glycogen supply by 25–50% (Coyle 1984)
- **Critical:** Complete replenishment of glycogen stores takes 24 hours — a good dinner and full night's sleep are NOT enough to fully restock
- When climbing on successive days, consume more calories throughout the day (to spare glycogen) — expect to hit the wall sooner on day 2 vs. day 1

**4. Low Blood Glucose**
- Blood glucose = only fuel source for brain and nervous system
- As glycogen depletes, muscles become increasingly reliant on blood glucose → blood glucose drops → exhaustion and mental fatigue
- Ingesting carbohydrates throughout the day delays this fatigue

**5. Central Fatigue**
- Brain-based, not muscular — the central governor limiting stress/damage you can levy on yourself
- Realized through: reduction in neural drive (recruitment), impaired coordination, decreased concentration and focus, diminished ability to execute difficult motor skills or maximal movement
- **Hardest on the CNS:** Repeated high-intensity movements like lunging and campus training; excessive amounts of fingerboard, HIT, campus, or the same type of bouldering movement over and over
- Most noticeable after back-to-back-to-back hard days: generalized malaise, inability to summon peak strength/power even after days of rest = hallmark of central fatigue
- **Recovery:** Nerve cell takes up to 7× longer to recover than a muscle cell (Bompa 1983). If you still feel physically "off" after a few successive rest days, you may be experiencing central fatigue → may take 2 to 10 more days away from training and climbing to recuperate completely

### 2.2 Three Recovery Periods

**Recovery is exponential, not linear** (Figure 12.1, Bompa 1983):
- ~70% of complete recovery occurs in the first 1/3 of the recovery period
- ~90% after 2/3 of total recovery time
- The final 10% takes the remaining 1/3 of time

#### Period 1: RECHARGE (Immediate / Short-Term Recovery)
- **Timeframe:** 10 seconds to 60 minutes following completion of muscular action
- **Includes:** The 10-second shakeout mid-crux, the mid-climb rest you milk for 5–20 min
- **Metabolic processes:** Resynthesis of ATP-CP (complete in <5 min) + removal of H+ and lactate (<60 min)
- **Practical:** After ~3 minutes, majority of ATP-CP resynthesized; after ~40 minutes, blood pH returned to near-normal (90% of H+ removed)

#### Period 2: REFUEL (Medium-Term / Intraday Recovery)
- **Timeframe:** 60 minutes to 24 hours following exercise
- **Includes:** Breaks between climbs at the crag, the overnight period after a training/climbing day
- **Defined by:** Replenishment of blood glucose and glycogen stores (muscles + liver), minor repair of tissue microtraumas
- **Key fact:** ~90% of refueling occurs in the first 16 hours (2/3 of total recovery period). A typical 12-hour break between consecutive climbing days → only ~80% glycogen replenishment (assuming immediate refueling upon finishing day 1)

#### Period 3: REBUILD (Long-Term Recovery)
- **Timeframe:** 1 to 4 days following strenuous exercise
- **Muscle growth and neuromuscular adaptation** occur during this period
- DOMS: Minor may subside in 48 hours; severe may take 4+ days
- **Critical for supercompensation:** A single rest day allows recharge + refuel, but truncates rebuilding → muscle rebuilds to baseline but not ABOVE baseline. Chronic under-resting → decreased performance, injury, illness risk
- **Whether training or projecting:** The ability to accelerate recovery is tantamount to elevating your absolute level of performance

## 3. Accelerating Short-Term (Intraclimb) Recovery

### 3.1 Limit Fatigue by Climbing More Efficiently

The most simple yet powerful method — limit the magnitude of fatigue in the first place through economy of movement and optimal climbing technique. This reduces ATP-CP usage, glycogen consumption, and metabolic by-product production, meaning less fatigue to recover from and faster return to baseline strength.

**Key insight:** Most climbers move too slowly, possess less-than-ideal technique, and stop to place gear or think when they should be pushing on. While lack of experience and technical ability are the real limiting factors, slow climbing and hesitation will lead you to believe that a lack of strength is the primary problem. Some dedicated technique-training practice (see Ch. 4) will go a long way.

### 3.2 G-Tox Recovery Technique

**Problem with traditional shakeout:** The dangling-arm shakeout provides some recovery, but often not enough. When hanging at a marginal rest, it's possible to expend as much energy hanging on with one arm as is being recouped in the other → zero-net gain in recovery.

**The G-Tox technique (developed by Hörst):**
- Alternate the position of your resting arm between the normal dangling position and an above-your-head raised-hand position, every 5 to 10 seconds
- Confirmed by university research (Luke Roberts, 2003): noticeable increase in recovery rate

**Why it works:** The pump (discomfort and swelling in forearms) is largely due to increasing intracellular acidosis and hypoxia. Contractions of as little as 20% of maximum intensity begin to hamper capillary blood flow, and at 50% of maximum contraction blood flow is largely occluded. When contraction ends, blood flow resumes (reperfusion) at an accelerated rate to re-oxygenate tissue and remove metabolic by-products. This reactive hyperemic response = "the pump."

**Why dangling alone is suboptimal:** In the dangling-arm position, the rate of blood flow INTO the muscles may slightly exceed the rate of venous RETURN ("old blood" exiting the muscle) → traffic jam that perpetuates the pump and slows recovery.

**Why the raised-arm position helps:** Gravity aids venous return of blood toward the heart. By getting blood out of the arm more quickly, this practice enhances removal of H+ and other byproducts, returning to homeostatic baseline more quickly.

**Why not just keep the arm raised the whole time?** The raised-arm position requires some muscular contraction in the upper arm, shoulder, and chest — these muscles would fatigue. **Best protocol = alternating between the two positions every 5–10 seconds.**

### 3.3 Active Rest Between Climbs

**Evidence (Watts 2000):** Fifteen advanced climbers attempted to redpoint a 5.12b gym route, with eight engaging in active rest (recumbent cycling) and others in passive rest immediately after. Active-rest group returned to preclimb lactate levels within 20 minutes, while passive group took 30 minutes. **Low-intensity active rest accelerated the clearing of lactate from the blood by almost 35%.**

**Practical application at the crag:** Upon completing a pumpy route or redpoint attempt, instead of sitting down and resting passively, grab your water bottle and go for a casual 20-minute walk. This will accelerate removal of H+ and lactate, and provide a mental break from the action.

**Additional evidence (Monedero 2000):** After 15 minutes of rest, blood lactate removal was greatest in the group performing COMBINED active rest and massage. You can further improve recovery by performing self-massage on your most fatigued muscles (forearms, upper arms, shoulders).

**Draper 2006 study:** Shorter periods of active recovery (walking between bouldering trials) also provided positive effects compared with passive rest. **Bottom line for bouldering: it's better to walk around between ascents or attempts and sustain a higher heart rate to speed recovery.**

### 3.4 Pre-Hydrate

Muscles are more than 70% water. Dehydration hurts performance and slows recovery. Pre-hydrate by consuming up to a quart of water in the hours preceding a workout or climbing. Continue sipping 8 ounces every hour or two (twice this if hot).

### 3.5 Tips to Accelerate Short-Term Recovery (Summary)

1. **Limit fatigue** by climbing with maximum economy, optimal technique, and brilliant strategy
2. **Accelerate recovery at mid-climb shakeouts** by using the G-Tox technique
3. **Engage in active recovery** between climbs by walking around and performing light stretching and massage — research shows this accelerates recovery by up to 35% compared with just sitting around
4. **Do not smoke** — decreases circulation, slows recovery, inhibits collagen production (slows healing of tendons and ligaments)
5. **Camel up** by drinking a quart of water in the hours preceding a workout; continue drinking 8 oz every hour or two
6. **Add moderate generalized aerobic exercise** to your training program — a stronger cardiovascular system provides faster recovery both at mid-climb rests and between boulder problems/routes

## 4. Accelerating Medium-Term (Intraday) Recovery

**Definition:** The medium-term recuperation occurring throughout the day and up to 24 hours following exercise. This is the REFUEL period.

**Critical importance:** What you do (or don't do) during this recovery period plays a direct role in how much energy you'll have during the latter part of a long day of climbing, and it's the primary factor in how much recovery you can acquire in a single night of rest.

**Consuming the right carbohydrates at the right time is the single most vital action to accelerate recovery.** Still, stretching, massage, foam rolling, and relaxation exercises will also increase your rate of recuperation.

### 4.1 Refuel Early and Often

**The single biggest error** in recovery strategy by most climbers is delayed consumption of calories during and after a day of climbing. The natural tendency is to become so engaged in climbing that you forget to eat and drink. Compounded by the fact that strenuous exercise naturally suppresses hunger.

#### Refueling While You Climb

- Take in your first dose of calories between 1–2 hours after beginning your climb
- For cragging: a piece of fruit, a Protein Plus PowerBar, or 8 oz of sports drink after completing the first strenuous climb
- Continue eating a small serving of food every 2 hours throughout the day
- All-day climbing: total of ~4 snacks (e.g., 2 pieces of fruit and 2 energy bars)
- Half-day or bouldering: halve these amounts
- Full-day hard climbing: minimum 600–800 calories consumed during the course of the day
- **Favor low- and medium-GI foods during activity** — maintains steady blood sugar and concentration
- **Switch to higher-GI foods and beverages ONLY upon completing your day** on the rocks or reaching end of workout

#### Post-Exercise Glycogen Replenishment Protocol

**Critical finding (Burke 1999):** Waiting 2 hours after exercise to consume carbohydrates reduces glycogen replenishment by 50% compared with eating immediately. Delayed refueling after training slows recovery and rebuilding processes and may delay complete recovery by as much as a full day.

**First 30 minutes after climbing (MOST CRITICAL WINDOW):**
- Ingestion of high-GI foods immediately after exercise substantially increases the rate of muscle glycogen replacement (Richter 1984)
- Glycogen resynthesis may take place another 40% faster if protein and carbohydrate are consumed together (Niles 1997)
- **Best protocol:** 4:1 ratio of carbohydrate to protein consumption (Burke 1999)
- Example for a 72 kg climber: ~100 g carbohydrate and ~25 g protein
- Practical options: pint of Gatorade/juice/glucose-based sports drink (~50g high-GI carb) + high-protein energy bar or whey protein shake (~20g protein)
- **Chocolate skim milk** is a most excellent post-exercise recovery drink (contains over 50g carbohydrate and 17g high-quality protein per pint)

**Two hours after climbing:**
- Eat a complete meal with ~65:15:20 macronutrient ratio (carb:protein:fat)
- A moderate serving of pasta, a piece of lean meat, and a large salad or vegetables
- High-GI foods are best eaten immediately after exercise; medium- and low-GI foods are more advantageous in the 2–24 hours after exercise (slower, longer-lasting trickle of glucose for steady glycogen resynthesis)

**Before going to sleep:**
- A small meal of carbohydrate and protein within 30 minutes of going to sleep will further support glycogen resynthesis and tissue remodeling overnight
- Skim milk may be the perfect before-bedtime food: low-GI carbohydrate, high-quality protein, and the amino acid tryptophan (precursor to serotonin → slows brain activity)
- A tall glass of skim milk (consider mixing in some whey protein powder) or skim milk on a small bowl of whole-grain cereal before bedtime

### 4.2 Stretch and Massage the Hardest-Working Muscles

**Combining active rest and massage** accelerates recovery by enhancing removal of metabolic by-products from the working muscles. Deep-fiber-spreading sports massage is also an effective practice to enhance medium- and long-term recovery.

**Sports massage — how it works:**
- Traditional surface massage ("rubbing"): brief benefits, little residual effect
- **Sports massage:** Deep-fiber-spreading technique producing hyperemia (vasodilation) through the full depth of the muscle. State of hyperemia lasts long after the procedure has ended → enhanced blood flow accelerates recovery and aids healing
- Also reduces the number of small and often unfelt spasms that regularly occur in muscles → if unchecked, these spasms rob you of coordination and induce mechanical resistance and premature fatigue

**Cross-fiber friction technique:**
- Most effective stroke for climbers
- Executed with a braced finger: a simple push followed by a short push back and forth across the muscle fiber
- Keep the stroke short and rhythmic; gradually increase pressure to penetrate deeper

**Key muscles to target:** Finger flexors and extensors, pronator teres and brachioradialis (upper forearm), biceps and triceps, and the large muscles of the shoulders and back.

**Application:** 5–10 minutes of massage as part of your regular warm-up routine, along with Ch. 6 warm-up activities and stretches.

**Sports massage to accelerate recovery (post-climb):**
- Focus on climbing's most overloaded muscles: forearms, upper arms, and back (first to tire, typically the slowest to recover)
- **Direct-pressure technique** for trigger points: Apply firm, constant, straight-in pressure with a braced finger, Armaid, wooden Bodo/shepherd's crook, or friend's elbow. Hold for 15–30 seconds. Helps relieve known and unknown spasms, increase local circulation, and aid healing
- **NEVER** apply sports massage tactics to tendons, joints, or injured tissues
- Conclude massage with some foam rolling and mild stretching

### 4.3 Use Relaxation Techniques

**Progressive Relaxation Sequence** (from Ch. 3) is highly effective for:
- Relaxing muscles and quieting the mind during midday break from climbing
- Before going to sleep
- 10–20 minutes performing progressive relaxation, then sit up for a few minutes → enhanced performance on next climb

### 4.4 Tips to Accelerate Medium-Term Recovery (Summary)

1. **Refuel early and often.** Consume a piece of fruit, balanced-style energy bar, or 8 oz of sports drink every hour or two throughout the day. Favor low- to medium-GI foods and drinks
2. **Kick-start recovery** at end of workout/climbing by consuming a **high-GI sports drink or 4:1 carb-to-protein drink within the first 30 minutes** of cessation. Chocolate skim milk is an excellent choice
3. **Within 2 hours,** consume a meal comprising approximately 65:15:20 (carb:protein:fat)
4. **Avoid excessive alcohol** — provides little quality nutrition, enhances dehydration, suppresses growth hormone response, and possibly slows protein synthesis
5. **Stretch, massage, and use a heating pad/whirlpool** to increase blood flow to sore, tired muscles. Use cross-fiber friction massage, foam rolling, and direct pressure to relieve trigger point pain and spasms
6. **Engage in progressive relaxation** (see Ch. 3) to further relax muscles and quiet the mind during midday breaks and before going to sleep

## 5. Accelerating Long-Term (Interday) Recovery

**Definition:** The long-term recuperation from a severe workout or a couple of hard days. Full recovery could take 1 to 4 days, depending on intensity and volume.

### 5.1 DOMS as a Signal

When you wake up with sore muscles (DOMS), it's a sign that microtraumas occurred and that a recovery period of at least another 24 hours is needed. Two choices:
1. Go climbing/work out for a second straight day (performance will be less than ideal, injury risk increased)
2. Take a day or two off and allow neuromuscular system to recuperate to a level of capability higher than before the workout (supercompensation)

**Weekend climbing trips** are the classic situation: regardless of sore muscles, given proper nutrition, a good warm-up, and a prudent approach, you can usually get away with climbing sore on day 2. But choosing to rest is clearly the intelligent decision when training indoors or during off-season training cycles.

**Proper rest is as important as training stimulus** in becoming a stronger climber, and under-resting is a primary cause of injury. Enthusiastic indoor and sport climbers are most commonly guilty of under-resting.

### 5.2 Eat Frequent, Small Meals

Instead of 3 typical meals, consume **6 smaller meals or snacks** spaced evenly throughout the day:
- Avoid high-GI foods (less effective for recovery after the first 2 hours post-exercise)
- Select low- and medium-GI foods for all meals
- Drink at least 2 quarts of water throughout the day
- At least 3 of your meals should contain a significant portion of protein (e.g., eggs + skim milk + whey protein at breakfast; low-fat yogurt + skim milk or tuna at lunch; lean red meat, chicken, or fish at dinner)
- Each meal should include some carbohydrate, and at all costs avoid fat-laden fried foods and any snack foods containing hydrogenated oils
- Strive for a macronutrient profile of roughly **65:15:20** for each major meal
- The other 3 feedings need be only a couple hundred calories each — low- and medium-GI foods (fruit, balanced-type energy bar)

### 5.3 Take a Multivitamin

Daily multivitamin as a must; additional magnesium is beneficial and may help muscles relax and return to normal function.

### 5.4 Stretch, Foam Roll, and Massage Sore Muscles

Gentle stretching, foam rolling, and sports massage are widely accepted as effective means to lengthen muscles and enhance recovery following strenuous exercise. **Stretch and foam roll for at least 10 to 20 minutes per day** following the procedures outlined in Ch. 6.

### 5.5 Get Plenty of Sleep

**Minimum:** 6 to 7 hours of sleep per night
**Ideal after strenuous training/climbing:** 8 to 10 hours
**For mega-long day climbs, multiday big-wall or alpine ascents:** 8 to 10 hours (or more)

Sleep is vital for any climber serious about training. Most neuromuscular regeneration occurs during sleep. Hörst recommends identifying low-value activities (surfing the Net, watching TV, certain social events) that can be reduced or eliminated to allow for more sleep.

### 5.6 Heating Pad

Apply a heating pad to sore muscles, twice daily for 10 to 15 minutes, to increase blood flow and accelerate recovery and healing. **Never** place a heating pad on a swollen or acutely injured body part.

### 5.7 Engage in Light Activities (Active Rest Days)

Active rest is beneficial for long-term recovery because it enhances circulation of nutrients to damaged muscles and produces a general loosening effect on stiff muscles.

**Best active-rest activities for climbers:** Hiking, jogging, light mountain biking, and even some limited less-than-vertical or big-hold climbing (ARC training protocol). **Crucial:** Low enough intensity that you don't get heavily winded or pumped and break only a light sweat. Limit yourself to 30–60 minutes of active-recovery exercise.

### 5.8 Possess a Positive, Calm Personality

**Subtle but powerful:** A positive, relaxed, easygoing attitude not only puts you in a better performance state, but it has been proven to increase recovery and maybe even encourage muscular growth.

**Hormonal mechanism:** Strenuous exercise causes a release of growth hormone (anabolic — positive) but also cortisol (catabolic — breaks down muscle). Chronic stress and Type A, aggressive behavior → higher levels of cortisol (Williams 1982) and reduced growth hormone levels. Behavior modification and reduction of stressors can reverse this effect (Dinan 1994).

**Conclusion:** A relaxed approach to climbing and a humorous attitude about life will play an underlying but beneficial role in enhancing training adaptations, accelerating recovery, and boosting climbing performance.

### 5.9 Long-Term Recovery Tips (Summary)

1. **Eat small, frequent meals** — consume 6 smaller meals and snacks comprising low- to mid-GI foods and protein sources
2. **Drink 8 oz of water** every 1–2 hours to rehydrate and flush toxins from your body
3. **Take a daily multivitamin;** supplemental magnesium is also highly recommended
4. **Avoid excessive consumption of alcoholic beverages** — provide little quality nutrition, enhance dehydration, suppress growth hormone response, and slow protein synthesis following exercise
5. **Engage in daily stretching, foam rolling, massage,** and active-recovery activities such as hiking, biking, and easy running
6. **Apply a heating pad** to sore muscles, twice daily for 10–15 minutes, to increase blood flow and accelerate recovery and healing. Never place on swollen or acutely injured body part
7. **Get at least 6–7 hours of sleep** per night; 8–10 hours (or more) after mega-long day climbs or multiday big-wall/alpine ascents
8. **Strive for a calm, relaxed, easygoing disposition every day** — this will help lower cortisol levels and foster more rapid recovery
