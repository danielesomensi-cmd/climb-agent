## 1. Core Premise and Framing

Up to three-quarters of all recreational and elite climbers have suffered a climbing injury. Fortunately, only a small number of injuries are severe traumas produced by falls — **the majority are overuse and minor acute injuries** that most commonly occur in the fingers, elbows, and shoulders.

**Hörst's framing:** This chapter is written as a coach's advice, not a medical dissertation. He urges seeking professional treatment for any injury that is more than "minor," as well as any injury that remains symptomatic for more than a few weeks. **Critical warning:** Many climbers have fallen into the trap of self-treatment and trying to "climb through" an injury — these approaches often make matters worse and can lead to unnecessarily long-term downtime.

**References recommended:** Schöffl & Hochholzer, *One Move Too Many* (most comprehensive expert coverage of climbing injuries); Dr. Lisa Erikson's *Climbing Injuries Solved* (physical therapist perspective, dozens of effective self-treatment methods).

## 2. Epidemiology of Climbing Injuries

### 2.1 Sites of Nonfall Injuries (Schöffl 2015, n=836 patients, 911 injuries)

| Site | Frequency |
|------|-----------|
| **Finger** | **52%** |
| Shoulder | 17% |
| Hand | 13% |
| Elbow | 9% |
| Leg/Foot | 4% |
| Knee | 2% |
| Other | 3% |

**Trend note:** The frequency of shoulder injuries has increased significantly since Schöffl's previous study a decade earlier — from 5% (6th most common) to 17% (2nd most common). One theory: the escalation of shoulder injuries is the recent proliferation and inappropriate use of campus training boards. Elbow injuries slightly decreased (13% → 9%).

### 2.2 Contributing Factors

**Near-unanimous conclusion from injury studies:** Occurrence of overuse injuries is directly proportional to climbing ability and the perceived importance of climbing to the individual.

Key data points:
- Doran (2000): At the 5.12 level, nearly 88% of climbers surveyed had experienced overuse injuries in the prior two years. By comparison, only 20% of individuals climbing at levels below 5.9 incurred overuse injuries — the lowest relative frequency of all categories
- Wright (2001): Linear relation between lead-climbing grade and overuse injuries
- Consensus: incidence of overuse injuries has increased since the advent of indoor walls and sport-climbing tactics — preponderance of overhanging terrain at indoor climbing facilities is a contributing factor to the increase in upper-body injuries
- Schöffl 2015: More experienced, older climbers had significantly more overuse injuries than acute injuries
- **Important new finding (Schöffl 2015):** A4 pulley injuries are now more common than A2 pulley injuries; incidence of growth plate fractures in youth climbers has increased 600% in the decade since his previous study

**Training practices as cofactors:** Fingerboards and dynamic double-handed campus training were popular among the climbers in Doran's study. These high-stress, ultra-specific forms of training may be injurious or at least exacerbate low-grade preexisting injuries. These conclusions underscore the importance of a prudent, mature approach to climbing-specific training that knows when to say enough and errs on the side of over-resting rather than overtraining.

## 3. Finger Injuries

### 3.1 Anatomy Overview

Flexion of fingers and wrist is produced by muscles of the forearm that originate from the medial (inside) elbow and terminate via long tendons that attach to the middle phalanx (MP) and distal phalanx (DP) of each finger. Both flexor tendons (FDS and FDP) pass through a tunnel-like, synovia-lined tendon sheath held close to the bone by **five annular pulleys (A1, A2, and so forth) and three (sometimes four) cruciform pulleys (C0, C1, C2, C3)**. The A2 and A4 pulleys are the most important (Lin 1989).

**Critical behavioral note:** At the early (mild) stage, finger injuries are easy for a passionate climber to ignore or deny — rationalized by the belief that "it's okay to climb with one injured finger since there are nine healthy fingers to carry the load." The truth: continuing to climb on an injured finger may increase the severity of the injury and ultimately double or triple (or more) the downtime needed to recover.

### 3.3 Tendon Pulley Injuries

The most common finger injuries experienced by climbers. Involve partial tears or complete ruptures of one or more of the flexor tendon annular pulleys.

**Key facts:**
- Historically A2 was the most commonly injured; recent study of 140 pulley injuries found a **shift toward A4 being the most frequently injured** (Schöffl 2015)
- Injuries to A2 and A4 can range from microscopic to partial tears and, in the worst case, a complete rupture
- Small partial tears are generally insidious — they develop over the course of a few climbs, a few days of climbing, or even gradually during a climbing season
- **Overdependence on the crimp grip**, with its near-90-degree flexion of the PIP joint, may increase risk — crimping produces a tremendous force load on the annular pulleys and is therefore considered a more stressful grip than the open hand
- Less frequent are acute ruptures during a maximum move on a tiny crimp hold or one-finger pocket — feeling or hearing a pop = likely sign of a significant partial tear or complete rupture

**Symptoms of A2 pulley injury:**
- Pain and swelling at the base of the finger
- Can range from slight to so debilitating that you can't perform everyday tasks like picking up a jug of milk
- Swelling may limit range of motion during flexion
- Bowstringing may be felt or seen (Marco 1998) if one or two additional pulleys (usually A3 and A4) are ruptured
- Slight tears may be asymptomatic when the finger is at rest, but become painful during isometric contraction (as in gripping a hold) or when pressing on the base of the finger near the top of the palm

**Treatment — immediate response:**
- Treatment of a pulley injury must begin with **complete cessation of climbing and discontinuation of any other activity that requires forceful flexion of the injured finger**
- Doing anything that causes pain will slow healing of the injured tissue and may make the injury worse
- The prudent response to developing finger pain is to **cease climbing immediately** to prevent further damage — a low-grade tweak may heal in a matter of weeks; trying to "climb through the injury" all but guarantees a worse tear and a much longer withdrawal

**Treating an A2 Pulley Injury — 7-step protocol:**
1. Immediately cease climbing and any other activity that requires forceful flexion of the injured finger. Consult a doctor if there is noticeable bowstringing on the flexor tendon
2. Use ice and NSAID medications only if the injury produces palpable or visible swelling. Cease use of ice and NSAIDs as soon as swelling diminishes. Smokers should consider breaking the habit, since smoking has been shown to slow healing of tendons and ligaments
3. Immobilize with a splint or buddy taping for a few days up to a week or two, if daily activities cause acute pain
4. As pain decreases — and only when all swelling is gone — begin light daily finger activity including finger flexions; squeezing putty, foam blocks, or a rubber doughnut; mild stretching; and massage. This light exercise is important to accelerate and ensure proper healing
5. Use a heating pad for ten to fifteen minutes, three times a day, to increase blood flow and accelerate healing. Alternating heat and cold (ice bath) may further enhance beneficial circulation
6. Use the therapy described in steps 4 and 5 for at least two to four weeks before beginning a gradual return to climbing. Use prophylactic taping ("H method" is best) every time you climb, and spend the first week or two climbing relatively easy routes with big holds and good footholds
7. Return to maximal climbing if easy climbing yields no pain. Continue taping for at least six months, since complete tendon remodeling can take many months. Climb smart!

**Return timeline (French study of 12 elite climbers with A2 injuries):**
- 8 subjects were able to successfully return to climbing within five days of rest (Moutet 1993)
- More severe pulley tears may require as much as two or three months of rest before progressively returning to climbing
- Regardless of degree of injury and length of withdrawal: use of prophylactic taping is essential during return to climbing
- Dr. Schöffl recommends taping the injured finger for up to **six months**

**Surgical intervention:** In the case of a complete rupture or multi-pulley injury, surgical reconstruction is often necessary. Schöffl recommends a conservative nonsurgical approach for single-pulley injuries, and recommends surgical repair when two or more pulleys are ruptured.

### 3.4 Prophylactic Taping Methods

Reinforcing flexor tendon pulleys with athletic tape is common practice. Three valid methods:

**A2 Ring Method:**
- Firm circumferential taping at the distal end of the proximal phalanx — just above the A2 pulley and immediately below the PIP joint
- Use a narrow strip of tape, approximately 0.4 inch (10mm) wide
- Wrap as tightly and as close to the PIP joint as possible without restricting blood flow
- Remove and reapply fresh tape every few hours to maintain necessary tightness throughout a full day of climbing

**Figure-8 Method:**
- May provide additional tendon support to A3, A4, and cruciform pulleys
- With a slight bend in the finger: begin with two turns of tape over the proximal phalanx (on top of the A2 pulley), then cross under the PIP joint and take two turns around the middle phalanx, then cross back under the PIP joint and conclude with another turn or two around the base of the finger

**H Method (recommended by Schöffl — most effective):**
- Been shown to be the most effective method in terms of decreasing the tendon-to-bone distance of a bowstringing pulley
- Begin with a 4-inch-long (10cm) piece of 1 or 1.5-inch (25mm) wide tape
- Use a rip or cut the tape longways from each end so that there's just a 0.5-inch (12mm) "bridge" in the middle, forming the center of the "H"
- Position this bridge over the palm side of the PIP joint, then wrap the free ends of the tape around the finger just above and below the PIP joint
- Remove and reapply fresh tape every few hours to maintain necessary tightness throughout a full day of climbing
- Use the strongest, stickiest tape available (German Leukotape is best)

### 3.5 Flexor Tendinitis, Tenosynovitis, and Tendon Rupture

Several other injuries can produce pain and diminished function in ways similar to an injured annular pulley. Diagnosis can be difficult — see a physician to be completely sure.

**Tenosynovitis/Tendovaginitis:** Inflammation of the tendon sheath impairs flexor tendon function, leading to increasing pain and inflammation. A constant dull ache with each finger movement and pain that radiates down the tendon is the hallmark symptom. **Withdrawal from climbing is essential** — follow the A2 pulley treatment protocol.

**Flexor tendon rupture/avulsion:** In rare cases a flexor tendon may rupture or avulse at the point of insertion. Jebson (1997) states that FDS tendon rupture may occur with the crimp grip, while FDP tendon rupture is more likely with an open-hand pocket grip. Either rupture or avulsion would occur acutely with immediate onset of pain, finger swelling, and an absence of active flexion of the PIP joint (FDS rupture) or DIP joint (FDP rupture). **Surgical reattachment may be required.**

### 3.6 Capsulitis and Sprained Joint Capsule

**Capsulitis:** Most often presents as visible swelling and a reduced range of motion in the middle (PIP) joint of the finger. Though not debilitating, a long season of climbing — especially hard climbing with habitual use of the crimp grip — often leads to swelling of the PIP joint and, most bothersome, the sensation of achy, stiff fingers upon waking in the morning. These symptoms develop from cartilage stress and an increase in synovial fluid — usually the result of persistent, forceful loading of a sharply flexed PIP joint (as in crimping).

**Treatment:** A reduction in climbing frequency, along with a conscious shift to using the open-hand grip, should prevent worsening of the condition. Use ice to address the swelling in acute cases. Low-grade chronic capsulitis may improve somewhat with finger flexing exercises, mild stretching, and application of heat. It may take complete withdrawal from climbing for the joint to fully heal.

**Sprained joint capsule:** Finger jamming (in cracks) and dynamic pocket pulling on just one or two fingers can sprain the joint capsule, producing similar symptoms to capsulitis but typically causing more acute pain. Buddy taping or splinting may be helpful during the early stages of healing. Prudence dictates withdrawal from climbing and a few weeks of movement therapy before a gradual return.

### 3.7 Collateral Ligament Injuries

Collateral ligament sprains occur in climbers particularly as a result of a powerful lunge or awkward torque off a "fixed" finger (as in a jam or tight pocket). A ligament sprain manifests with mild to moderate pain and swelling around the PIP joint, but with no loss of stability (Jebson 1997).

**Treatment:**
- Incomplete injuries: typically involves splinting the PIP joint for 10 to 14 days, after which buddy taping can be used and range-of-motion exercises started (Bach 1999)
- Climbing can gradually be reintroduced despite persistent low-grade pain and swelling, which may take months to resolve (Jebson 1997)
- Complete collateral ligament injuries: usually treated operatively; full function is often restored in approximately three months

### 3.8 Growth Plate Injury in Junior Climbers

**Most disturbing injury trend in youth climbing.** Epiphyseal (growth plate) fractures in fingers — common symptoms are slow onset of pain and swelling of the middle finger joint, and in some cases the inability to crimp grip on holds. The condition is easily diagnosed via X-ray.

**Key data:**
- Hochholzer (2005): evaluated 24 junior climbers with non-traumatic epiphyseal fractures — only one of the injured climbers was a girl, so boys appear to be at significantly greater risk
- Schöffl (2015): 600% increase in growth plate fractures among youth climbers
- Adolescent climbers are most at risk during their growth spurt years (ages 11–14 in girls, 12–16 in boys), although fractures can occur up to age 18
- Campus training is a leading cause; epiphyseal crack can also develop in youths who engage in frequent intensive bouldering involving copious dynamic movements and repeated extreme crimping moves

**Contributing factors:** Inappropriate training (e.g., campus training, hypergravity training, intensive fingerboard training), a singular focus on hard bouldering, and simply climbing too much (more than three or four days per week) are all contributing factors.

**Treatment:** Complete withdrawal from climbing is essential for adolescents experiencing finger joint pain. Parents and coaches must enforce a rest period of at least a few months or more away from climbing until the youth climber is asymptomatic.

**Prevention protocol for youth climbers:**
- Ages 11–16: absolutely no double-dyno campus training
- A small amount of controlled hand-over-hand campus laddering on large holds may be permitted by climbers strong and mature enough to do it with smooth, low-impact movements
- Conscious favoring of the open-hand grip over the crimp grip on all but the smallest holds
- Youth climbers should do little, if any, hypergravity training (climbing or hangboard training with weight added to the body)
- Limit climbing activity to **three days per week** during the growth spurt (most GP fractures occur between ages 13 and 15)
- Disallow use of advanced training exercises such as campus training, hypergravity training, and intensive fingerboard training until at least **age 16 or the end of puberty** (achievement of maximum adult height)

### 3.9 Carpal Tunnel Syndrome

Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) = a condition in which a nerve passing through the wrist, with the flexor tendons, is exposed chronically to too much pressure. Affects a small number of climbers; its occurrence does not appear to be disproportionately common in climbers (Robinson 1993). Symptoms include numbness, burning, and tingling of the fingers; may become worse at night or during activity or elevation (Lewis 1993). Treatment: cessation or lowering intensity of climbing, anti-inflammatory medicines, splinting of the wrist in a neutral position at night for three to six weeks. Surgical decompression of the carpal tunnel may be required if conservative treatment fails.

### 3.10 Arthritis

**Reassuring findings for veteran climbers:** Anecdotal evidence from many 20- and 30-year veterans, as well as recent studies, indicates that predictions that rock climbers will become severe arthritis sufferers are not quite panning out.

- One study compared radiographs of hands of veteran elite climbers with an age-matched control group — an increased rate of osteoarthritis for several joints was found in the climber group, though no significant difference in the overall prevalence of osteoarthritis was found between the two groups (Rohrbaugh 1998)
- A more recent study concluded that climbers are **not at an increased risk** for developing debilitating osteoarthritis — it also discovered that climbers' finger and hand bones are wider (indicating that additional bone is deposited superiosteally) and stronger (Sylvester 2006)
- Still: individuals who predominantly use the crimp grip may experience some swelling and arthritis in the PIP and DIP joints
- **For aging climbers with joint swelling/pain/stiffness:** Glucosamine sulfate 1,500mg daily has been shown to reduce pain and stiffness, and — more importantly — to slow the degradation of affected joints (Reginster 2001). Effects are cumulative, not immediate. Glucosamine sulfate and MSM (1.5 to 3.0 grams each) taken in long-term daily doses show the most statistically significant beneficial results.

## 4. NSAID Warning — "Vitamin I" Section

Daily use of NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, aspirin) does have drawbacks, including risk of ulceration of the stomach, impaired kidney function, and anti-blood-clotting effects. Furthermore, **regular NSAID use may actually slow muscle cell regeneration and hamper healing of muscles, ligaments, tendons, and cartilage** (Almekinders 1999, 2003). In aggregate, these risks and side effects make a compelling case against regular NSAID use — and may help explain why so many ibuprofen-using climbers experience tendon injuries.

**Alternatives:**
- **RICE method** (rest, ice, compression, elevation) for acute injury — ice the injured area for 20 minutes, three to six times a day. Highly effective for controlling swelling and reducing pain in the hours following acute injury. Continued use of RICE beyond the first few days following injury will inhibit the healing process
- For DOMS: measures that increase circulation will promote healing — low-intensity general exercise, foam rolling, whirlpool, heating pad
- **Omega-3 essential fatty acids** (fish oil supplements): shown to be effective for treating musculoskeletal injuries and dysogenic diseases (Maroon 2006). Heart-healthy dose = 1 gram omega-3 EFA/day; anti-inflammatory benefit for climbers = 2 to 4 grams/day

## 5. Elbow Tendinopathy

Pain near the bony medial (inside) or lateral (outside) epicondyles of the elbow is a common ailment among serious climbers. In most cases the onset of pain is gradual — frequent climbing produces microscopic injury that fails to repair before the tendon is subjected to additional strain. A tendinosis cycle develops and amplifies as breakdown exceeds repair and microtraumas accumulate over many weeks and months.

**Critical insight:** The repair and strengthening process occurs more slowly in tendons (10x slower than in muscles). Eventually the muscles are able to create more force than the tendons can adapt to — the result is injury.

**Good pain vs. bad pain distinction:** You can more easily manage tendinopathy by early recognition of symptoms and proactive treatment. Early recognition ("a few weeks") vs. trying to "climb through it" could mean the difference between six weeks and six months (or more) of climbing downtime.

**Three types of tendon injury:**
- **Tendinosis:** gradual onset through increasing incidence of painful twinges or soreness during or after climbing — the most common type
- **Tendinitis:** acute onset of pain in the midst of a single hard move, usually followed by inflammation and palpable swelling
- **Tenosynovitis** (paratenonitis/tenosynovaginitis): inflammation and degeneration of the outer layers of the tendon and the synovia-lined tendon sheath

**Slow healing note (Lead-better 1992; Liu 1995):** After acute injury, the strength of a repaired tendon can remain as much as 30 percent lower than normal for months or even years.

### 5.1 Medial Epicondylitis (Golfer's/Climber's Elbow)

Pain near the medial epicondyle in the tendons connecting the pronator teres muscle and/or the finger flexor muscles to the knobby, medial epicondyle of the inside elbow.

**Root cause mechanism:**
- All muscles that produce finger flexion are anchored to the medial epicondyle
- The muscles that produce hand pronation (turning the palm outward to face the rock) also originate from the medial epicondyle
- Biceps contraction produces supination (turning of the palm upward), but in gripping the rock you generally need to maintain a pronated, palms-out position
- This battle between the supinating action of the biceps pull and the necessity to maintain a pronated hand position strains the typically undertrained teres pronator muscle and its attachment at the medial epicondyle
- **Contributing factors:** Climbing too often, finger training too hard, resting too little

**Two-phase treatment:**
- **Phase I:** Withdrawal from climbing (and all sport-specific training) + commencement of pain-reducing and anti-inflammatory measures. Icing the elbow 20 minutes, 3 to 6 times a day; limited use of NSAIDs only following injury (cease use within days to a week); cortisone injection possibly helpful in chronic or severe cases (though Nirschl 1996 notes this may be detrimental to the healing process). Duration: 2 weeks to 2 months depending on severity
- **Phase II:** Retrain and rehabilitate injured tissues through mild stretching and strength-training exercises. Since forearm-muscle imbalance plays a primary role in elbow injuries: vital to perform exercises that strengthen the weaker aspects of the forearm — **hand pronation for medial tendinosis** and **hand/wrist extension for lateral tendinosis**. During rehab phase: avoid any exercise or activity that causes sharper pain or lingering/worsening pain after a workout. Pull-ups, fingerboarding, and campus training are often problematic and should only be done in small doses, if at all, until the condition has resolved

**After Phase II:** After 3–4 weeks of pain-free training, begin a gradual return to climbing. Start with low-angle and easy vertical routes, and take a month or two to return to original level of climbing. Continue stretching and strength-training exercises **indefinitely** — as long as you are a climber, you must engage in these preventive measures. Failed rehabilitation and relapse into chronic pain may eventually lead to a need for surgical intervention.

**Prevention:** Daily forearm stretching and twice-weekly antagonist muscle training of the wrist extensor muscles; post-climbing and rest day tissue management via self-massage or an Armaid — will help release spasms and adhesions, lengthen the muscle, and increase circulation.

### 5.2 Lateral Tendinosis (Tennis Elbow)

Irritation of the tendons that attach the **finger/wrist extensor muscles** to the lateral epicondyle on the outside of the elbow. The finger extensor muscles are antagonists to the finger flexor muscles used so prominently in gripping the rock, and therefore the extensors are often disproportionately weak compared with the flexors.

**Key insight:** Grip strength is greatest when your hand is in the extended position — this explains why your arm "chicken wings" away from the rock when you struggle to grip small crimp holds. The extensor muscles facilitate this chicken-wing position; thus constant straining to grip small crimp holds can lead to tendon injury where the extensor muscles attach to the lateral epicondyle. **Note: Reducing your use of the crimp grip will lower your risk of tendinosis.**

Without rest and treatment: condition will progressively worsen to the point that climbing becomes prohibitively painful and even everyday tasks are hampered. Since such a severe case often requires a 6-month (or longer) rehabilitation period away from climbing, it's paramount that you take necessary steps to mitigate this injury early on.

**Treating Elbow Tendinosis — 8-step protocol:**
1. Cease climbing and climbing-specific training
2. Apply ice and take NSAID medications only if the injury produces palpable swelling (most elbow tendinopathy does not) or persistent pain. Cease use of ice and NSAIDs as soon as swelling and pain diminish — further use may slow healing
3. Never use NSAIDs to mask pain in order to continue climbing while injured. Regular use of NSAIDs (and smoking) may actually weaken tendons!
4. If no swelling is present: begin mild stretching, light massage, and use of a heating pad (10–15 minutes) three times per day. Most important: twice-daily use of the forearm stretches shown in Chapter 6
5. Use an Armaid daily to improve forearm muscle tissue quality (never use on tendons)
6. If swelling is present and pain is minor: engage in rehabilitative exercises on an every-other-day basis — arm circles, finger flexions, massage, or use of a heating pad. Use reverse wrist curls and reverse arm curls for lateral tendinosis and forearm pronators for medial tendinosis
7. Cautiously return to climbing when your elbow is pain-free and no sooner than after two to four weeks of strength-training exercise. Begin with easy, foot-oriented climbing for the first few weeks, and limit use of the crimp grip. Cease climbing if you experience pain while climbing and immediately return to step 2
8. Commit to long-term training of the forearm pronator and extensor muscles, and enjoy daily stretching and Armaid use for as long as you are an active climber

## 6. Shoulder Injuries

The shoulder joint takes a lot of punishment from climbing, especially those obsessed with V-hard bouldering, roped climbing on steep terrain, and intensive climbing-specific training. Shoulder injuries are the **second most common climbing injury** (Schöffl 2015). A variety of injuries can occur, from impingement or tendinitis to a partial dislocation, rotator cuff, or SLAP tear.

**Critical observation:** The SLAP tear is now the number one shoulder disorder among climbers (Table 13.2), and the number of climbers needing to go under the knife for a SLAP or rotator cuff repair has increased alarmingly. Today's super-steep sport climbs, jumpy/lunging boulder problems, and the popularization of campus training have all contributed to this increase. Each of these activities places great strain on the shoulder joint, which, without sufficient development of the stabilizing muscles, will become unstable, causing impingement, tendinitis, or worse.

**Shoulder injury distribution (Table 13.2):**

| Diagnosis | Frequency |
|-----------|-----------|
| SLAP tear | 33% |
| Impingement | 26% |
| Shoulder sprain | 11% |
| Dislocation | 10% |
| Supraspinatus tendinitis | 5% |
| Instability | 5% |
| Tendinosis of long biceps tendon | 3% |
| Rupture of long biceps tendon | 3% |
| Rotator cuff tear | 3% |

### 6.1 Impingement Syndrome, Bursitis, and Tendinitis

These conditions are closely related and a common source of shoulder pain in sports that demand consistent, forceful overhead arm movements. Without sufficient strength in the rotator cuff muscles and large shoulder muscles, the head of the humerus (upper arm bone) will elevate. Tight biceps and pectoralis muscles compound the problem by inwardly rotating the shoulders. Weak rhomboid and trapezius muscles fail to place the scapula in the proper position for overhead arm moves. All combined, these physical handicaps are at the root of most shoulder problems.

**Treatment:** Begins with steps to reduce pain and inflammation — icing 20 minutes, 3–6 times/day; limited use of NSAIDs; cessation of climbing and overhead hand movements. As pain subsides: gentle stretching and strengthening exercises can be introduced gradually — these rehabilitative exercises are essential to lower the risk of injury relapse upon returning to climbing (see the Training Rotator Cuff and Scapular Stability exercises in Chapter 6). In minor cases followed by dedicated rehabilitation: return to climbing may be possible in one to two months. More serious cases: six months or more.

### 6.2 Shoulder Instability

A condition that's become increasingly common among high-end climbers with a taste for overhanging routes, as well as individuals who engage in excessive fingerboard training with poor technique (relaxed, shrugged shoulders). The condition develops gradually from long-term, repeated exposure to straight-arm hangs, Gaston moves, and severe lock-offs, as well as from overzealous stretching, or climbing on overhanging routes day after day on hard boulder problems without adequate rest and training of the stabilizing antagonist muscles.

**Key mechanism:** Constant stretching of the ligaments + growing imbalance of the muscles that surround and stabilize the shoulder joint → instability and risk of injury. The force of the levering motion on the shoulder joint increases when the arm is extended with the elbow pointing outward (as in grabbing a high Gaston hold, chicken winging on a small crimp hold, or making a long reach on overhanging rock).

**Treatment:** Two-phase process of resting until pain diminishes, then use of rehabilitative exercises to strengthen the rotator cuff muscle group. Climbing activity must be markedly reduced or eliminated during the rehabilitative process. Avoid any overhead motion or activity that causes pain in the shoulder. Anti-inflammatory medicine and ice applied twice daily. Rehab may require anywhere from 2 to 6 months before you can reintroduce climbing activities.

### 6.3 SLAP Tear

One of the most severe — and unfortunately most common — injuries experienced by modern sport climbers and boulderers. SLAP = acronym for "superior labral tear from anterior to posterior" — an injury that almost always requires surgery.

**Symptoms:** Aching pain after a strenuous workout or day of climbing; a "catching" feeling in the shoulder while climbing; shoulder pain while sleeping; decrease in arm and grip strength.

**Cause:** Out-of-control dynamic arm moves, campus training with straight-arm grabs (or any similar move while climbing on steep terrain), and power training or near-limit steep climbing while in a high state of fatigue — results from the inability to properly stabilize the humeral head and scapula during forceful overhead movements.

**Treatment:** Usually begins with arthroscopic surgery to repair the tear and address any other associated injuries. A long, gradually progressive rehabilitation follows, starting with a few weeks of range of motion exercise, then about two months of slow introduction of strengthening exercises. Return to full activity: six months or more. As a rule, an extremely fit climber with a lower-grade SLAP tear will be the quickest to return to rigorous climbing.

## 7. Knee Injuries

Injury to the knee is a relatively new phenomenon in climbing that correlates directly to the proliferation of indoor and sport climbing. New climbing techniques like the drop-knee weren't popularized until the 1990s but are now a staple move of steep indoor and outdoor climbs.

**Meniscus tear:** Repeated use of the drop-knee, especially under high force load, can tear the specialized cartilage in the knee called the meniscus (Stelzle 2000). A partial or total tear can occur during forced rotation of the knee while the foot remains in a fixed position. In climbing, these tears most often occur in severe drop-knee positions. Symptoms range from mild pain and no visible swelling to severe pain, swelling, reduced mobility, and the knee popping, clicking, and locking in certain positions.

**Arthroscopic surgery** is very successful in relieving pain and restoring full function to the injured knee. Recovery: 3 days to 7 days of rest, then a gradual return; full return to training and climbing typically takes 2 to 3 months.

## 8. Youth Climbing Injuries — Special Section

Climbing is a wonderful brain-, body-, and character-building sport for kids, but it presents a very real risk of injury. Professional instruction and coaching can largely eliminate the risk of serious injuries due to falls or human error. Still, there's the hidden, more subtle risk of various overuse injuries.

**Hard-and-fast rule:** Any youth climber, regardless of age, experiencing chronic pain in the fingers (or elsewhere) should cease climbing for a few weeks and consult a doctor if the pain continues. As a hard-and-fast rule, climbing and climbing-specific training must be limited to an **aggregate of four days per week**. The guidance of an adult climber or professional coach is extremely beneficial both in helping structure workouts and in monitoring rest and nutritional habits.

**Youth nutrition and sleep:**
- Calorie restriction is inappropriate in almost all situations for adolescents
- Youth athletes should be encouraged to eat "three squares" a day, including lots of fruits, vegetables, lean meats, and low-fat dairy products
- Healthy snacks such as energy bars, bagels, and even low-fat chocolate milk are excellent as between-meal and climb-time snacks
- In terms of sleep: nine to ten hours per night is best, with eight hours being the bare minimum

**Balanced approach conclusion:** The best way to prevent injuries, including mental burnout, is for youth climbers to participate in activities and sports outside the climbing world. Youths can perform at national or world-class level while at the same time participating in other extracurricular activities and sports. Example: Sasha DiGiulian ran cross country and track in high school, climbed a few evenings per week at the gym, aced her classes — and upon completing high school became one of the first women to climb 5.14c/d.

## 9. Ten Tips for Reducing Your Injury Risk

Hörst's observation after more than 30 years as a coach: if a person climbs passionately for many years with the intention of pushing their limits, the likelihood of suffering some kind of acute or overuse injury is close to 100 percent.

1. **Focus on technique training over strength training.** Many overuse injuries result from too much strength and power training too early in an individual's climbing career. Beginner and intermediate climbers are at risk because tendons strengthen at a much slower rate than muscles. It's important to avoid the added stress of using highly specific training tools, such as a fingerboard or campus board, during the first year or two in the sport

2. **Perform a progressive warm-up before every training and climbing session.** Elevate your heart rate (and break a light sweat) by way of five to ten minutes of jogging, hiking, rowing, or cycling; gym climbers can also do a few sets of jumping jacks or burpees. Next, do a few of the mobility exercises and stretches detailed in chapter 6 — most important are the finger, arm, and shoulder warm-ups, although doing some core and lower-body stretching is also beneficial. Begin your climbing with a few moderate boulder problems (or routes) and progress to problems/routes near your limit over the course of 30 minutes to an hour. A brief cool-down is also beneficial: 5 to 15 minutes of light aerobic activity + a few minutes of foam rolling or stretching

3. **Regularly train the antagonist and stabilizing muscle groups.** Training the antagonist muscles is one of the most overlooked — and vital — parts of training for climbing. Muscle imbalances in the forearms, shoulders, and torso are primary factors in many of the injuries covered in this chapter. **Priority one** is training scapular stability and the rotator cuff (to protect your shoulders) as well as the extensor muscles of the forearm (to protect your wrists and elbows). The time and equipment involved is minimal — most antagonist-muscle exercises can be performed at home with nothing more than a few pairs of dumbbells and a TRX trainer. Time commitment: 20 to 40 minutes, twice per week. **No excuses — just do it!**

4. **Stretch and foam roll daily to enhance recovery and improve mobility.** A daily ritual of foam rolling and stretching will not only become one of the more pleasurable parts of your day, it will also go a long way to helping you move better, climb harder, and reduce risk of injury. Begin foam rolling the large muscles of your legs and back — locate tight spots, knots, and spasms, dwell on trigger points for a minute or two until tension diminishes. Address tight/sore spots in the climbing muscles of the forearm and upper arm with an Armaid or Rubbit. Conclude with some gentle stretching of the muscles, especially the hamstrings, hip flexors, lower and upper back muscles, and arms and forearms

5. **Climb with self-awareness and caution through potentially injurious-feeling moves.** With increasing climbing experience comes a growing sixth sense of potentially injurious climbing moves. This type of self-awareness is critical for advanced climbers pushing their limits in the gym and at the crags. The goal: consciously evaluate the situation — is what you are feeling "good pain" (muscle pump and acute fatigue) or "bad pain" (a tendon or joint being stressed to its limit) — then make a snap decision as to whether or not to proceed. Surely no boulder problem or route is worth getting injured over

6. **Avoid passive hanging on straight arms, especially a single, fully extended arm.** Five common straight-arm situations to avoid:
   - Dropping all the way into a straight-arm position (with relaxed shoulders) when doing pull-ups
   - Doing fingerboard training with fully straight arms and shrugging shoulders
   - Hanging on a straight arm with a completely lax shoulder and disengaged scapular stabilizers while at a rest position on an overhanging climb
   - Catching a lunge with a fully extended arm and shoulder
   - Any campus training move, especially drop downs and long upward grabs, that forcefully engages a straight arm and extended shoulder

7. **Don't climb to complete exhaustion.** Doing a high volume of hard climbing — and in particular pushing yourself to the limit late in the day — increases injury risk. Know when to say "when" in training or climbing: such wisdom comes from experience. **Practical rule:** End your climbing day early (with a little gas left in the tank) when you're planning to climb again the very next day — limits the day's accumulated stress and avoids the end-of-day injury trap

8. **Don't climb and train (specifically) more than a total of four days per week.** Climbing is obviously a stressful activity that focuses high loads on the relatively small muscle groups and tendons of the fingers, arms, and shoulders. If you climb hard for a total of four days per week (combined indoor and outdoor): do no other climbing-specific training on the three remaining days of the week. Keep in mind that even with three days of rest out of seven, your body will struggle to repair microtraumas in the most stressed tendons and muscles. You can do some generalized training (aerobic conditioning, antagonist and stabilizer exercises, yoga and other mobility movements, etc.) on your three climbing rest days per week. Most important: **Immediately reduce your climbing days to three (or even two) at the first sign of finger, tendon, or joint pain**

9. **Use periodization or autoregulation to vary your training schedule.** Sports scientists consider periodization to be the gold standard for medium-term planning of an optimally effective strength-training program. As described in chapter 5, periodization involves a premeditated variation in workout focus, intensity, and volume, which in the long run produces a maximum training response. Periodization and autoregulation may also reduce the risk of overuse injury, since the training focus and intensity change every workout or every few weeks at the most. While beginners will see steady improvements simply climbing a few days per week, intermediate and advanced climbers — who climb and train with greater intensity — must introduce some variation into their program. Furthermore, these periodized schedules all include an extended rest period (a few days to a week) that offers precious catch-up time for recovery deficits and healing time for any localized pain or systemic fatigue. **Autoregulation** = a self-directed, ad lib-like approach to varying workout intensity based on an aggregate of subjective feelings of fatigue and objective biofeedback early in the workout. Successful autoregulation requires an accurate, intuitive sense of what's optimal given your current state, as well as a more-than-basic knowledge of exercise physiology. The bottom line: The goal of autoregulation is to consciously select and engage only in exercises that will target a muscle group or movement in an optimal way. Likewise, you must willfully avoid exercises that will stress not-yet-recovered muscle groups (from the previous workout)

10. **Make getting proper rest and nutrition top priorities.** Getting proper rest and nutrition seems like a no-brainer for a serious athlete, yet Hörst is often surprised by the bad dietary and sleep habits he observes among serious climbers. Chapter 11 on "Performance Nutrition" and Chapter 12 on "Accelerating Recovery" provide many useful strategies to employ as you increase the priority placed on these subjects. If you use these strategies, most of the time, you will outperform the masses and reduce your risk of overuse injuries. Consistent lack of sleep and poor nutrition will slow recovery between workouts and days of climbing — it will also increase vulnerability to injury. Taking control of your diet and sleep habits is most important during periods of high-volume, high-intensity training and in the days leading up to a big climb or competition. Ultimately, strive to make your modifications in these areas permanent lifestyle changes.
