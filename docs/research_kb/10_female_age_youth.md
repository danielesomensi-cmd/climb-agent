# Topic 10 — Female-Specific, Age, and Youth Considerations

> **Project:** climb-agent knowledge base
> **Scope:** How should the engine adapt training for female climbers, youth (<18), and older climbers (40+)?
> **Status:** DRAFT v1
> **Date:** 2026-03-16
> **Language:** English (knowledge base standard)
> **Cross-references:** Topic 06 (RED-S, body composition), Topic 07 (injury prevention, D35 age gates)

## Executive Summary

Three populations require specific engine adaptations: (1) female climbers face RED-S risk and may benefit from menstrual cycle awareness (though evidence for cycle-based training is inconclusive); (2) youth climbers (<16-18) have vulnerable growth plates requiring strict training restrictions; (3) older climbers need adjusted recovery timelines and injury prevention emphasis. The engine's approach should be: collect sex and age at onboarding, apply hard safety gates for youth, offer optional menstrual cycle tracking for females, and adjust recovery parameters for older users.

## 1. Female-Specific Considerations

### 1.1 Menstrual Cycle and Performance

**The current evidence is inconclusive.** An umbrella review by Phillips et al. (2023, *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living*) examined all published meta-analyses and systematic reviews on menstrual cycle phase and resistance exercise. Key conclusion: it is premature to conclude that short-term hormonal fluctuations appreciably influence acute exercise performance or longer-term strength/hypertrophic adaptations to resistance training. Highly variable findings are likely due to poor and inconsistent methodological practices.

**McNulty et al. meta-analysis (51 studies):** exercise performance may be trivially reduced only in the early follicular phase (days 1-5). That's all.

**However, individual experience matters.** Lattice Training's Female Climber Series emphasises that:
- 93% of athletes report some menstrual cycle-related symptoms
- ~65% report symptoms affecting training
- Tracking your cycle for 3-6 months can reveal individual patterns
- Adaptations can range from syncing deload weeks to adjusting session intensity
- Expectation and perception play a significant role in symptom experience

**Practical framework (Lattice, Climbing Doctor):**
- **Late follicular phase (post-period, pre-ovulation):** estrogen rising, energy/mood improving → may be a good time for higher intensity work
- **Luteal phase:** both estrogen and progesterone high → "glycogen sparing" (burns more fat) → theoretical benefit for endurance work, but no conclusive performance evidence. RPE may feel elevated. Amygdala activity increased → fear response/anxiety may be biologically elevated.
- **Early follicular (menstruation):** energy/mood may be lower. Ligamentous laxity may peak around ovulation. Extra care for finger joints recommended during this period.

### 1.2 RED-S Risk in Female Climbers

Already covered extensively in Topic 06. Key data:
- 15.8% amenorrhoea prevalence in elite female competition climbers (Joubert et al., 2022)
- Climbing culture's "lighter = better" mentality is a documented risk factor
- IFSC uses BMI margins for competition eligibility, but more medical supervision needed
- Missing 3+ menstrual cycles = significantly increased injury risk (Lattice Training)
- Engine safety rule D64: never encourage weight loss, flag fatigue + declining performance patterns

### 1.3 Engine Approach for Female Climbers

The engine should:
- Ask sex at onboarding (for assessment benchmarking, not for differential programming)
- Offer **optional** menstrual cycle tracking (not mandatory — many women find it unhelpful or invasive)
- If tracking enabled: allow users to note cycle phase and symptoms; suggest intensity adjustments if they report low energy or elevated RPE (defer to their subjective experience, not algorithmic rules)
- Never prescribe cycle-phase-based training as if it were evidence-based — the evidence isn't there yet
- Always flag amenorrhoea or irregular cycles as potential RED-S indicators requiring medical consultation

## 2. Youth Considerations (<18)

### 2.1 Growth Plate Injuries — The Critical Risk

**Schöffl et al. (multiple studies, 2004-2024):** documented a 600% increase in epiphyseal fractures over the past decade in youth climbers. In a 2023 prospective analysis, 45% of all injuries in adolescent climbers were growth plate-related.

**Key facts:**
- Growth plates (epiphyses) in fingers don't finish growing until approximately age 17
- Epiphyseal fractures typically occur during the adolescent growth spurt: ages 10-14 for girls, 12-16 for boys
- Most common during the 1-2 years of highest growth velocity
- 64% of youth growth plate fractures were associated with the crimp grip during high-intensity movements
- Only 15% of surveyed adolescent climbers correctly identified growth plate injuries as the most common youth-specific injury (Meyers et al., 2020)

### 2.2 Youth Training Restrictions

**Consensus from Schöffl, Hörst, Consuegra (Ch.8), and climbing medical community:**

| Training element | Under 16 | 16-18 | Over 18 |
|-----------------|----------|-------|---------|
| Campus board (double dynos) | ❌ NEVER | ⚠️ With caution, after skeletal maturity assessment | ✅ With prerequisites |
| Campus board (controlled laddering, large holds) | ⚠️ Small amounts, 1-2×/week, smooth movements only | ✅ With prerequisites | ✅ With prerequisites |
| Max-weight hangboard | ❌ NEVER | ⚠️ Only after 2 years systematic training (D35) | ✅ With prerequisites (D35) |
| Min-edge hangboard | ❌ NEVER | ⚠️ Only after 2 years systematic training | ✅ With prerequisites |
| Hypergravity training (added weight) | ❌ NEVER | ⚠️ With caution | ✅ Progressive |
| Full crimp grip | ⚠️ Avoid; favour open-hand | ⚠️ Minimise; favour open-hand | ⚠️ Minimise on hangboard (D72) |
| Total climbing + training | Max 4 days/week | 4-5 days/week | As programmed |

**Additional youth guidelines (Hörst, Schöffl):**
- All youth climbers should have an off-season (1-4 months) with little or no climbing
- Playing a second sport is strongly recommended through age 16 (develops diverse motor skills)
- Any chronic pain in fingers = immediate cessation of climbing + medical consultation
- Dorsal finger pain at PIP joint is a red flag requiring immediate attention
- Monitor weekly training hours: >10-12 hours/week during growth spurts = significantly elevated risk
- Body fat and growth should be monitored; deliberate underweight for competition = harmful to developing bodies

### 2.3 López-Rivera Contraindications (already in D35)

From Consuegra Ch.8:
- Climbers <16 years: should not do max-weight or min-edge hangboard protocols
- Climbers with <2 years systematic training: should not do these protocols regardless of age

## 3. Older Climbers (40+)

### 3.1 Key Physiological Changes

- **Recovery slows:** after 40, recovery from training takes progressively longer. This manifests as lingering soreness and feeling more tired after workouts.
- **Muscle loss (sarcopenia):** after age 30-35, gradual loss of both number and size of muscle fibres, particularly Type II (fast-twitch). Minimised by continued strength training and adequate sleep.
- **Tendon adaptation even slower:** tendons that were already the slowest-adapting tissue become more vulnerable. Progressive loading is even more critical.
- **HGH reduction:** growth hormone secretion decreases with age, further slowing recovery. Adequate deep sleep becomes even more important.
- **Bone density concerns:** especially for female climbers post-menopause. Strength training has a protective effect.

### 3.2 Engine Adjustments for Older Climbers

- **Extended recovery between sessions:** where a 25-year-old might recover in 48h from a hangboard session, a 45-year-old may need 72h+. Engine should allow user to set recovery multiplier.
- **Conservative volume progression:** the <10% weekly increase rule (D71) should be even more conservative for older climbers.
- **Prioritise warm-up:** even more important with age — reduced tissue elasticity increases injury risk from insufficient warm-up.
- **Strength training is essential:** combats sarcopenia and maintains bone density. Should not be de-prioritised in favour of climbing-only programs.
- **Sleep emphasis:** HGH-dependent recovery makes sleep quality the #1 modifiable factor for older climbers.

## 4. Implications for climb-agent

| Finding | Impact | Priority |
|---------|--------|----------|
| Menstrual cycle evidence is inconclusive (Phillips 2023) | Engine should NOT prescribe cycle-phase-based training as evidence-based | v1 |
| Individual cycle tracking can be useful (Lattice, 93% report symptoms) | Offer optional tracking with subjective adjustments, not algorithmic rules | v2 |
| Growth plate injuries: 600% increase, 45% of youth injuries (Schöffl) | Hard age gates essential for youth users | v1, CRITICAL |
| Under-16s: no campus, no max hangboard, no hypergravity, max 4 days/week | Engine must enforce these restrictions when user age <16 | v1 |
| Older climbers: slower recovery, increased injury risk | Adjustable recovery multiplier, conservative progression | v1 |

### New Decisions

| # | Decision | Rationale | Action |
|---|----------|-----------|--------|
| D80 | **Hard age gates for youth users (<16): no campus, no max hangboard, no hypergravity** | Schöffl (2004-2024): 600% increase in epiphyseal fractures. 45% of youth injuries are growth plate. López-Rivera: <16 = contraindicated for advanced hangboard. | Engine checks age at onboarding; if <16, these exercises are blocked entirely. If 16-18, warnings + prerequisites. |
| D81 | **Youth training cap: max 4 days/week climbing + training** | Hörst, Schöffl consensus: chronic overuse is the primary risk for youth. >10-12 hours/week during growth spurts = significantly elevated injury risk. | Engine limits session scheduling for users <18 to max 4 days/week. |
